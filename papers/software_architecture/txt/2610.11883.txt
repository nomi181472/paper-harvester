Abstract
Earth system models (ESMs) have grown tremendously in realism, yet key uncertainties persist
in the climate response to greenhouse-gas forcing, particularly due to cloud radiative feedbacks.
In addition, their software architecture was not designed for accelerator hardware or modern artificial intelligence (AI). Here we present legoESM, a composable, differentiable, multiscale ESM
written in JAX. It builds on decades of community-developed parameterizations and numerical
methods, recast in a unified framework by AI coding agents under a human-specified scientific
contract and verified through benchmarking. Dynamical cores, physics schemes, grids, complexity levels and components are swappable like building blocks, and can use conventional physics
or machine-learned emulators. A single code base spans metre-scale large-eddy simulation to
global simulations and weather to climate. End-to-end differentiability enables gradient-based
calibration, variational data assimilation and online training. legoESM ’s modular architecture
enables systematic evaluation of diverse model variants to explore structural uncertainty and test
hypotheses. legoESM produces realistic simulations across scales, reduces land-surface temperature bias through gradient-based calibration, and scales efficiently on GPUs to kilometer-scale
simulations. It offers an open, community infrastructure for hypothesis testing, research and
teaching in Earth sciences and a template for multiscale physical systems.

Introduction
Earth system models (ESMs) have improved substantially over recent decades, both in their fidelity
with respect to the observed climate and in the breadth of processes they represent 1–6 . Yet,
important biases persist 7,8 and the uncertainty in the response of the climate system to greenhousegas forcing remains large, with equilibrium climate sensitivity still being uncertain at the ∼50%

level 9 . Projections of rainfall extremes 10 , of ocean heat uptake, and of the strength of the land
carbon sink 11,12 differ substantially between models and are often inconsistent across different
regions of the world. Further, ESMs have to a large extent not embraced the data revolution: only
a fraction of the wealth of observations and observational products (satellite retrievals, reanalyses)
is currently being used for model calibration, largely because of limitation in the capability of those
models to tune parameters with current sampling methods.
Three developments could, in principle, reduce these uncertainties. First, machine learning (ML)
has been used to emulate or replace empirical parameterizations such as deep convection 13–16 or
ocean mesoscale eddies 17–20 , improving key processes such as rainfall extremes and ocean eddy
fluxes, and demonstrably reducing systematic errors, such as in cloud cover, precipitation and
circulation 21–23 . Yet, because ESMs are typically written in Fortran or C, ML components are
coupled through one-way bridges that feed a neural-network prediction into the physics model 24 . As
a result, online training, that is, learning through the model’s own evolution, is generally impossible,
and the resulting hybrid systems are prone to instability 15 and cannot be further tuned against
observations. Second, as models have grown in complexity, many modeling centers have adopted
automated methods to tune and calibrate uncertain parameters 22,25,26 . However, conventional
physics-based models generally do not provide an adjoint: that is, the gradient of model outputs
with respect to these parameters is not available. Calibration must therefore rely on derivativefree approaches, such as Monte Carlo or ensemble methods 27–29 . Because these methods require
many model evaluations, their computational cost grows rapidly as the number of parameters
increases. Third, only a handful of models or components have been ported to modern hardware,
such as graphics processing units (GPUs) 30,31 , and this remains the exception rather than the
norm. This has limited the ability to go to higher resolution to better resolve key processes such as
deep convection 32,33 or to run models on other platforms, such as the cloud, further limiting ESM
accessibility.
In parallel, weather forecasting has recently witnessed a (second) revolution (after the first: data
assimilation): data-driven models trained on decades of reanalysis now rival or surpass physicsbased forecasts and run at a fraction of the computational cost of physics-based models 34–38 . Yet,
purely learned AI weather models tend to drift and can lose physical realism at long lead times 39 ,
and on their own they do not provide the conservative, multi-component interpretable system that
climate projection requires. More fundamentally, machine-learning emulators, even if skillful and
fast, mainly reproduce input–output relationships without representing the physical mechanisms
that generate them. This data-driven revolution has, moreover, so far largely been confined to the
atmosphere, where decades of reanalysis provide abundant training data. Yet, the sparser ocean
and land records make comparable data-driven fidelity much harder to achieve, although AI ocean
emulators are now emerging 40,41 . More broadly, ESMs are not merely predictive tools but also research tools: their capacity for hypothesis testing and mechanistic attribution is central to scientific
progress. Preserving that capability while gaining the computational and optimization advantages
of differentiable, ML-enhanced methods is a central motivation for process-based, differentiable
ESMs.
In addition, and related to hypothesis testing, climate science has had a long tradition of using a
hierarchy of models, spanning idealized to complex configurations 42–44 . Such a hierarchy has been
fundamental to building physical understanding of the climate system. Modern ESMs can themselves span a wide range of resolutions, timescales, and configurations, yet moving systematically
across levels of complexity often requires substantial changes in model configuration or even the use
of different modeling systems. A more modular framework could make these transitions easier and
more controlled, enabling hypotheses and counterfactuals to be tested consistently across scales
and levels of complexity.

Finally, most current ESMs are written in compiled languages (Fortran, C/C++) that are less
widely used across the broader scientific community than Python, which can limit adoption by
researchers from other disciplines and by downstream users, leading to slow model evolution. The
recent weather AI revolution has demonstrated that it is not only model performance that drives
widespread adoption, but also ease of use 45 .
The confluence of large-scale datasets, GPU-accelerated computing, and differentiable programming frameworks capable of scaling gradient-based optimization enabled the machine-learning revolution. Earth system science is now positioned to leverage the same convergence, but doing so requires ESMs written in modern, automatically differentiable languages that can natively use GPUs
and other emerging architectures, support gradient-based optimization, and interface directly with
machine learning.
As such, we argue that a modern ESM should be (i) easy to use, and ideally written in Python,
the lingua franca of modern data science, yet with high performance; (ii) hardware-agnostic, running on any of central processing units (CPUs), graphics processing units (GPUs) and tensor
processing units (TPUs) as accelerators keep evolving; (iii) composable, so that different numerical
methods, physical parameterizations or entire modules (for example the whole atmosphere) can be
swapped, combined and trained jointly for controlled experiments and hypothesis testing; (iv) able
to integrate ML seamlessly, whether by replacing a single parameterization or an entire component (for example swapping the whole atmosphere for a neural network, as in recent AI weather
models 34–38,46 ), which requires the code to be end-to-end differentiable; (v) solvable on many or
adaptive grids tailored to the problem at hand; and (vi) able to run across spatial and temporal
scales.
A growing ecosystem of differentiable models has begun to demonstrate the benefits of parts
of these ideas, though so far this has been for one component or one spatial or temporal scale
at a time. For the atmosphere, differentiable dynamical cores and intermediate-complexity models such as the JAX Circulation Model 47 , the neural general circulation model NeuralGCM 46
and SpeedyWeather.jl 48 have demonstrated the use of gradients for calibration or online learning. For the ocean, JAX and automatic-differentiation efforts, from Veros 49,50 to FESOM-JAX 51 ,
are bringing the same capability to state estimation, e.g., for data assimilation. More broadly,
DJ4Earth 52 uses compiler-level program transformations to make several Julia models (ShallowWaters.jl, SpeedyWeather.jl, Oceananigans.jl and DJUICE.jl) differentiable and performance-portable,
although these components are not yet coupled and differentiability has been demonstrated for each
component individually. For the land surface and hydrology, differentiable models such as DifferLand 53 , JAX-CanVeg 54 and differentiable process-based hydrology models 55 have demonstrated
that they can be used to train physical parameters or neural components end to end against observations, building on the broader concept of differentiable programming in the geosciences 55–57 .
Yet, at present, no unifying infrastructure exists that addresses all of those needs for the entire
Earth system, rather than a targeted treatment of one piece in isolation.
Here, we present legoESM, a novel differentiable and composable modeling infrastructure for
weather and climate science. Rather than a single ESM, it is an open, modular framework in which
differentiable bricks of individual processes or full Earth components can be assembled, coupled
and benchmarked to address diverse scientific questions or hypotheses. This modeling ecosystem
is conceived as an evolving, agile, community-based infrastructure to test ideas and hypotheses,
with clear ML-inspired metrics, across weather and climate (Fig. 1a). Distinctively, legoESM was
developed almost entirely from the ground up using AI coding agents—autonomous software agents
that implement, test, and revise code in response to a human-authored scientific specification and
based on clear benchmarks. To ensure that agent-generated code remains physically consistent and
scientifically faithful, development is constrained by explicit contracts, which specify the required
3

interfaces, physical invariants, units, and expected behaviour of individual components. Implementations were further evaluated using faithfulness oracles (trusted reference solutions) for physical
processes and systematic benchmarks against analytical solutions, established test cases and reference datasets. This approach allowed AI agents considerable autonomy in software implementation
while keeping the scientific requirements and criteria for correctness under human control. legoESM
is intended to change how we conduct research and teach weather and climate science through its
ease of use, flexibility, and agility.

A model assembled from interchangeable bricks
Conventional ESMs remain relatively rigid. Although components such as dynamical cores and grids
can often be exchanged within established modelling frameworks 58,59 , parameterizations and their
interactions have evolved together over decades, making it difficult to modify or replace individual
components without substantial effort elsewhere in the model. Moreover, obtaining a well-calibrated
and physically credible simulation after such changes can be costly and time-consuming 25,26,28 .
legoESM aims to reduce these barriers through a modular, differentiable architecture in which
alternative schemes and hypotheses can be exchanged and tested, and new model configurations
could potentially be rapidly calibrated and evaluated.
Written in Python with JAX 60 (a differentiable machine-learning library) mostly by AI coding
agents, legoESM is assembled from interchangeable bricks (Fig. 1c) at levels ranging from individual parameterizations to entire model components. For example, the atmosphere can range
from a fully physical to a fully machine-learned (emulated) component, as in recent AI weather
models 34–36,46 . Differentiability allows access to the model gradients (with respect to parameters
or variables) at numerical precision and seamless interoperability and training between physics and
ML, with end-to-end optimization 55,56 . The atmosphere, ocean, land, and sea ice are independent
and composable packages. Each package can run on its own, so a single command yields a working
ocean or atmosphere model, and the same code allows composing components as a fully coupled
system (e.g., ocean-atmosphere) without modification through a differentiable coupler. All of those
packages build on one shared foundation (the core) composed of grids, differentiable operators,
thermodynamics and time integration, and depend on that substrate but not on each other. A
scheme, a grid or an entire component can therefore be swapped seamlessly (Methods), and the
composed model can be optimized end-to-end in any configuration, unlike standard ESMs.
Writing the model in JAX enables two key properties: the same code runs natively on any
current hardware (CPUs, GPUs and TPUs), and will be able to compile to accelerators that do not
yet exist without any rewriting, sidestepping the multi-year multi-person effort of porting a legacy
model to new hardware. Second, the entire model is differentiable: the gradient of any output with
respect to any parameter or variable is obtained automatically, with no hand-written adjoint. This
enables efficient gradient-based optimization of any set of variables or parameters, as well as native
data assimilation (shipped for the atmosphere in this first version, see below).
The model configuration is defined through three axes that can vary independently: (i) the
numerical grid; (ii) the model complexity; and (iii) the set of physics schemes or machine-learning
emulation (Fig. 1b). A grid is first chosen: legoESM supports cubed-sphere (FV3-style, with a
duo-grid treatment of panel edges) 61–63 , latitude–longitude 64 , Gaussian/spectral 65,66 , unstructured
Voronoi (Model for Prediction Across Scales, MPAS) 67–69 and NEMO-style tripolar 70,71 grids, as
well as doubly periodic Cartesian planes for large-eddy and cloud-resolving simulations. The model
complexity is flexible, spanning a ladder of physical complexity, from shallow-water to hydrostatic
to nonhydrostatic atmospheres, and from a prescribed sea surface temperature (SST) to a slab to
4

a full three-dimensional ocean, and a single framework ranges from metre-scale turbulence to the
entire Earth circulation (Supplementary Figs. S1–S3 and S5 for shallow water, S4 for a hydrostatic
baroclinic wave, S9–S11 for nonhydrostatic large-eddy and cloud-resolving simulations, S13 for an
atmosphere over prescribed SST and S14–S18 for the three-dimensional ocean). The physics are
additive and composable: each process computes its tendency from the same model state and the
tendencies are summed, so a parameterization can be added or removed cleanly (Supplementary
Information §3). Mass is conserved by construction, and energy and momentum are tracked with
explicit budget closure. legoESM currently offers more than 160 selectable parameterizations, with
tens of convection, turbulence and microphysics choices (Extended Data Tables 1–10), and any
module or Earth component can be exchanged for a machine-learned alternative (e.g., a spherical
Fourier neural operator (SFNO) emulation of the entire atmosphere), giving a continuous ladder
from fully physical to fully AI based instantiation of the model. Full equations and numerics
are presented in Supplementary Information §1, and the source is openly available at https:
//github.com/climate-federation/legoESM.
Development by AI agents under a scientific contract. legoESM was built through a
human–agent workflow that we summarize here (details in Methods). We, human scientists, first
wrote, with an agentic coding environment, a global plan and a specification (a project “constitution”) stating the vision of the model (composable, swappable, differentiable) and the model
requirements (e.g., conserved quantities and unit consistency). Throughout, we, humans, dictated
the scientific logic and the agents implemented and reviewed the code. We used coding agents
built on independent large-language-model (LLM) families (Claude, Anthropic; Codex, OpenAI;
and GLM, Zhipu AI), with clear rules to limit duplication. Shared physical constants and core
routines (for example, thermodynamics) were centralized in the core package (the model substrate)
and never re-derived elsewhere. For the hardest problems, such as multi-node scaling, grid-imprint
artifacts at cubed-sphere panel edges, and faithful high-resolution ocean and atmosphere-only simulations, we used a persistent-iteration (“Ralph”) loop 72 , which re-ran an agent on the same task
and fed test and diagnostic results back at each cycle until a target criterion was met, so that the
agent did not stop prematurely. We note also that agents not only coded but also leveraged a vast
array of benchmarks, from the published literature to open-source datasets and codes, and were
used to assess visual and physical realism (based on the published literature) and to relate biases
to physical processes. This use of the multi-modal capabilities of LLMs therefore went well beyond
code generation, to building, testing and validating a new model.
The AI agents rapidly produced code of good quality, yet also generated hallucinations, which
were at times subtle. Because credible climate simulation requires accurate code, every component was validated along a ladder of increasing complexity. First, every substantial piece of code
was reviewed by at least one adversarial code-review agent built on an LLM family (Codex, OpenAI) trained separately from that of the main coding agent (Claude). The review often found
subtle errors or incorrect judgments, or challenged the choices made by the coding agent, which
accelerated convergence and reduced defects. Second, the agents wrote unit tests covering most
of the code, testing the accuracy and physical realism of small portions of code (several of them
are available in the code repository). Third, physical equations were tested in asymptotic singlephysics cases against well-known theoretical behaviour, such as convergence at the design order
under Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL)-controlled refinement (Supplementary Figs. S24 and S25).
Finally, components were tested with several interacting processes switched on, against increasingly
stringent, larger-scale benchmarks, from dynamical-core tests of rising complexity (detailed below)

The ocean followed a different validation strategy, as fewer clear benchmarks existed than for the
atmosphere. Beyond unit tests and adversarial review, idealized asymptotic tests verified, for both
the ocean and the atmosphere, that the operators (many of which were shared between the two)
reproduced their analytic targets: vector-invariant (Burgers) advection (Supplementary Fig. S24a),
Laplacian viscous decay with the expected k 2 scaling (Supplementary Fig. S24b), the Coriolis tendency (Supplementary Fig. S24c), and undamped and damped inertial oscillations (Supplementary
Fig. S24d,e), while finite-volume transport and gravity-wave terms converged at their design orders
under CFL-controlled refinement in both the atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. S25a) and the ocean
(Supplementary Fig. S25b). Idealized and then realistic ocean cases were then run against oceanmodel oracles (NEMO 70 , MITgcm 90 and Oceananigans 91 ) on latitude–longitude, cubed-sphere,
unstructured (MPAS/Voronoi 92 and Finite-volumE Sea ice–Ocean Model (FESOM)-type 93 ) and
NEMO-style tripolar grids (Supplementary Figs. S14–S17). In an idealized basin twin experiment,
legoESM stayed within 5 × 10−3 ◦ C (r.m.s.) of NEMO 5.0.2 in sea-surface temperature after one
year (Supplementary Fig. S15). Even with an oracle’s source in hand, validation did not reduce to
line-by-line comparison: the codebases decomposed the equations differently, and tendencies matching the oracle to 3–4 digits over the interior could still hide order-one errors in a few critical cells
(e.g., topography-adjacent faces controlling the form stress that sets circumpolar transport). These
residuals were instead exposed by comparing term-by-term budgets, integrated over dynamically
relevant regions at identical model states, against the oracle’s native trend diagnostics, which amplified what pointwise metrics hid and localized the responsible discretization. This iteration revealed
missing processes (freshwater runoff), stability-motivated coefficient substitutions, and boundaryadjacent stencil differences that were successively corrected to obtain faithful simulations.

Validation of the land component was less direct, as many of its processes are biological and represented by empirical relationships rather than first-principles equations, and hence lacked benchmark cases. Soil water flow (Richards’ equation) and soil heat diffusion were checked for mass and
energy conservation and physically consistent behavior, using agent-based diagnostics and visual
inspection. Simulated fluxes were then evaluated against eddy-covariance measurements at several
flux-tower sites and at the global scale, including the seasonality of the turbulent heat fluxes by
biome, the global pattern of latent heat flux, and the zonal distribution of gross primary productivity (Supplementary Figs. S19–S22). Evaluation criteria were met within only a few iterations,
with the exception of soil evaporation where high biases desiccated soil. Therefore, a modernized
soil-evaporation parameterization was implemented. The surface parameters were then calibrated
by gradient descent at eddy-covariance sites (Fig. 2; described in the section on composability and
differentiable calibration below). Finally, the initial pools of the dynamic carbon model, based on
DifferLand 53 , were estimated either through an analytical linearized solution or by differentiable
optimization, with comparable results (Supplementary Fig. S23). Comparing the pools with observational estimates revealed, for example, too little soil carbon in northern high-latitude soils 94 ,
and the agents iterated on the parameters and structure of the carbon model until the initial pools
were realistic.
The resulting modeling infrastructure, developed over 9 months, comprised about 620,000 lines
of model code in the packages, about 824,000 lines of tests, through which automated continuous
integration enforced the physical contracts (Methods), and about 555,000 lines of simulation and
analysis scripts.
Two features of this workflow mattered as much as the end result. First, code generation was fast:
with agents handling implementation and testing, adding a new parameterization or dynamical core
(historically a months-to-a-year undertaking in an established model) shrank to days, compressing
the development cycle between a scientific idea or hypothesis and a tested, benchmarked implementation. Second, human experts in the loop were critical. They set the scope and strategy at
all stages of the project, and without supervision the agents tended to diverge, coding unneeded
or even unintended features or spending compute on irrelevant items, and often losing track of
the ultimate objective. Human input was needed for physical choices and for setting main project
directions, for example deciding which reference solution or oracle a component should reproduce,
which parameterizations to include, the overall architecture of the model, or whether to keep or
replace a simple parameterization with a more complex one based on human-defined objective, or
requesting denial experiments that remove a single process to assess its effect on the result. This
back-and-forth between the agents’ questions and the scientists’ responses or hypotheses reduced
the overall speed, but in our opinion largely improved the quality and clarified the scope of the
end software. Our workflow was therefore closer to recent multi-agent “co-scientist” systems, in
which scientists specify goals and steer agents that generate and test hypotheses 95 , than to a fully
autonomous research pipeline.

Many grids, one set of equations
A central design choice in legoESM is that the governing equations can be solved on different grids
tailored to best suit the problem. In the atmosphere, legoESM provides shallow-water, hydrostatic
primitive-equation and nonhydrostatic compressible-Euler dynamics on four global grid families:
the cubed sphere 61–63 , latitude–longitude finite-volume grids 64 , Gaussian/spectral transforms 65,66
and unstructured Voronoi (MPAS) meshes 67–69 , together with a doubly periodic Cartesian plane on
which the nonhydrostatic core runs large-eddy and cloud-resolving simulations. The same physics
8

therefore runs through a controlled hierarchy of dynamical cores and grids. The ocean solves
the Boussinesq hydrostatic primitive equations 96 ; nonhydrostatic effects are negligible at basin-toglobal scales, so no nonhydrostatic ocean core has been included yet. The ocean equations run
on the latitude–longitude, cubed-sphere, MPAS/Voronoi and tripolar grids. Production simulations use the latitude–longitude C-grid, in its curvilinear form on the eORCA1 tripole mesh 70,71 .
The MPAS, cubed-sphere and spectral ocean configurations serve idealized and benchmark experiments. The specific discretizations, transport operators and their community lineages are listed
in Supplementary Table S1. As an illustration, the same shallow-water equations solved on the
cubed-sphere, lat–lon, icosahedral and spectral grids give near-identical (within numerical bounds)
solutions across all four discretizations, including flow over an isolated mountain (Williamson 5)
and the propagating Rossby–Haurwitz wave (Williamson 6) 85 (Supplementary Figs. S1, S3 and S5),
and the cubed-sphere C-D core reproduces the Jablonowski–Williamson baroclinic wave 88 (Supplementary Fig. S4). Removing a residual grid imprint at cube-panel boundaries, visible only on the
meridional wind and not in error norms, was one of the problems for which the persistent-iteration
(Ralph) loop described above proved essential, leading to the implementation of the duo-grid treatment 63 that eliminates it (Supplementary Fig. S4c). Our advection schemes across all grids (dycores) were validated against a substantial suite of dycore benchmarks, given in Supplementary
Information §2, ensuring that the formulations were numerically sound across all cases.
While many of the grids, numerical schemes and parameterizations can be used as composable
elements, legoESM’s ocean component builds on the community’s knowledge and provides recipes
that closely shadow the combinations in existing production models, reproducing their numerical
schemes and, where verified against the reference model’s own output, their trajectories. A NEMO
shadow recipe is certified statement by statement against NEMO 5.0.2 on its idealized test cases,
and shadow recipes exist for Veros and Oceananigans, so that a legoESM configuration can be
pinned to a trusted production model before its components are swapped.

Composability and differentiable multiscale calibration
Swapping physical schemes or model components to assess their impact has been a long-standing
scientific need. Although many contemporary ESMs have adopted increasingly modular software
architectures, most process representations remain tightly coupled to other model components,
reflecting in part technical debt accumulated over decades of development. Exchanging such components therefore still requires source-code modifications and careful bookkeeping. In legoESM,
all model components follow a common tensor-in/tendency-out interface, taking the model state
as input and returning a tendency. This standardized interface allows individual process representations, or larger parts of the system (e.g., the entire atmosphere), to be exchanged without
modifying the rest of the model.
We illustrate this capability along with legoESM multiscale capability with atmospheric turbulence and deep-convection parameterizations (Supplementary Figs. S6–S8), which govern turbulent
exchange in the lower atmosphere and much of tropical rainfall, respectively, and remain important
sources of uncertainty and error in climate models. Nine turbulence and ten convection schemes
(Extended Data Tables 4 and 2) are first run unchanged in single-column configurations: the turbulence schemes are evaluated against reference turbulent cases in legoESM large-eddy simulation
mode, while the convection schemes are evaluated in radiative–convective equilibrium (RCE) at
three sea-surface temperatures (295, 300 and 305 K) against legoESM cloud-resolving simulations
of the same cases. All experiments therefore use the same model infrastructure, which can itself operate in large-eddy or cloud-resolving configurations, conserving other exactly similar components
9

(e.g., radiation scheme or microphysics). The schemes exhibit clear differences in skill (Supplementary Information §3), enabling controlled comparisons of their off-the-shelf performance. All
ten convection schemes reach a statistically steady equilibrium, but their errors against the cloudresolving temperature and humidity profiles differ by up to a factor of about seven, from the
eddy-diffusivity/mass-flux (EDMF) scheme, which is closest to the reference, to the Kuo and deepconvective-adjustment schemes (Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8). Once implemented, switching
among schemes requires only a one-line configuration change. We then calibrate the parameters of
each turbulence and convection scheme against the corresponding large-eddy and cloud-resolving
reference high-resolution simulations; a single parameter set per scheme is optimized jointly across
all cases for generality. Comparing default and calibrated performance (Supplementary Fig. S6 for
the turbulence closures against LES and Supplementary Figs. S7 and S8 for the convection schemes
before and after calibration) demonstrates how legoESM’s differentiable infrastructure complements
its modular design: process representations can not only be interchanged and compared under
consistent conditions, but also systematically optimized against reference data. Because differentiability is built into legoESM, parameter or initial-condition calibration becomes a gradient-based
optimization problem 55,56 . Gradients propagated through the model via jax.grad allow parameters to be optimized directly against data using modern gradient-based methods 97,98 , rather than
derivative-free searches 25,27 , which are nonetheless still available. The benefit of calibration differs
strongly between convection schemes (Supplementary Fig. S7a). The Tiedtke scheme improves
about threefold (joint score from about 8.2 to 2.8), rising from the lower half of the ranking to
third, whereas most schemes change little; for the mass-flux and Zhang–McFarlane schemes the
jointly tuned parameters even score slightly worse than the defaults and differ between optimization seeds, a reminder that fitting one parameter set to several climates is a non-convex compromise
(see below). Even after calibration, skill depends on the climate: the Kuo, Zhang–McFarlane and
deep-convective-adjustment schemes degrade markedly as the surface warms from 295 to 305 K,
whereas EDMF improves (Supplementary Fig. S7b). A scheme that performs well in the presentday tropics therefore need not remain skillful in a warmer climate, which is precisely the regime
that matters for projections, and legoESM makes such cross-climate tests a routine part of scheme
evaluation.
We further extend this approach to the global land surface by tuning per-plant-functional-type
surface parameters globally by gradient descent against ERA5 99 mean-annual skin temperature
and MODIS albedo (Fig. 2). We reduce the global mean warm temperature bias from +1.09 to
+0.30 K while keeping the surface albedo essentially unbiased (+0.002 to +0.004), with the largest
improvements over the previously too-warm arid and semi-arid land. This is the same machinery
that was used to calibrate turbulent- and convection-scheme parameters against the high-resolution
references, and that we also used to adjust a cloud-fraction critical-relative-humidity threshold using
gradients of the top-of-atmosphere shortwave and longwave fluxes so that the planetary albedo
falls in its observed ∼30% range, and to estimate the initial land-carbon pools (Supplementary
Fig. S23; see the development section above). The same gradient-based strategy can also replace
an entire component: a classical physics suite, a column neural network, or a full AI emulation,
such as spherical neural operator 100 for the atmosphere (a learned dynamical core) can be swapped,
which is demonstrated for the WeatherBench AI-weather model intercomparison (Supplementary
Information §3), and the ocean can run as a prescribed sea surface, a simple single or multi-layer
slab, or a full three-dimensional model, all from one driver. The differentiable parallelization layer
makes this workable at scale. Exact reverse-mode gradients flow through distributed halo exchange
(via a custom adjoint that swaps send/receive ranks) and through the global reductions used by
the mass-conservation fixers (Methods).
The same adjoint capability also underpins variational data assimilation, which is natively en10

abled in the model through differentiability. A three-dimensional variational (3D-Var) analysis is
implemented for the atmosphere: the analysis increment is obtained by differentiating the observation operator and the background term through the model, and it extends to 4D-Var through
the differentiable time-stepping kernel, with no separately coded adjoint model. Single-observation
experiments for both 3D-Var and 4D-Var, including the upstream propagation of the analysis increment that only 4D-Var recovers, are shown in Supplementary Figs. S26 and S27 (Supplementary
Information §10), demonstrating faithful 3D- and 4D-Var capability of the model for weather to
climate data assimilation.
Accessibility to gradients is also useful for diagnostics and process understanding, legoESM
makes it natural to pose questions that are challenging in conventional models, for example one
can ask which processes a given bias is most sensitive to. The gradient of any scalar diagnostic J
(a global-mean radiative imbalance, a regional precipitation bias, a measure of tropical variability)
with respect to every internal parameter and boundary input is obtained in a single reverse pass,
at a cost essentially independent of the number of parameters; ranking those sensitivities attributes
the diagnostic to the processes that control it, where the brute-force equivalent would demand
thousands of perturbed parameter integrations 101–103 . This shifts the emphasis from solely using
the model for predictive skill to also using the model as a tool for understanding.
We note that these gradients are exact for the discretized model, but they are not always useful
for calibration. For chaotic, autoregressive dynamics, gradients through long integrations can vanish
or explode 104 , and implicit solvers, iterative fixers and threshold-like physics can make them noisy
or ill-conditioned, although probabilistic or ensemble formulations can mitigate many of these
issues. There can also be a trade-off between forward accuracy and the stability of the reverse pass;
NeuralGCM, for instance, builds on a comparatively simple spectral dynamical core 46 . legoESM
does not remove these limitations, but it lets users check gradients (for example, against finite
differences) and choose, for each application, the grid, dynamical core, rollout length and loss (for
example, time-averaged or ensemble statistics) that best balance forward fidelity against gradient
quality.

Bridging scales and the model hierarchy
Weather and climate processes span an enormous range of scales, from turbulent eddies at metre
scale to circulations spanning the entire planet. Traditionally, different scales and regimes have
often required distinct model configurations and representations of unresolved processes. legoESM
instead provides a modular, swappable framework designed to facilitate experimentation across a
range of spatial scales and resolutions. At sufficiently high resolution, the framework can support
large-eddy and cloud-resolving configurations 79 , while retaining the flexibility to reuse, adapt, or
replace physical parameterizations as appropriate. This enables systematic investigation of which
parameterizations and process representations remain applicable across scales, which require retuning, and which must be reformulated or replaced as processes become explicitly resolved 82 .
The model is validated across scales: first as large-eddy simulation against reference cases and
against reference codes (MicroHH 73 ), reproducing the expected mean profiles and turbulence structure (Supplementary Figs. S9 and S10). Enlarging the domain and adding moist physics turns
the model into a cloud-resolving model tested in radiative–convective equilibrium 84 and against
the SAM model 83 , and the model can spontaneously organize convection into clusters (“selfaggregation”; Supplementary Fig. S11). The warm-rain microphysics closely matches a particlebased (super-droplet) reference 106,107 (Supplementary Fig. S12). Global simulations against established models
A stringent test of the framework is whether its component, built from the same composable elements, can reproduce a mature model on a complex grid, such as an operational ocean model. For
this case, the three-dimensional Boussinesq ocean uses a split-explicit baroclinic/barotropic solver 96
with K-profile parameterization (KPP) vertical mixing 111 and Gent–McWilliams/Redi (GM–Redi)
eddy parameterization 112,113 , and runs on all supported grids. Figure 3 compares forced legoESM
ocean integrations against NEMO 70 on two of the most demanding meshes: the NEMO-style tripolar (eORCA) grid, whose folded northern boundary is a classic source of numerical artifacts, and
the unstructured MPAS/Voronoi icosahedral grid. On both grids the simulated sea-surface temperature closely tracks NEMO (Fig. 3a): zonal-mean differences stay within about ±0.3 ◦ C between
60◦ S and 60◦ N, and the largest departures (up to ∼0.9 ◦ C) are confined to the Antarctic margin
(Fig. 3b). Zonal-mean sea-surface salinity agrees to within a few hundredths of a psu outside the
polar regions, and the mixed layer is somewhat deeper than in NEMO at northern mid-latitudes.
The two legoESM configurations differ from each other far less than either differs from NEMO,
so the residual differences reflect model formulation, relative to NEMO, rather than the choice of
grid (Supplementary Fig. S16). A centennial spin-up library, an Atlantic meridional overturning
circulation (AMOC) tracker, conservation diagnostics, and a peer-comparison fidelity harness support quantitative model intercomparison (Supplementary Information §5), and the same code runs
eddy-permitting global configurations on 15-km MPAS and FESOM2 93 meshes (Supplementary
Fig. S17). That an agent-built ocean reproduces a mature community model on geometrically
awkward grids is a strong test of the framework. Similar comparative tests have also been done
on simpler configurations that are representative of different aspects of the global circulation at
different resolution, e.g. the DINO 114 .

Performance and scalability
Writing legoESM in JAX enabled flexible portability; identical code runs unmodified on a laptop
and on distributed multi-node high-performance computing systems, at single or double precision.
A configuration can therefore be developed and tested on a laptop and run at production scale, and
prototyping is fast enough to be scientifically useful: a 2.8° dry Held–Suarez integration completes
four simulated years in under an hour on an eight-core laptop, and in under six minutes on a
single A100 GPU. At production scale, a single A100 GPU runs the ∼20-km lat–lon atmosphere
7.2× faster than a full 128-core CPU node. Because the two architectures differ, such comparisons
are best normalized by energy or allocation cost; using nominal thermal design powers (400 W
for one A100 and 2 × 280 W for the two CPUs of a node), this corresponds to roughly a tenfold
advantage per unit of nominal processor power. Laptop-GPU speed-ups are given in Supplementary
Information §7.
To further improve the scaling on CPUs and GPUs, we used agents to tune distributed scaling on
MPI and multi GPUs for all grids, for the atmosphere and ocean. As much as possible, operators
were shared between the ocean and atmosphere. Two algorithmic changes found with agentic
optimization dominated the gains: parallelizing the correlated-k radiation over its g-points led to
a ∼6× speed up on the radiation kernel and a split-explicit barotropic ocean solver was iterated
on to avoid the global-reduction latency wall of the implicit solver, being up to 1.65× faster at 64
ranks. At production resolutions on the DKRZ Levante system the model uses added hardware
efficiently (Fig. 4): the ∼5-km lat–lon atmosphere retains roughly 75% parallel efficiency from 4 to
128 A100 GPUs at single precision, and the ORCA12-class tripolar ocean scales close to ideal up to
64 GPUs. Node for node (four A100 GPUs versus 128 CPU cores), the GPU partition is roughly
an order of magnitude faster for the atmosphere, although, as noted above, such node-to-node
comparisons do not account for differences in cost or energy use. Strong scaling is limited by the
work available per device, not by total problem size, and a GPU needs a much larger subdomain
than a CPU core to stay saturated: on the unstructured MPAS and FESOM2 meshes, speed-up
flattens once the per-GPU subdomain becomes small and halo exchange can no longer be hidden
behind computation (Fig. 4b,d,e). Documented limits remain (Supplementary Information §7):
spectral transforms are communication-bound and admit no domain-decomposed path, and the
structured grids outperform the unstructured MPAS core in per-cell throughput, as expected from
their memory-access patterns. For context, a GPU-optimized hydrostatic ocean dynamical core
(Oceananigans) simulates about a decade of near-global ocean per wall-clock day at 8-km resolution
on 64 A100 GPUs 116 ; a like-for-like comparison with the legoESM ocean, at matched resolution,
time step and hardware, is left for future work.
The throughput reported in Fig. 4 is for forward model steps only; critically, reverse-mode differentiability is preserved: gradients flow correctly through MPI halo exchange and global sum
reductions, verified against single-rank references to machine precision (Methods). A full gradient step for a dry dynamical core costs only 5.4x a forward step on a single GPU, and gradient
checkpointing cuts peak memory effectively (from 8.0 to 0.73 GB) at no measurable wall-clock cost,
making end-to-end gradients practical for short rollouts on a single device.

Conclusion
We present legoESM, a novel modelling framework for the Earth system made of composable,
differentiable building blocks. legoESM shows that a single, high-level, differentiable code base
can span the ESM hierarchy, from large-eddy and cloud-resolving simulation to global atmosphere
and ocean simulations, while remaining composable enough to swap dynamical cores, grids, physics
schemes and complexity levels as interchangeable bricks, and differentiable end-to-end to calibrate
variables, physical parameters, and train embedded neural networks by gradient descent through the
model. Concretely, the architecture makes practical several things that are challenging or impossible
in a conventional model: differentiating a whole-model back to the parameter or input that controls
it (end-to-end sensitivity and attribution); training an embedded neural component online, through
the model’s own evolution rather than on offline single-step tendencies; and, although not new in
itself, running models across scales from a large-eddy column to a global simulation within the
same differentiable code, which facilitates clean cross-scale comparison. These capabilities, rather
than any single simulation, are the novelty we wish to demonstrate.
Two points deserve attention. First, treating differentiability and composability as architectural
building blocks, rather than features added onto an existing model, makes the hierarchy continuous
and the model flexible. Any of the parameterizations or component can be coupled to other parts
of the system based on either physics or AI and, importantly, trained in tandem. Second, the
model was built largely by AI agents, but its trustworthiness rested on a human-authored scientific
definition, choices and validation. We found adversarial agent review and persistent iteration loops
essential precisely for the problems where physical intuition is hard to encode, such as resolving
grid-imprint artifacts at cubed-sphere edges (Supplementary Fig. S4c). A key aspect of the model
concerns the role of benchmarks. Agentic development of legoESM was possible only because the
community has, over decades, distilled its understanding into open, well-posed benchmark cases
and datasets, from the standard dynamical-core tests to intercomparison projects. These were used
to give the agents unambiguous targets against which the model could be checked, and iteratively
corrected. Such benchmarks remain sparse for some components, notably the land surface, sea ice,
biogeochemistry and the coupled system, where fewer well-posed, openly shared reference cases
exist. Community efforts to create and share such baselines would directly benefit agent-assisted
model development.
We believe that legoESM represents a shift in computational science: while a model’s source
code was once the primary repository of physical understanding, in an agentic workflow the code
17

becomes comparatively inexpensive to regenerate, and it is the tests, benchmarks and reference
datasets, the accumulated work of the whole community, as well as the methodology to reach the
final model, that increasingly encode what we know and that must be curated and preserved most
carefully. This is an important point for the weather and climate community in the future and likely
requires a community effort to fully exploit the potential. Finally, legoESM builds on the immense
body of physical understanding encoded in community models developed over decades (from the
parameterizations of the Community Earth System Model 3 , the ocean physics of NEMO 70 , the
dynamical cores pioneered at GFDL, and many others), repackaged in a modern, differentiable
composable and swappable infrastructure. This paper is a demonstration of the potential of this
new type of architecture rather than a finished ESM. The model as of now is not as complex
as established operational models. The framework is not meant to be finished but rather to be
an agile, evolving infrastructure for and by the community. This raises governance questions for
a federated framework: a core team can curate, test and integrate contributions into a common
reference version, or users can fork the code and adapt it for their own purposes, which requires
less long-term maintenance but makes it harder to integrate and evaluate improvements across the
framework. A combination, in which contributions that pass the shared benchmark and contract
tests are merged into a reference version, seems most promising.
Two caveats apply. Gradient-based calibration allows data assimilation, initial state estimation
and optimal parameter tuning to reduce model bias and errors, but it does not remove structural
error in physics-based parameterizations. A well-tuned simulation can therefore still carry substantial biases due to its inherent model structure. The flexibility of the model nonetheless allows
replacing defaulting structures with pure machine learning models with many degrees of freedom
and see whether those can address the biases. Moreover, gradients through long rollouts of chaotic
dynamics can become noisy or explode 104 , so calibration windows, time-averaged losses or ensemble
statistics must be chosen with care to address those issues. Further, the model capability to swap
parameterization and to use gradients for sensitivity can be used to pinpoint sources of biases.
We expect that this will become an important strategy in the future to refine parameterizations.
While the differentiable coupler makes assembling components straightforward, we anticipate the
same hard coupled-system problems that challenge established models, such as spurious deep-water
formation in the Labrador Sea, drift in the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation 117 , or biases
in tropical interannual variability 8,118 that are likely to remain difficult to resolve. However, the
capacity to calibrate parts of the model much faster should enable more rapid testing of solutions
for coupled model problems. Such differentiable, AI-ready infrastructure is a concrete step toward the hybrid ESMs that are increasingly seen as a route to sharper, more actionable climate
projections 32 . By making the full coupled system transparent, with common coding and inlinedocumentation standards set from the outset, easy to differentiate and easy to run on modern
hardware, we hope to lower the barrier to experimentation and to invite the community to build
on and contribute to it.
To make the modelling framework usable and verifiable by the community, we include a set of
curated reference configurations, idealized dynamical-core tests, radiative–convective equilibrium,
and test cases (from large-eddy simulations to single column models) as well as AMIP, OMIP and
LMIP and cross-reference, so that a student or a new group can reproduce a well known baseline
before modifying it. As such, legoESM is intended as an open, evolving research and educational
tool, for theoretical insight, hypothesis testing and clean counterfactual experiments, and, in time
and with sustained community contributions, potentially evolving towards a full-fledged weather
and climate model.
Finally, our modelling strategy aims to be a template that can extend beyond weather and
climate. The design choices underlying legoESM (composability, differentiability, hardware porta18

bility, and agent-assisted development under a human-defined scientific contract) are responses to
structural challenges shared by many multiscale physical systems: astrophysics, materials science,
plasma physics, fluid dynamics, and neurosciences. Those systems all couple processes across vastly
different scales, all benefit from gradient-based inverse methods, and all face the same tension between legacy code and modern accelerators. A composable, differentiable, agent-built framework
in which domain scientists specify the governing logic, conserved quantities and criteria while AI
agents write and mechanically verify the implementation offers a template for simulation-based
science more broadly, wherever physical models are complex and data are abundant.

Methods
The complete governing equations and numerical methods of all components are given in Supplementary
Information §1; this section summarises the computational implementation. End-to-end differentiability,
conservation and stable-shape (non-retracing) JAX execution are treated throughout as hard constraints.

Agentic development methodology
legoESM was implemented by large-language-model coding agents in an agentic coding environment under
a human-authored specification. Most of the code was written by Claude (Anthropic; the Fable, Opus and
Sonnet model families) driving edits, test execution and debugging, and a second agent based on Codex
(OpenAI), a separately trained LLM family, performed adversarial review of every substantial change; GLM
(Zhipu AI) was used for part of the implementation. A human-maintained specification fixed the conserved
quantities, sign and unit conventions, valid scheme sets, reference literature and per-component acceptance
criteria, and domain scientists reviewed and adjudicated the scientific choices at each step (expert-in-theloop). For hard, slowly-converging problems (multi-node scaling; cube-edge grid artifacts) the persistentiteration loop described in the main text 72 was used; community agent-skill libraries (agent-skills 119 ,
superpowers 120 ) were used in the final hardening stages to reach production-level code quality and to surface
subtle bugs. Agents also iteratively improved weak and strong CPU and GPU scaling, including multinode MPI and multi-GPU performance, on the NSF NCAR Derecho and DKRZ Levante supercomputers.
Physical contracts were enforced mechanically throughout development by continuous-integration tripwires
(see Verification harness below).

Differentiable distributed execution. Distributed runs use initialize_distributed to build

the MPI topology, then scatter to rank-local tiles, step, and gather only for I/O. Halo exchange uses a

305 K. LES and CRM cases used the nonhydrostatic plane core, the latter following the RCEMIP RCE300
protocol 84 (Supplementary Information §4). Ocean comparisons (Fig. 3) used MPAS/Voronoi (icosahedral)
and tripolar (eORCA) grids against NEMO 70 . Single-device throughput and speed-ups were measured on
a consumer-class RTX GPU, and strong scaling (Fig. 4) on the DKRZ Levante system with four NVIDIA
A100-80GB GPUs or two 64-core AMD EPYC 7763 CPUs per node (Supplementary Information §7). Full
settings are in Supplementary Information §1–§10.
