A-SAW: The Agentic Software Architecture Workflow

arXiv:2610.13647v1 [cs.SE] 9 Oct 2026

FINN KLATT, University of Liechtenstein, Liechtenstein

AI assistance in software engineering focuses on implementation and verification, leaving the design phase,
and in particular the maintenance of architecture documentation, comparatively underserved. Architecture
as Code makes architectural artifacts versionable, diffable, and machine-readable, but it does not reduce the
effort of keeping them updated, so design documentation still ends up as a fragmented, author-dependent,
and only partially trusted record of why a system looks the way it does.
In this paper we introduce the Agentic Software Architecture Workflow (A-SAW), a process-level artifact
that extends Architecture as Code with a persistent agent layer: an IDE-integrated agent drafts architecture
documents from a version-controlled C4 model, a typed element catalog, and instrumented templates. Every
draft passes a mandatory human review gate before it is accepted. We evaluate it in a large industrial
organization along two tracks: a rubric assessment of 45 architecture documents including 13 matched
source→regeneration pairs, and 13 task-anchored practitioner interviews. Our results show that the workflow
collapses the between-author variance that made quality unpredictable (𝑆𝐷 19.2 → 3.8; ratio 5.0, 95% CI [2.9,
9.2]). Intrinsic quality also rises, by a median of +19 points across the matched pairs (𝑝 = 0.001, 𝑟 = +0.97).
The cohort means of 58% and 89% bundle templates, catalog, agent, and a richer regeneration context. So
they bound the agent’s contribution from above rather than isolating it. In the agent-generated documents,
completeness (100%), consistency (93%), and machine-readability (99%) reach or approach the rubric maximum,
while traceability reaches 79%. After the hands-on task, the interviewed practitioners described their trust
as conditional. They tied it to how much the agent had to infer beyond the data in the repository and to
how many reliable generations they had seen. Their trust also covered whether the recorded design is sound.
Because the rubric measures none of these, high structural scores do not by themselves show that a document
can be trusted. Eleven of the 13 participants rejected agent output that no human had reviewed, and one
warned that polished output may lead reviewers to skip the review. Our work supports keeping human review
mandatory and concentrating it where the agent must infer beyond the repository.

Introduction

Large language models have moved from autocompletion to agentic workflows, and in software
engineering they are now widely adopted [12, 38, 58]. Agents now localize, reproduce, and fix
bugs [57], coordinate in role-distributed teams [22, 56], and use commercial issue trackers under
engineer supervision [47]. That effort is not well distributed across the life cycle: the study by Hou
et al. [23] finds the majority of AI for Software Engineering approaches target code generation
and software testing, and few target software design and software architecture. The asymmetry is
understandable: code compiles or it does not. In contrast, architectural artifacts are communicative and partly subjective because business intent enters in the design phase. These intents are
constraints later phases inherit [39].

In this domain, the main problem is not the generation of the software architecture, but its
maintenance during the iterations and the entire software life-cycle. van Heesch and Avgeriou
[50] elaborate on architectural knowledge vaporization: the rationale behind a design as well as
its alternatives and trade-offs are the least recorded category of architectural knowledge. They
evaporate as systems evolve and their designers move on. What remains diverges from the running
system [18] and scatters across wikis, file shares, and repositories [19]. The Architecture as Code
(AaC) paradigm [7] addresses part of this: treating diagrams and specifications as plain-text source
under version control makes architecture reviewable, diffable, and, decisively for what follows,
machine-readable. But AaC solves storage and versioning, not the maintenance problem. A repository full of PlantUML does not update itself, flag its own drift, or synthesize rationale; Bucaioni
et al. [7] indeed list minimizing drift and ensuring conformance among the paradigm’s own open
challenges.
In this paper, we design the Agentic Software Architecture Workflow (A-SAW) extending AaC
with a persistent agent layer between the artifact store and the human reviewer. An artifact layer
holds version-controlled, machine-readable C4 models and design documents governed by a typed
element catalog that acts as a naming contract across every view; an agent orchestration layer
assembles context from that catalog, the system model, instrumented templates, and a featurescoped context folder, and generates a structurally complete first draft; and a human review layer
approves every draft. Hallucination is constrained structurally rather than by exhortation, through
five measures: catalog-grounded element references, context-folder scoping, explicit gap marking
instead of plausible filler, an automated check for phantom identifiers and macro misuse, and a
two-role approval gate.
We evaluate A-SAW using a rubric assessment on 45 architecture documents across three cohorts
(pre-tooling manual authoring, partial AaC adoption, and agentic generation). It exploits a matchedpairs design in which each of the 13 agentic documents regenerates a named human-authored
source. The main result shows that the standard deviation of document scores falls from 19.2 to
3.8 (ratio 5.0, 95% CI [2.9, 9.2]). Scores near the rubric maximum have little room to vary, so we
also check traceability, the dimension furthest below that maximum (79% in the agentic cohort).
Its standard deviation decreases as well, from 22.9 to 7.4. Quality is therefore much more uniform
across documents, and the 19-point gap between architecture and detailed design documents in
the manual cohort does not recur in the agentic one. Intrinsic quality also increases, by a median
of +19 points across the matched pairs (𝑝 = 0.001, rank-biserial 𝑟 = +0.97); the cohort means
of 58% and 89% bundle templates, catalog, agent and a richer regeneration context, and bound
the agent’s contribution from above rather than isolating it. 13 practitioners each performed a
30-minute hands-on regeneration task and were then interviewed. They reported that the workflow
cut first-draft effort from days to minutes and made design rationale easier to find. Their trust was
conditional: none expressed unconditional confidence in the diagrams they had just watched being
generated. Most insisted on human review (11 of 13), and their effort shifted from authoring toward
verification.
As a result, the contributions of this paper are:
• A process-level artifact: the A-SAW, a three-layer design (artifact / agent orchestration
/ human review) extending Architecture as Code with agentic first-draft authoring under
mandatory review, together with the element catalog as a machine-verifiable naming contract
across all C4 views (Section 3).
• An industrial evaluation with a matched-pairs design: 45 documents across three
cohorts, 13 of them matched source→regeneration pairs, quantifying the quality gain and
the variance collapse (Section 4.2).

2.1

Background
Architecture documentation and its quality

Clements et al. [10] establish the views-based framework together with the principles a sound
description must satisfy: descriptive completeness, internal consistency across views, recorded
rationale mapped to the requirements that motivated it, and usability for its intended readers.
Our rubric operationalizes those four properties as its dimensions (Section 4.1). Empirical work
approaches the same target from the practitioner side: Aghajani et al. [2] taxonomize the documentation problems developers actually report, and Aghajani et al. [1] survey how practitioners
rank their relevance, external corroboration that the properties we score are the ones that are
relevant in practice, though the instrument itself remains ours. We generate Architecture Design
Documents (ADDs) and Detailed Design Documents (DDDs), not Architecture Decision Records in
the narrow one-record-per-decision sense of Tyree and Akerman [49]: an ADD describes a feature’s
structure and embeds its decisions, with their alternatives and rationale, in dedicated sections.
In the vocabulary of ISO/IEC/IEEE [26], the workflow produces and maintains an architecture
description composed of views, with the element catalog playing the role of its correspondence rules.
2.2

Architecture as Code and the C4 model

Bucaioni et al. [7] define AaC not as "drawing diagrams with text tools" but as treating architectural
artifacts with the rigor applied to source code, version control, peer review, a text-based representation, generalizing docs-as-code from prose to the model itself. The consequence that matters here
is machine-readability: unlike binary formats, PlantUML sources can be read and written by an
LLM, which is what makes agent-assisted maintenance possible at all. Within this landscape the
C4 model [6] (Context, Container, Component, Code) supplies a hierarchical abstraction whose
upper levels serve as a durable system model, and following model-driven practice [41] we treat
its sources as the governing specification and the rendered diagram as a derived view. Measured
against the criteria Medvidovic and Taylor [31] set for architecture description languages this
notation is deliberately informal, trading analytic power for readability, low authoring friction,
and legibility to language models: the workflow’s objective is documentation upkeep, not formal
verification.

capabilities, enabling more autonomous behavior. Almost all realizations target implementationphase tasks. Where generative AI has reached architecture, Esposito et al. [14] report that rigorous

verification of outputs is typically absent. They also identify that hallucination, fluent but sourceunfaithful output [25], is among the most frequently reported challenges. We use architectural
hallucination for its manifestation here: a design that is internally plausible yet misaligned with
the actual system. This paper distinguishes two senses of the word agentic: In the orchestration
literature [40] agentic denotes a model-invocation pattern, tool calls, branching, self-correction,
inside a bounded task. Our usage is at the process level: the agent is a standing participant in an
organizational design loop over a persistent version-controlled context store, under institutional
review governance, while the individual act of generation is a single well-scaffolded pass. The
levels-of-automation continuum of Parasuraman et al. [36] removes any remaining ambiguity:
the workflow is high on information acquisition and analysis and deliberately low on decision
selection and action implementation, sitting at roughly level 4 of their ten-point scale, "the computer
suggests one alternative," which the human accepts, revises, or rejects. Retaining that decision point
is precisely the "last chance" error-trapping mechanism they recommend wherever an incorrect
automated decision is expensive. The workflow is therefore agentic without being autonomous, by
design rather than by limitation.
3 Our Approach
3.1 Research Method and Setting
Our research question is prescriptive rather than descriptive, not why architecture documentation
decays, but how a workflow can be designed so that it does not. Thus, we follow Design Science
Research [20, 37]: build a real artifact, deploy it, and evaluate it in use. The design iterated through
three refinement rounds before summative measurement, and the evaluation is naturalistic [52],
i.e., based on real users in an organization. Because documentation drift is a known problem and
its building blocks are individually established, the artifact is judged on demonstrable advance over
current practice rather than conceptual novelty [15].
The setting is a large-scale industrial software product organization that adopted the C4 model
as its architectural visualization standard roughly 18 months before this study and was, at its start,
migrating from a repository-based modeling tool to text-based definitions with PlantUML. About
15 architects and senior developers performed design work during the period studied. The first
author occupied a dual role throughout, as embedded practitioner building the artifact and as
investigator collecting and analyzing evaluation data, characteristic of action-research-adjacent
DSR in industry [8, 42]. It enables authentic data, tacit context, and the ability to iterate in a live
setting. It increases the risk of designing toward one’s own criteria and reading ambiguous evidence.
Three measures reduce that risk: triangulation of interview claims against the independent rubric
measurement, protocol standardization, and three exclusions from the interview pool. We excluded
the researcher, the company-side supervisor (whose design involvement exceeded normal exposure,
and who instead served as pilot participant), and the original author of the document used in the
hands-on task, who could reconstruct it from memory rather than recover it from the artifacts.

3.3

Conceptual Model: Adding an Agent Layer to Architecture as Code

Architecture-as-Code makes architectural artifacts reviewable, diffable, and reproducible, but they
remain passive: they record the state of architectural thinking at the moment they were written
and decay from that moment forward. The A-SAW introduces a persistent agent layer between
the artifact store and the human reviewer. This turns the repository from a place where humans
commit documents into the authoritative context source from which an agent reads constraints,
synthesizes documentation, and surfaces inconsistencies. Figure 1 shows the three layers and the
cycle connecting them.
The artifact layer holds version-controlled documents and diagrams in a feature-scoped structure.
Documents are machine-readable (Markdown with embedded PlantUML), follow standardized
templates, and reference every element by a catalog identifier shared across all views; this layer is
the ground truth, what humans read, review, and approve. The agent orchestration layer is IDEintegrated and operates on the artifact layer through repository-resident skill files, complemented
by separate prompt, instruction, and agent files (distinct file types serving different roles); agents are
invoked on demand, never commit to the main branch, and only propose. The human review layer
gates every proposal: reviewers validate catalog accuracy, assess decision rationale, supply external
references the agent cannot reach, and sign the approval table. Authorship accountability stays
human; the agent supplies first-draft coverage and structural consistency. The structure attacks
knowledge vaporization [50] by making rationale-carrying documentation the default output of the
design workflow rather than an optional retrospective effort. It produces as a by-product exactly
the structured, machine-readable context whose absence drives hallucination in retrieval-based
systems [59]. Retrieval over the repository is neither implemented nor planned here, but the artifact
layer maintains its substrate.

3.4

Architecture-as-Code Foundation

View selection. Three C4 views are used. The System Context (C1) and Container (C2) views are
maintained at system level as the status quo of the involved systems and form the ground truth
against which each feature is situated: C1 is the primary scoping input for Product Owners, C2 the
primary concern of architects. The Component (C3) view is generated selectively, typically within
a DDD, though it may also be generated standalone. The Code view is out of scope, consistent with
Brown [6]’s advice; where lower-level detail is needed, the DDD captures it at a more appropriate
abstraction.
Element catalog. A single file, Item_List.md, is the naming authority for every architectural
element. Elements carry one of seven typed identifier prefixes: A_ actor, S_ internal system, SE_
external system, C_ container, CD_ database, CQ_ queue, and B_ visual boundary. Every diagram
references those identifiers in its macro calls rather than a free-form display name, yielding a
machine-verifiable naming contract spanning all views and documents (R4). Listing 1 shows the
contract in use together with the two violation classes the post-generation check targets.
Repository structure. Each feature owns a folder holding its ADD.md or DDD.md, a context/
sub-folder of pre-existing inputs (capability specifications, vendor API documents, requirements
summaries, upstream design references), and its embedded PlantUML sources; system-level artifacts
(the C1/C2/C3 diagrams, the catalog, the templates) live under a shared path visible to every feature
branch. This instantiation deliberately consolidates architecture artifacts in a dedicated central
repository rather than co-locating them with the code they describe, as the original AaC formulation
suggests: the organization’s code spans many repositories, and centralization is what makes a
single naming authority and a single system model possible.

3.5

Templates and Agentic Generation

Two document types. An ADD describes a feature at the context and container levels and is
written when a feature introduces or changes a container, an external dependency, or a cross-system
interaction; its template has twenty sections, from scope and requirements to embedded C1/C2
(optionally C3) diagrams and design decisions. A DDD refines an ADD into implementation details
for one service or component in thirteen sections, adding, for example, component interaction
diagrams and API specifications. Both share the same envelope, so the same traceability obligations
apply at both levels. In the Design Decisions section, each entry must name at least one considered
alternative and why it was rejected (R5); this operationalizes the reasoning-knowledge category
van Heesch and Avgeriou [50] adopt.
In the refinement rounds, output quality depended more on the specificity of the scaffold than on
prompt wording. Templates with only headings and brief placeholders yielded drafts that were
structurally present but semantically thin, and expected-content descriptions with worked examples
improved them ("list each functional requirement as a separate statement beginning with The system
shall. . . " instead of "describe the requirements"). Later rounds added input validation at invocation
and a visual marker that keeps generated decisions findable in long documents.
Invocation and context assembly. A version-controlled skill file defines the draft/review/improve flow. The IDE assistant can select it autonomously when a request matches, or the practitioner
invokes it explicitly, e.g., with the slash command /new-architecture-doc from a prompt file,
supplying the five inputs listed in Figure 1. The agent then assembles context from four repository
sources, in the order shown there: the element catalog as naming authority; the system-level
C1/C2/C3 diagrams, which place the feature and separate internal from external systems by catalog
prefix; the document template; and the feature’s context/ folder, the practitioner’s main lever
over what the agent knows. Generation ran through GitHub Copilot, backed predominantly by
Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.8. Because the quality controls are natural-language instructions
that the model interprets, behavior depends on the backing model (Section 6). In the evaluated
configuration the agent had no access to the issue tracker or other external systems (possible via
the Model Context Protocol or an API token, but not enabled), so requirement identifiers had to be
in the context folder or be added by the reviewer; Section 4.3 reports the consequence.
Generation and validation rules. The skill enforces five structural rules. Every template
section must appear, with a visible [TODO: . . . ] marker instead of silent omission or plausible
filler where context is insufficient. C4 elements are referenced only by catalog identifier, and new
elements receive a proposed identifier flagged for catalog addition; macro selection follows the
identifier prefix. Feature-scope diagrams carry a new/modified legend, and design decisions follow
the R5 structure. The same review procedure runs at authoring and again at merge-request review,
because catalog correctness matters at both points. It blocks on identifiers that do not match the
catalog and flags proposed identifiers without a catalog entry or internal identifiers emitted with
the external-system macro; it also scans mandatory sections for unresolved placeholders. Taking
over these machine-verifiable checks leaves the reviewer to judge content plausibility and the
quality of the recorded rationale.

Human-in-the-Loop Governance

The workflow is built on the principle that the agent produces and the human decides, the stance
validated at industrial scale by Takerngsaksiri et al. [47]. The paper found practitioners accept
agentic assistance precisely when they retain authority to review, edit, and approve every output.
All agent-generated artifacts enter as merge requests. The document information table names the
generating agent explicitly whenever no human expert review has occurred; in production use
the field carries the practitioner’s name, and signing is what transfers responsibility. The approval
table specifies which roles must sign: the authoring architect, who validates diagram accuracy,
decision rationale, and catalog correctness; and the chief architect or system owner, who approves
consistency with the broader architecture (for a DDD, the architect owning the target container
also reviews the component, data model, and API sections). The revision history records every
change between draft and approval, including which reviewer requested it, producing a trail that
distinguishes human refinement from the agent’s first draft.
Five structural measures constrain architectural hallucination, responding directly to Esposito
et al. [14]’s finding that rigorous verification of generated architecture is typically absent: (i) cataloggrounded generation restricts element references to registered identifiers, removing the freedom to
invent names for existing elements; (ii) context-folder scoping limits reading to files the practitioner
placed there, so the agent can neither roam the repository nor reach external systems; (iii) explicit
gap marking surfaces what the agent could not establish instead of hiding it behind plausible
prose; (iv) the automated post-generation check catches the most common mechanical hallucination
patterns before human review begins; and (v) the two-role approval gate is the last barrier no draft
bypasses. Finally, non-determinism is treated as a documented property rather than a defect: the
same inputs may yield different drafts, which is precisely why output is governed as a first draft
requiring validation, with the proportion of human-confirmed content growing over the document’s
revision history.
4

Evaluation

We evaluate the A-SAW against four research questions (RQs):
RQ1 How does agentic, repository-grounded generation change the measured quality of architecture design documentation relative to manual authoring?
RQ2 Which documentation-quality properties does the agent close, and which remain humandependent?
RQ3 How do practitioners experience the workflow, and how far do they trust and govern its
output?
RQ4 Where do the measured quality of the artifacts and the perceptions of practitioners converge,
and where do they diverge?
4.1

Experimental setup

Corpus and cohorts. The corpus is a census of the 45 eligible architecture documents from the
partner organization, in three cohorts: A, pre-tooling manual authoring (12 ADD, 7 DDD; 𝑛 = 19);
B, partial AaC adoption under uncontrolled tooling use (6 ADD, 7 DDD; 𝑛 = 13); and C, unreviewed
first-pass regenerations by the finalized workflow (8 ADD, 5 DDD; 𝑛 = 13). Each document’s last
substantial-edit date, from commit or page history, separates A from B at February 2026. Candidates
from the architecture repository and wiki spaces were screened manually for primary design
artifacts that were substantive enough to score without speculation, reasonably complete, and not
duplicated. A draft label alone did not disqualify a document.

The Cohort C regeneration protocol. The workflow stabilized after the active featureauthoring period, so no organic Cohort C corpus exists. We constructed one by controlled regeneration. The 13 targets were selected on one structural criterion, a context/ folder that still
held the document’s original inputs rather than on expected output quality to avoid cherry-picking.
Each ran on a throw-away branch prepared by a three-step decontamination protocol: delete
the targets, delete downstream and derived artifacts, and check the remaining context/ files by
content, which removed three more files carrying derivation markers. The agent saw only the feature’s context/ folder, the catalog, the system diagrams, and the templates; its output received no
human correction before scoring and carried an attribution string marking it agent-generated and
unreviewed. Cohort C therefore measures the ability to structure and regenerate from already-rich
context; it does not test novel design from a blank slate. The 13 sources (nine from Cohort A, four
from Cohort B) average 70.5 against a Cohort A mean of 58%, so the targets were better than the
baseline average, as the criterion intended. Because the agent re-grounds each draft in the current
catalog, a regeneration may narrow the scope it inherits, and it is scored against its own scope.
The rubric. The rubric is grounded in Clements et al. [10], supported by [7, 18, 50], and has
four dimensions with twelve sub-criteria (Table 4): D1 completeness; D2 consistency, internal
and against the shared system model; D3 traceability of decisions to requirements and of their
rationale; and D4 maintainability for later readers, human or agent. Only self-containedness
among D4’s three sub-criteria is intrinsic, so the intrinsic analyses report D4 under that name.

criteria recorded in the instrument. Intrinsic sub-criteria are toolchain-independent and comparable
across cohorts; process-state dependent ones depend on infrastructure built during the project. They
are reported separately and never enter intrinsic means, which keeps Cohort A from being penalized
for standards that did not yet exist. Inapplicable sub-criteria are dropped rather than scored zero,
so per-document means are weighted; those in Table 2 therefore need not equal the unweighted

Interviews. Thirteen practitioners (7 architects, 6 engineers; professional experience from under
one year to over three years) each took part in a ∼60-minute session in June 2026. It opened with a
∼30-minute hands-on task on a branch from which the original ADD of one shared feature had

RQ3: How Do Practitioners Experience and Govern the Workflow?

The five scale-type anchor questions distribute as follows (𝑛 = 13; categories collapsed from the
coded responses, ambiguous ones classified by dominant content). Qualitative codes are given

Context availability improves due to one key mechanism. Ten of thirteen rated the new
approach better for locating design rationale and two same-to-better (12 of 13 judging it at least as
good), and none judged the old approach categorically superior. The mechanism they described is
that the structured repository moves context assembly from manual labor to standing infrastructure:
"it would take me days to do that on my own [. . . ] and probably even with AI, but without all the
context, the result would not be as good as this one" (P04). Two participants named the condition
attached to that gain: the labor is relocated, not eliminated, to whoever curates the feature context
("that job was saved, but that also is some work," P03), and two others identified an unresolved
staleness boundary, since repository-resident context can lag decisions still being negotiated
elsewhere (P07, P09).
Trust clusters at medium, and four patterns explain why. The modal response was "medium"
(5 of 13): None reported unconditional high trust, and even the two high ratings carried caveats.
(i) Trust decays with abstraction level: four participants described a gradient in which trust is
highest where the agent draws on existing structured data and lowest where it must infer across
undocumented systems, "it goes from a 90 to C1 to 60, 70 to C4" (P06), llustrating lower trust at more
detailed abstraction levels. (ii) Trust is earned, not granted: "at least for four to five designs, it should
generate those documents [. . . ] accurately, then only I will be able to trust it" (P01), calibrated
reliance in the sense of Lee and See [28], accruing over observed interactions rather than tracking
surface plausibility. (iii) Gains are attributed to input structure, not model capability: "I know that the
information it takes is much more structured, [so] the agent makes less errors" (P06). Consistently,
all four "less trust now" responses came from the no-prior-use group, suggesting that first exposure
can temporarily lower trust by making limitations salient while sustained use rebuilds it, suggestive
only at seven no-prior-use versus six experienced participants. (iv) Trust is split by modality: several
trust the agent more for template compliance than for diagram semantics, "in terms of following a
template [. . . ] I would trust AI-generated output more, because humans miss some details" (P13).

5.1

Discussion
Structural Quality and Trust

The divergence of Section 4.5 looks paradoxical only if trust is treated as a single quantity that high
quality should produce. Lee and See [28] distinguish trust in an automation’s performance, process,
and purpose, and McKnight et al. [30] draw a parallel distinction among competence, integrity,
and benevolence beliefs. The conditions participants attached to trust (Section 4.4) map onto these
dimensions, as an interpretive alignment rather than a validated coding. The abstraction-level
condition concerns performance trust, a competence belief that high consistency scores cannot
establish where the agent must infer across a thin system model. Trust earned through repeated
reliable use is process trust, which no single draft can supply. Whether the recorded design is right is
purpose trust, an integrity belief outside the documentation-quality construct. The polished-output
hazard runs the other way and has no McKnight counterpart. It is an irony of automation [3]: the
more fluent the automation, the less vigilantly it is monitored.
Process trust also accrues asymmetrically. People who see an algorithm err lose confidence in it
faster than in a human making the same error, and stay reluctant even when it outperforms the
human on average (algorithm aversion [13]). One visible error can therefore set back trust built
over many reliable generations, which strengthens the case for a review gate that intercepts errors
before they reach the practitioner. For practice, higher artifact quality alone does not establish
that artifacts will be trusted and relied upon. The same irony carries a skill-degradation risk that a
single-session design cannot observe [3]: teams that rely on the agent for first drafts may lose the
fluency to write architecture documentation unaided, and with it the expertise that review depends
on. Review should therefore remain a genuine cognitive act.

Transferable Design Principles

Five principles are intended to transfer; following Lincoln and Guba [29], the receiving context
judges transferability against Sections 3.1 and 4.1. (P1) Ground the agent in a persistent, versioncontrolled context store, such as the central AaC repository that makes grounded first drafts possible.
(P2) Enforce a machine-verifiable naming contract: a shared, typed identifier catalog turns an informal
convention into a constraint the agent satisfies by lookup instead of inference. (P3) Separate the
artifact, agent-orchestration, and human-review layers, so that automation and governance coexist
and accountability sits at the approval gate. (P4) Calibrate review to the agent’s competence gradient:
concentrate review where the agent must infer, and guard it against the polished-output hazard.
(P5) Design for earned trust and progressive autonomy: start with every output reviewed, and grant
more autonomy along the levels-of-automation continuum [36] only as reliability is demonstrated.
P2 and P4 are empirically derived here: P2 from the measured reduction in cross-document
inconsistency, the problem Szczepanik and Chudziak [46] independently report, and P4 from the
abstraction-level condition that four of the thirteen participants stated, a thin base offered as such.
P1, P3, and P5 are literature-supported [7, 36, 47] and consistent with our experience, but untested. In
particular, every regeneration target was selected for sufficient context, so P1’s premise that output
quality tracks context richness is assumed. The transferable contribution lies less in any single
principle than in their composition into one governable workflow.

5.3

Practical Implications and Boundary Conditions

Prerequisites and the practitioner’s quality lever. Besides the preconditions listed under
boundary conditions below, the workflow needs an IDE assistant with repository-resident extension
support. Building the catalog and the C1/C2 ground truth took a multi-month infrastructure phase,
which is required for coherent multi-author documentation at scale, with or without an agent.
After that, we expect output quality to depend above all on how complete the feature’s context/
folder is at invocation. Its preparation deserves first-class status with per-document-type guidance,
and part of the saved drafting effort is likely to be re-spent there. The highest-value extension is
connecting the issue tracker, whose absence accounts for 7 of the 13 agentic documents scoring
zero on ticket linkage, together with a convention that links tickets to features (requirements as
code, or a ticket reference in the context/ folder), because access alone does not tell the agent
which ticket belongs to which feature.
Protect review time, and frame the case as reallocation. Error detection required both domain expertise and dedicated review time (Section 4.4), so a workflow sold on speed can undermine
its own quality assurance unless part of the saved time is reinvested. Review should be budgeted
and non-optional, and it should go to reviewers with the relevant domain expertise, supported
by a checklist and the mandatory catalog and macro checks. The business case should promise
reallocation rather than reduction: a faster, more consistent first draft, with review effort that may
approach the drafting effort saved, or exceed it for complex features, but is spent on higher-value
work.
Boundary conditions. We expect the quality and consistency findings to depend on structural
preconditions more than on this organization: a C4 system model maintained at container level,
version-controlled text-based diagrams with a shared macro library that an LLM can read and
write, a typed element catalog or equivalent naming authority, and a codebase spread over enough
repositories that centralization pays. Where these hold, the direction of the quality and variance
findings should recur even if the magnitude differs. The findings should not be extended to greenfield
design of novel architecture, which the evaluation did not test. In small single-repository settings,

6.1

Threats to Validity
Construct and internal validity.

The rubric’s construct boundary (Section 4.1) leaves “effectiveness of the design phase” only
partly operationalized, so part of the consistency–trust gap reflects construct scope rather than a
measurement discrepancy. A single rater in a single pass makes measurement error inseparable
from true document variance, and without a second rating neither Cohen’s 𝜅 [11] nor the intraclass
correlation [44] can be computed. The rater also shares a model family with the generator, inviting
self-preference bias [16, 54], and was not blind to cohort, since Cohort C carries an agent-generated
attribution string; unrecorded document length leaves verbosity an unmeasured rival for part of the
level difference. Only the 0–3 behavioral anchors and triangulation against the practitioner track
bound these biases. Cross-cohort comparison is associational, and even the matched pairs cannot
separate the agent’s contribution from the instrumented templates and the richer regeneration
context. Two design effects push in opposite directions: first drafts lack revision history, which
depresses decision auditability against the workflow, whereas regenerating from existing context is
easier than blank-slate design. The first author’s dual role as designer and evaluator leaves residual
bias in the rubric and in reading ambiguous evidence, which triangulation, protocol standardization,
and the three subject-pool exclusions (Section 3.1) reduce but do not remove.
6.2

External validity and reliability.

A single organization and stack at one C4 maturity level support transferability [29] rather than
statistical generalization, so magnitudes are feasibility evidence, not predictions. In the interview
task, the time box makes in-session acceptance a poor proxy for endorsement, and the single
shared feature limits coverage of feature complexity. Participants’ unfamiliarity with the feature
understates error detection relative to a domain expert, and prior exposure to a demonstration
is a social-desirability risk that the task-first design only partly offsets. One human coder validated the AI-assisted first pass, so no inter-coder reliability statistic exists. Because the quality
controls are natural-language instructions, behavior depends on the backing model and would need
re-validation on another; the principles are model-agnostic, but their instantiation is not. The exploratory conversations that seeded the problem analysis were unrecorded, so their themes, though
corroborated by the baseline scores and later interviews, cannot be re-analyzed independently.

7.2

Architecture documentation, decisions, and Architecture as Code

Our four rubric dimensions operationalize, rather than extend, the views-based canon of Clements
et al. [10]; an externally grounded construct would instead come from the reported-issue taxonomy
and practitioner rankings of Aghajani et al. [2] and Aghajani et al. [1], so our derivation from the
canon and from Section 3.2 is treated as a construct-validity threat in Section 6. We target the
knowledge vaporization van Heesch and Avgeriou [50] characterize, but make rationale-carrying
documentation the workflow’s default output rather than a discipline the architect must sustain,
and adopt the first-class treatment of decisions of Tyree and Akerman [49] in substance without its
one-record-per-decision format. Our cohort comparison adds one data point to the thin empirical
base on degradation reported by Herold et al. [18], for documentation quality rather than code–
architecture conformance, and where Hesse and Paech [19] treat requirements and architecture
documentation jointly we address the architectural side alone. Our agent layer attacks the drift
and conformance challenges Bucaioni et al. [7] leave open for Architecture as Code, with the
deliberate deviation of centralizing rather than co-locating artifacts, and generalizes the docs-ascode precursor of Thomchick [48] from prose to the model. We adopt the C4 model of Brown [6],
Code view excluded on its own advice, as an agent’s machine-readable context source rather than
the comprehension aid Vázquez-Ingelmo et al. [51] evaluate; the premise of Rodrigues da Silva
[41] that a diagram is a derived view of a specification; a notation deliberately informal against
the criteria of Medvidovic and Taylor [31]; and the view/viewpoint/correspondence vocabulary of
ISO/IEC/IEEE [26], borrowed without claiming conformance.
7.3

Trust, automation, and the evaluation of generated artifacts

That near-ceiling measured quality does not produce high trust we explain (Section 5) with the
performance/process/purpose decomposition of Lee and See [28] and the competence/integrity/benevolence beliefs of McKnight et al. [30]; the misuse–disuse distinction of Parasuraman and
Riley [35] names the polished-output hazard one participant surfaced; the scale of Parasuraman et al.
[36] places the workflow at level 4; the ironies of automation of Bainbridge [3] we carry forward
as a longitudinal risk this design cannot observe; and the asymmetric confidence drop after an
observed error shown by Dietvorst et al. [13] is why we argue the review gate protects accrued trust
rather than only catching defects. On measurement we adopt the LLM-judge paradigm Zheng et al.
[60] establish while treating as live threats the reliability problem Gu et al. [16] identify as central
and the self-preference bias Wataoka et al. [54] demonstrate, defending the scores by triangulation
rather than by the paradigm’s reputation. Architectural hallucination is the domain-specific case of
the taxonomy of Huang et al. [25].
8

Conclusion

We propose A-SAW, a process-level artifact that extends Architecture as Code with a persistent
agent layer: an IDE-integrated agent fills instrumented templates from a version-controlled C4
model and a typed element catalog, and every draft passes a mandatory human review gate. Across
45 documents and 13 task-anchored interviews in a large industrial organization, intrinsic quality
rose by a median of +19 points over 13 matched pairs (𝑝 = 0.001, 𝑟 = +0.97), and the standard
deviation of document scores fell from 19.2 to 3.8, while traceability remained the lowest dimension.
