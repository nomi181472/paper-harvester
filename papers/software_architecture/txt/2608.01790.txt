Logarithmic Resonance at Mixed Boundary Junctions
in Gradient-Dependent Semilinear Equations
Marin Mišur∗

arXiv:2608.01790v2 [math.NA] 4 Aug 2026

August 5, 2026

Abstract
The regularity of solutions to elliptic partial differential equations degrades
severely at mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary junctions, characterized classically
by an O(r1/2 ) leading singular function. While this linear behavior is well documented, the introduction of gradient-dependent semilinear perturbations alters the
local asymptotic profile. This article proves that gradient penalties scaling linearly
with |∇u| induce a highly localized O(r−1/2 ) source term that resonates with the
half-integer spectrum of the principal homogeneous differential operator. This nonorthogonal resonance causes standard separable polynomial assumptions to fail at
order O(r3/2 ). We establish a generalized resonance theorem that forces the emergence of a logarithmic anomaly, providing the exact analytical formulation of the
resulting r3/2 ln(r) profile alongside rigorous local Sobolev regularity bounds for
the remainder. By calculating the exact logarithmic coefficients and angular offsets
for ℓ1 , ℓ2 , ℓ∞ , and arbitrary ℓq -norm penalties, we demonstrate the universality
of this obstruction. Finally, we formalize the corresponding enriched continuous
Galerkin space (XFEM), establish its quasi-optimality, and present finite element
experiments that confirm the predicted localized pollution, recover the predicted
logarithmic coefficients across the ℓ1 , ℓ2 , and ℓ∞ penalties on a curvature-free flat
junction, and show that resolving the junction recovers optimal degree-of-freedom
efficiency in standard finite element solvers; we close by outlining the targeted software architectures required for a fully enriched implementation.

Keywords. Mixed boundary value problem; Dirichlet–Neumann junction; corner singularity; semilinear elliptic equation; gradient-dependent nonlinearity; logarithmic resonance; asymptotic expansion; Fredholm alternative; extended finite element method
(XFEM).

The regularity theory of elliptic partial differential equations establishes that solutions
experience a severe loss of regularity in regions where boundary conditions abruptly transition. For mixed boundary value problems, the junction between a Dirichlet boundary

ΓD and a Neumann boundary ΓN alters the local Sobolev space in which the solution
resides. In the classical case of the linear Poisson equation with smooth data, the leading
exceptional function near a locally flat mixed junction takes the well-known singular form
u0 = c0 r1/2 sin(θ/2) [7, 16].
This O(r1/2 ) singular profile is a cornerstone of computational mechanics and numerical analysis, as it dictates the degradation of global convergence rates for standard
Galerkin finite element methods. Classically, optimal linear or quadratic convergence
is restored by incorporating this exact analytical profile into the discrete approximation space, either via highly localized mesh grading strategies [1] or explicitly through
extended finite element methods (XFEM) [10, 13, 5].
However, the introduction of a semilinear term, particularly one possessing a dependency on the gradient of the solution, drastically complicates this local boundary
behavior. Such gradient-dependent nonlinearities frequently arise in generalized transport models, non-Newtonian fluid mechanics, and stochastic control problems. Indeed,
the very study of mixed Dirichlet–Neumann problems for such semilinear equations was
motivated in [11] by the Dirichlet–Neumann boundary problem for scalar conservation
laws [12], where, in a setting arising from traffic flow modelling, an elliptic regularisation of the conservation law renders the method of compensated compactness applicable.
Because the gradient of the leading linear singularity scales as O(r−1/2 ), evaluating a continuous semilinear penalty near the mixed junction generates a strong, highly localized
non-homogeneous source term.
This article establishes a generalized resonance theorem for these gradient-dependent
semilinear equations. We prove that the first-order semilinear correction cannot be captured by standard separable polynomial assumptions. Instead, the localized forcing induced by the O(r−1/2 ) gradient resonates with the λ = 3/2 eigenspace of the associated
homogeneous Sturm-Liouville operator. This non-orthogonal resonance breaks the standard separation of variables, mandating the emergence of a logarithmic anomaly of the
form r3/2 ln(r). The appearance of logarithmic factors when the radial exponent of the
forcing coincides with an eigenvalue of the associated operator pencil is itself a classical
feature of the Kondrat’ev theory of corner asymptotics [8, 9, 14]; the novelty established
here is that the resonance is generated intrinsically by the gradient-dependent nonlinearity, rather than by a geometric coalescence of exponents at a reentrant corner.
The manuscript is structured as follows. Section 2 establishes the geometric configuration and defines the rigorous functional constraints required for the base forcing
and gradient penalty terms. Section 3 presents the core theoretical contribution: the
generalized resonance theorem and the proof of the exact analytical expansion, formally
supported by local Sobolev regularity bounds. Section 4 applies this theorem to derive the exact logarithmic coefficients and angular offsets for a variety of specific vector
norms (ℓ1 , ℓ2 , ℓ∞ , and a generalized ℓq ), proving the universality of the resonance. To
bridge theory and computation, Section 5 formalizes the enriched continuous Galerkin
formulation (XFEM) required to capture this anomaly. Section 6 reports finite element
experiments, built on the discretization of [11], that probe the predicted resonance, quantitatively validate the logarithmic coefficient against its analytic value, and demonstrate
the efficiency of junction-adapted refinement, and finally, Section 7 discusses specific software implementation architectures alongside future topological extensions of this theory
to three-dimensional collision edges.

Geometric and Functional Framework

Let Ω ⊂ R2 be a bounded domain with a piecewise Lipschitz boundary ∂Ω partitioned
into a Dirichlet part ΓD and a Neumann part ΓN . We isolate a transition point P ∈
ΓD ∩ ΓN . We assume that near P , the boundary is locally flat (an internal angle of π).
We place the origin of a local polar coordinate system (r, θ) at P , such that ΓD aligns
with θ = 0 and ΓN aligns with θ = π.
Remark 1 (Global Smoothness vs. Local Geometry). The assumption that the boundary
is locally flat at the transition point P means only that ΓD and ΓN meet with internal
angle α = π, i.e. without a geometric corner; it is a statement about the opening angle,
not about the curvature of ∂Ω, and it is decoupled from the global smoothness of ∂Ω. A
domain may possess a globally C 1 or Lipschitz-continuous boundary (e.g., a smooth circle
or ellipse) while still presenting such an angle-π junction where the boundary conditions
abruptly transition. Since the operator pencil (5) and the leading exponents depend only
on the opening angle, the asymptotic expansion of Theorem 1, and in particular the
logarithmic coefficient A = −2κ/(3π), is a leading-order (r → 0) statement that holds at
any such junction. When ΓD and ΓN are curved rather than straight, however, curvature
enters as a finite-radius correction: the local Laplacian is no longer exactly separable, so
the measured coefficient departs from its flat-wedge value at any fixed r > 0 and converges
to it only as r → 0. This is precisely what the disc experiments of Section 6 exhibit,
where the extracted A/c0 overshoots the flat-wedge prediction and is recovered exactly
only on a genuinely straight junction. If the junction instead coincides with a physical
corner of angle α ̸= π, the leading linear singularity shifts, which alters the spectrum of
the local Sturm-Liouville operator.

Examples of Admissible Functions
To clarify the scope of these assumptions, we provide examples of forcing and penalty
functions that fall within this defined framework:
• Admissible Base Forcing f (x, u): The semilinear term utilized in the numerical
scheme by Mišur (2021), defined as f (x, u) = x21 + x22 + log(1 + |u|), is admissible
because it remains bounded or decays near the origin when evaluated at u0 [11].
Further polynomial examples include f (x, u) = c(x)|u|q for q > 0 and c ∈ L∞ (Ω),
since the evaluation uq0 ∼ rq/2 is guaranteed to remain in Lploc (Ω). Similarly, exponential nonlinearities common in reaction-diffusion equations (e.g., f (x, u) = eu )
are admissible because u0 → 0 as r → 0, ensuring the term remains bounded locally.
• Admissible Gradient Penalty g(∇u): The sum of the absolute values of the
Cartesian partial derivatives, g(∇u) = |∂x1 u| + |∂x2 u| (the ℓ1 -norm), is strictly
positively homogeneous of degree 1 and is therefore admissible [11]. The standard Euclidean norm g(∇u) = |∇u| (ℓ2 -norm) and the maximum norm g(∇u) =
max(|∂x1 u|, |∂x2 u|) (ℓ∞ -norm) naturally satisfy Assumption 2.
• Anisotropic Gradient Penalties g(∇u):
p In addition to standard isotropic norms,
anisotropic metrics such as g(∇u) = λ1 |∂x1 u|2 + λ2 |∂x2 u|2 are also admissible.
More complex, positively homogeneous directional penalties satisfy Assumption 2
as well, provided they scale linearly with the gradient magnitude and satisfy the
global Lipschitz constraint.
• Advective (Non-Norm) Penalties g(∇u): The admissible class extends beyond
symmetric norms. An advection-biased penalty g(∇u) = |∇u| + β · ∇u, with fixed
drift β = (β1 , β2 ) ∈ R2 , is positively homogeneous of degree 1 and globally Lipschitz
(constant 1+|β|), hence admissible, yet it is not a norm, taking negative values when
|β| > 1, and it models a dissipation biased along a preferred transport direction.

Evaluating on v(θ) gives the angular profile G(θ) = c20 1−β1 sin(θ/2)+β2 cos(θ/2) ,
and projecting onto the resonant eigenfunction yields the tunable resonance constant κ = c30 + c20 β2 .
Remark 5 (Sharpness of the non-orthogonality condition). The advective penalty shows
that Assumption 3 is a genuine hypothesis rather than an automatic consequence of
admissibility. The along-junction drift β1 leaves κ untouched, since the profile sin(θ/2) it
contributes is L2 -orthogonal to sin(3θ/2), whereas the transverse drift β2 shifts κ linearly,
and at the critical value β2 = − 23 the resonance constant vanishes. There the logarithmic
anomaly disappears and the solution regains a pure-power expansion at order r3/2 : a
suitably tuned transport bias can cancel the singular pollution.

standard Gauss rules; passing to polar coordinates dx = r dr dθ absorbs the r−1/2 gradient singularity and renders the integrand bounded. Both points are taken up among the
software considerations of Section 7.

6

Numerical Comparison

To probe the theoretical predictions, we carried out a sequence of finite element experiments built directly on the FreeFEM++ discretization of Mišur (2021) [11]: the semilinear
problem (17) on the unit disc, with the Dirichlet and Neumann arcs meeting at (±1, 0),
discretized with continuous P1 elements and solved by Picard (fixed-point) iteration to a
relative H 1 tolerance of 10−5 . All errors are measured against high-resolution junctionadapted reference solutions, graded towards the mixed junctions. Each reference carries
more total degrees of freedom (about 3.2–3.3 × 105 ) than the finest uniform test mesh,
with its bulk element size capped below that of the finest quasi-uniform mesh and its
junction grading refined to a minimum element size at least as small as that of the most
strongly adapted test mesh (Configuration B); it is therefore at least as fine as every test
discretization throughout the domain and far finer in the immediate vicinity of the junctions, where the singular structure, and hence the discretization error, concentrates. A
self-convergence check bounds the reference error: refining the adaptmesh interpolationerror target by a factor of two, from 10−4 to the adopted 5 × 10−5 , which raises the
reference from 1.7 × 105 to 3.3 × 105 degrees of freedom, shifts the reference by 2.1 × 10−3
in relative H 1 and 2.9 × 10−5 in the local L∞ near the junctions, both below every tabulated error, so the reported values are limited by the test discretization rather than by
19

the reference. The local error is sampled within a neighborhood of radius ϵ = 0.1 around
each junction.
We report four experiments:
• Resonance control. The gradient penalty is switched off and on (g(∇u) = 0
versus a norm penalty) on an identical quasi-uniform mesh family, isolating the
effect of the resonant term. We run this control for both the anisotropic ℓ1 -norm
|∂x1 u| + |∂x2 u| and the isotropic ℓ2 -norm |∇u|, whose predicted resonance constants
differ (κ = c0 /2 versus c0 /3).
• Configuration A (uniform baseline). The standard P1 Galerkin scheme on
quasi-uniform meshes, with no special treatment of the junction.
• Configuration B (junction-adapted). The identical solver combined with local
mesh adaptation concentrating degrees of freedom around the junctions (±1, 0),
realizing the “exact local mesh adaptation” route anticipated in Section 5.
• Coefficient extraction. From a high-order (P2 ) junction-adapted solution we
directly measure the logarithmic amplitude A predicted by Theorem 1, both on the
curved disc and on a curvature-free flat junction.

Direct Validation of the Logarithmic Coefficient

The experiments above confirm the localization of the resonance; we now confirm its exact
amplitude. Theorem 1 predicts the dimensionless ratio A/c0 = −2κ/(3πc0 ) between the
logarithmic coefficient A and the leading stress-intensity coefficient c0 : explicitly −1/(3π),
−2/(9π), and −1/(6π) for the ℓ1 , ℓ2 , and ℓ∞ penalties respectively (Section 4). To
measure it we take a high-order (P2 ) junction-adapted solution and, at each small radius

ρ, least-squares project the angular trace u(ρ, ·) onto the mixed eigenmodes sin (k + 12 )θ .

discretization artifact: the P1 and P2 extractions differ by only about 5%, so refining
the discretization order closes only a small part, of order 10%, of the gap; the residual
is the finite-radius signature of the disc’s curved Dirichlet and Neumann arcs, whereas
Theorem 1 is a flat-wedge (α = π) statement. Second, removing the curvature confirms
this diagnosis and, further, verifies the norm-dependence of the coefficient. Table 5 reports

|A/c0 |ℓ∞ in exact accord with κ = c0 /2 > c0 /3 > c0 /4. This simultaneously validates the
coefficient formula A = −2κ/(3π) and the norm-universality established analytically in
Section 4, not merely the existence and sign of the anomaly.

Taken together, these experiments confirm both the localized structure and the exact
analytic content of the theory: an energy-norm rate governed by the r1/2 singularity,
a junction-concentrated higher-order penalty contribution whose magnitude tracks the
resonance constant κ across norms (ℓ1 versus ℓ2 ), and, on a curvature-free flat junction,
logarithmic amplitudes matching the predicted A = −2κ/(3π) to within 1.6% for all three

7

Future Work

The theoretical framework established in this article opens several avenues for immediate
computational implementation and broader topological generalization.

7.1

Software Architectures for Enriched Solvers

Realizing the fully enriched (XFEM) solver anticipated in Section 5, and thereby a direct
validation of the logarithmic coefficient itself, requires a computational infrastructure capable of handling the enriched Galerkin formulation. Standard finite element packages
23

are highly optimized for polynomial bases, making the injection of localized logarithmic singularities a non-trivial software engineering challenge. On the analytical side, a
complete a priori convergence analysis of the enriched scheme, providing self-contained
blending-layer and stabilization estimates tailored to the r3/2 ln r enrichment and making
rigorous the leading-order rate stated conditionally in Proposition 3, will accompany the
implementation. Future work will pursue two distinct implementation architectures to
evaluate the enriched scheme:
• The FreeFEM++ Baseline: To provide direct continuity with the original
numerical scheme [11], one path involves augmenting the FreeFEM++ environment. Because the framework lacks native XFEM support for arbitrary logarithmic
anomalies, future work will require developing custom macros to manually intercept the stiffness matrix assembly. This entails accurately computing the enriched
integrals near the singularity and manually orchestrating the dense coupling blocks
