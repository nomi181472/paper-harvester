Abstract
Around 30% of global energy expenditure can be attributed to the building sector,
where a large portion of energy-consumption could be avoided by repairing existing faults.
Fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) software addresses this issue; however, its creation
and operation also have an environmental impact. The magnitude of this impact is influenced by the diagnosis architecture, as different architectures and methods have different
energy demands. Yet, simply considering the energy consumed by the software itself is
not sufficient to assess its overall environmental impact, since the diagnostic performance,
e.g., number of detected faults or number of faults missed, also contributes to its ecological
footprint.
In this paper, we propose an energy-consumption model that considers FDD performance and energy spend directly by the diagnosis software. In an initial experiment, we
compare several FDD architecture families, i.e., rule-based, model-based, classical machine
learning, and large-language-model-based, in simulation using performance and energyconsumption values collected from prior literature. The results show that considering the
computational energy and accuracy of FDD can change the relative benefit of the different
approaches. Computationally efficient machine learning methods, such as random forest,
provide the largest net savings on smaller buildings, whereas more resource-intensive approaches, such as fine-tuned large language models, become advantageous as building size
increases. Our findings suggest that overall energy efficiency depends not only on the
computational demand of the FDD software, but also on its diagnostic performance and
the scale of the building.

Introduction

The energy expenditure of residential and commercial buildings constitutes a critical challenge in achieving the United Nations’ climate targets. Recent estimates indicate that the
domain is responsible for 40% of the final energy-consumption in the European Union [15]
and 30% worldwide [18]. Roughly 75% of the EU building stock is energy inefficient [14],
with operational faults as a major contributing factor [8]. Notably, heating, ventilation, and
air conditioning (HVAC) systems account for a considerable portion of the energy demand in
the building sector. Studies estimate that simply repairing existing HVAC faults could substantially reduce the energy-consumption [2]. Thus, fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) for
building systems is a highly relevant strategy for ensuring energy efficiency and the achievement of sustainability goals.
1

Historically, FDD in buildings has primarily relied on knowledge-based approaches [30, 7,
59, 34]. However, in recent years, the widespread adoption of building automation systems
(BAS), increasing availability of sensor data, and advancements in data science have led to the
development of various machine learning (ML) techniques for FDD [75]. ML has proven to be
a suitable approach, which can achieve high detection and diagnostic accuracy while requiring
little to no prior knowledge of the system’s underlying composition or dynamics [33, 6, 73, 23].
One goal of FDD in buildings is to improve energy efficiency, yet sophisticated FDD methods can be computationally costly to train and operate, leading potentially to a substantial
amount of energy use and therefore additional carbon emissions. The questions of how much
energy software requires and which design choices influence its resource consumption have
been studied in software engineering previously. In software engineering, sustainability has
been established as a multi-dimensional property of software quality in its own right, which
includes technical, economic, social, and environmental aspects [38, 64, 5]. In our work, we
are interested in the environmental dimension, in particular, in the environmental impact of
FDD software. While most definitely the implementation itself, i.e., the underlying code, affects the ecological footprint of software, there have been studies that show that also software
architecture can measurably influence the energy demands of a system [16, 50, 25]. In general, architectural decisions shape a system’s structure and behavior. Therefore they influence
the quality and sustainability of software products. Moreover, changing design decisions is
expensive, especially later in the software life cycle [63].
With the adoption of complex ML methods, which are associated with high energy demands for training [61], the conversation on software sustainability has extended to the domain
of artificial intelligence (AI). A recent debate contrasts “Red AI”, which prioritizes performance without regard for environmental cost, with “Green AI”, which favors computational
efficiency and reduced energy use while maintaining acceptable accuracy [57, 65]. Recent
work addresses the architectural angle within the discussion around sustainable ML with the
goal to determine “green” architectural strategies [43, 27].
“Green software” and “Green AI” often aim to reduce the energy-consumption of the
software under consideration. In the building domain, the FDD software not only consumes
energy itself, but it is also intended to enable energy savings in the building it monitors. Thus,
to minimize the overall environmental footprint, one has to consider the energy consumed by
the software as well as the energy savings enabled by the software. In practice, this raises
the question of which of the available FDD techniques is the most overall energy-efficient
solution. Answering this question is more intricate than it initially seems, since the commonly
used methods differ largely in the computational resources they require and their diagnostic
quality. For instance, diagnosing a building from a small set of threshold-based rules can be
done very efficiently, however, if the rule-based system raises a large number of false alarms
the additional energy needed for the unnecessary maintenance activities offsets some of the
computational efficiency of the FDD approach. The reverse may also happen, where a model
is associated with substantially more resources to be able to diagnose a system, e.g., a deep
neural network, yet it may be able to detect faults more reliably and therefore may decrease
the energy expenditure of the building. Consequently, the computational energy required to
operate the diagnosis software has a direct environmental impact, whereas the energy savings
resulting from diagnosis activities have an indirect, enabling impact [37]. Often there might
be a tradeoff between diagnostic performance and environmental impact, yet both affect the
ecological footprint of a building. Thus, in order to answer the question which FDD technique
is the most overall energy-efficient solution, we must analyze which combination of the two

In this paper, we address this question in the context of building HVAC systems by making
the following contributions: First, we introduce an energy-consumption model that relates
fault detection performance of an FDD method to the energy-consumption of a building
affected by faults. Second, we extend this model with energy requirements associated with
the training of the diagnosis method as well as its inference. Third, we compile diagnosis
performance and energy-consumption data reported for the considered methods from the FDD
literature and use the resulting values to compare the methods in simulation experiments to
assess the suitability of the different diagnosis architectures and methods.

Diagnosis System Architecture

In this section, we discuss the different FDD methods considered in this study together
with their corresponding high-level software architectures. Afterward, we summarize different
evaluation metrics for FDD, which we later use to estimate the energy consumption of the
introduced methods.

2.1

Diagnosis methods

Rule-based systems have been successfully used in medicine [4] but also in diagnosis of engineered systems [54, 55]. The underlying idea of (rule-based) expert systems is to encode
domain expertise as statements such as if observation then cause to determine the reason
behind a certain observation or symptom.

i.e., a monitoring system [60], which stores the information in a system data base. The
evaluation component checks for consistency regarding the given rules, which are stored in a
rule base. This evaluation component can be as simple as any ordinary program implementing
if-then-else rules or a more sophisticated theorem prover that checks whether the current

Although rule-based systems can encompass a wide variety of knowledge, the interdependence between rules makes this type of system difficult to maintain as the size of the rule
base increases. As an answer to this limitation, researchers have proposed model-based diagnosis [11, 12, 53, 13]. Model-based diagnosis differs from rule-based diagnosis in the way
faults are detected. The former utilizes a model for detecting a faulty behavior, which requires means for executing the model in order to derive the expected system behavior from
the model. The expected behavior is then compared with the observed behavior. Hence, inference is more complex compared to ordinary rule-based systems. Therefore, each inference
step has a higher energy-demand.
Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of a model-based FDD. Even though its architecture is similar to the architecture of rule-based diagnosis, we see that instead of a rule
base a system model is exploited. As a consequence the evaluation component has to be more
sophisticated and allow for inference whether the observed values from the system database
contradict the values obtained from the model. For implementing the evaluation/inference
component we can follow two principles, (1) the use of logical models and reasoning engines
that are either based on consistency [53, 13, 48] or abduction [17, 68, 36], or (2) the use of
mathematical models, e.g., differential equations.
Machine learning (ML) is an essential subfield of AI and has gained a lot of attention,
mainly starting in the current century, also in the context of FDD. Many ML methods, such
as decision trees [51], support vector machines (SVM) [1], neural networks [20], clustering [26],
regression [45], or ensemble methods (e.g., random forests [21]) have been used for prediction and classification. In the case of FDD, we are interested in the latter. However, the
fundamental idea behind all ML methods is the same: we utilize data to learn a model. In
particular, we always distinguish two phases in ML, i.e., the training/learning of a model

Due to distinguishing training (or learning) from inference and reasoning, the software
architecture comprises additional components (see Figure 3). Importantly, we distinguish

model and system data. To work appropriately the system data and the training data should
have the same stochastic distribution.
Recently, the advances in large language models (LLMs) have led to an increasing amount
of research on LLMs in different application domains, e.g., FDD in BAS [40]. LLMs are
AI models that predict the next token or sequence of tokens from a given sequence of input tokens. Figure 4 shows the high-level software architecture of an LLM-application for
BAS diagnosis. Similar to ordinary ML, there is a component for learning. In practice, the
energy-consumption of this component is far higher than for other ML methods, because
LLMs are trained on a huge corpus of data and do not focus usually on the system under
surveillance. Since LLMs are general-purpose applications, there might be a further training step involved to introduce system specific information—we will later refer to LLMs with
additional application-specific training as fine-tuned LLMs. Alternatively, the application
might make use of reference system data and RAG in the further evaluation/inference step.
Furthermore, inference might be recursively applied until a suitable solution is found, e.g., a
classification result can be obtained. Hence, evaluation and inference are also more energy
demanding compared to ordinary ML methods.

Energy-consumption Model

Answering the question of which diagnosis methodology and straightforwardly its corresponding software architecture to use in order to obtain the most energy-efficient solution requires
a detailed analysis of (1) the method’s energy-consumption and (2) its effects on the energydemand of the system to be diagnosed. Before developing our energy-consumption model,
we need to state its scope. In our modeling approach, we consider a horizon of one year
and assume that the building, its HVAC system and the deployed diagnosis method remain
unchanged over this period. In particular, we do not model the aging of components or the
associated growth in fault probability of a system’s lifetime. Additionally, we represent faults
as an aggregate through an annual fraction of wasted energy rather than as a distribution
over individual faults. We further restrict our method to consider fault detection, rather than
fault identification and repair.

7

3.1

Energy-Consumption Model (ECM )

We start defining the energy-consumption of buildings for cooling and heating EHV AC considering the energy use intensity EU I of a building (i.e., the fault-free required energy per
square meter and year), its square meters A, and the fraction of the energy-consumption used
for heating or cooling sHV AC .
EHV AC = sHV AC · EU I · A

(1)

Faults often lead to an additional energy-demand that has to be considered. For example,
a broken sensor indicating the room has not yet reached the required temperature or a nonoptimal setup of parameters in HVAC may cause the system to use more energy for heating.
Hence, from a general perspective the additional energy lost is proportional to the optimal
energy used for heating and cooling. For computing this wasted energy, we introduce the
fraction of energy lost sF AU LT in the case of a fault.
EW AST ED = sF AU LT · EHV AC

(2)

It is worth noting that the wasted energy for a fault may be up to 40% [2]. Note further
that a more detailed model may consider different types of faults, fault frequency, and fault
duration. However, in any case the sum of wasted power over time can be summarized in the
annual fraction of energy lost.
The total energy needed for heating and cooling ET HV AC of a building in one year without
considering diagnosis is stated in the following equation:
ET HV AC = EHV AC + EW AST ED

(3)

After defining the model of energy-consumption of HVAC systems in buildings considering
faults, we now focus on the impact of FDD on BAS. In particular, we reduce our view on FDD
to fault detection and ignore fault localization, i.e., identifying the root cause of the fault. The
reason is that several diagnosis approaches in the context of BAS only consider the detection
of an unwanted or unexpected behavior but not on its correction, which often is carried out
by maintenance personnel. Unfortunately, no approach guarantees a 100% detection rate.
Therefore, we have to consider two errors. First, the case that an FDD approach classifies a
value to be wrong but it is not, which raises a wrong alarm. Such a false positive error or
type 1 error, causes further investigations but does not necessarily lead to a degraded system.
Second, we may not be able to classify a wrong value as indicating a fault. Such a false
negative error or type 2 error, does not lead to an alarm and a fault remains. Depending on
the classification outcome, we now describe the impact on the energy-consumption.
True positives (TP): There is a fault detected, which is a real fault. Hence, when correcting the fault, e.g., by replacing a component or changing a setup, a saving in the
energy-consumption, which is proportional to the wasted energy due to faults, is expected. The factor of detected faults is given by the true positive rate T P R. In our
case, we consider an annual T P R, i.e., T P Ra , since our time-horizon spans over a single
year.
ESAV IN G = T P Ra · EW AST ED

8

(4)

If all faults can be detected, then there is no energy wasted. Note that in practice, we
may still see a wasted energy due to faults, because there is always a delay of repair,
which causes additional energy to be wasted. However, compared to the total amount
of energy, this remaining wasted energy can be neglected.
True negatives (TN): There is no fault represented in the observations. Hence, no action
has to be taken and thus there is no impact on energy-consumption.
False positives (FP): False positives lead to unnecessary maintenance activities, which
may cause additional energy-consumption. Besides the energy that is needed to perform
the repair itself, a false positive may lead to the unnecessary replacement of components
still operating as intended. These are, however, secondary energy impacts.
Overall, we assume a wasted energy for FPs, which is proportional to the false positive
rate F P R but only applied to a fraction of the total energy needed for HVAC. The
latter is represented by the factor sF P . Again, we consider F P R as an annual rate
F P Ra .
EW AST ED F P = F P Ra · sF P · EHV AC

(5)

False negatives (FN): Not identifying all faults causes wasted energy. However, this is
already part of the energy-consumption model, since EW AST ED already accounts for
the additional energy-demand in case of a fault.
To complete the energy-consumption model considering any diagnosis method, we add
the following equation, which defines the total energy-consumption after diagnosis of a BAS.
EHV AC DIAG = ET HV AC − ESAV IN G + EW AST ED F P

(6)

Equations 1 to 6 define the overall energy-consumption model ECM . This model already captures important diagnosis-related parameters, i.e., the true positive rate, the false
positive rate, and the false negative rate. Other aspects, which are related to the diagnosis
methodology are not captured. Table 1 depicts all input parameters of the ECM model.

4.1

Input Data

For the experiments reported in this paper, we obtain the input values from research on FDD
in the building domain. In particular, our starting point was a recent review by Chen et
al. [6] on data-driven FDD methods for HVAC systems. We considered all comparison papers
that perform FDD on chiller or AHU systems to obtain the rates reported in Table 3. To
this end, we extracted all usable T P R/Recall and F P R values for our considered diagnosis
methods from the reviewed studies. Since, however, many studies we examined do not provide
the necessary data, i.e., T P R/Recall, F P R, or a confusion matrix to compute the metrics,
we utilized additional chiller- and AHU-specific sources in order to obtain at least a single
published value per FDD method.
Furthermore, the energy model of Section 3 requires annual values, i.e., T P Ra and F P Ra ,
which describe the behavior of the model over a full year. Even though the reported T P R
and F P R from the literature are based on a single experiment or a set of experiments, we use
a simplification by assuming that the rates remain stable throughout the year. Thus, for our
simulation, we use the range of values we were able to find in the literature for T P R/F P R
as an approximation for T P Ra /F P Ra .
Besides the information on the prediction accuracy of the considered architectures and
methods, our extended energy model EECM relies on information on the energy-consumption
of training the model (i.e., ET M ET HOD ), the utility factor U , and the energy consumed by
performing a single inference step (i.e., EBIN F ). Table 4 depicts those figures based on prior

Limitations

Our study combines a theoretical energy-consumption model with simulation experiments
using values from prior literature. The validity of the results and conclusions drawn therefore
depends on the model’s underlying assumptions and simplifications, as well as the quality of
the input data.

5.1

Model assumptions

As with any theoretical model, our proposed ECM and EECM rely on several assumptions
and simplifications that limit how accurately they represent a concrete building and its HVAC
system. First, we consider a one-year time horizon rather than the entire building life cycle.
During this period, we assume the building, its HVAC system, and the FDD methods used
remain unchanged. Consequently, our models do not capture long-term changes, such as
building modifications, replacement or degradation of HVAC components, or updates to the
FDD system. We also do not model component aging and the related increase in fault
probability. Given these simplifications, the energy-consumption models should be interpreted
as approximations rather than precise representations of a building’s behavior over its full
lifetime.
Second, we combine fault effects into a single parameter. Instead of modeling individual
fault types with different severities, occurrence frequencies, and durations, we approximate
the effects of all faults as a fraction of the annual HVAC energy loss. This simplification allows
us to compare the different diagnosis architectures and methods without requiring a detailed
fault model for each specific HVAC system. Consequently, our models do not account for the

18

distinct ecological impacts of individual faults.
Third, our models do not consider fault identification, localization, and repair. We assume
that once a fault is detected, it is immediately corrected and the associated energy savings
take effect. In practice, however, detecting a fault is often not sufficient. In order to restore
the nominal system behavior the faulty component has to be identified. Furthermore, there
is typically a delay between fault detection and the corresponding repair action. During this
interval, the fault persists and continues to waste energy. Our models do not factor in the
additional energy and resources that may be attributed to the repair and replacement activity, e.g., recommissioning. We assume these effects on the operational energy are negligible
compared to the annual HVAC energy-consumption.
Finally, as noted above, our models capture building scale through area and energy use
intensity but do not explicitly represent the complexity of the HVAC system. Two buildings
with a similar area and energy demand may differ in the number of HVAC components (i.e.,
the possible subsystems that may experience faults) and the effort required to diagnose and
maintain the system. Our models do not directly account for this.

5.2

Validity of input data and parameter estimates

Our simulation experiments are based on values obtained from prior literature on the diagnostic accuracy of FDD methods and the ecological impact of various architectures and
methods. The reliability of our results therefore depends on the quality, comparability, and
representativeness of the underlying input data. While we rely on prior published research for
our data collection, it was not conducted as an exhaustive systematic review; consequently,
other relevant studies may contain values outside the ranges reported.
Diagnostic performance data Our starting point was a recent review by Chen et al. [6],
which analyzes research on FDD diagnosis techniques for HVAC systems. Thus, the studies
selected from the review differ with regard to the HVAC systems under investigation, data
sets, fault types, operating conditions, experimental setups, and the diagnosis tasks considered
(i.e., fault detection or identification). Consequently, the values we obtained are not directly
comparable, but they reflect the heterogeneity across HVAC FDD.
The availability of suitable performance data has been an issue. Many studies we examined
do not report the required metrics (i.e., T P R, F P R, or confusion matrices from which these
metrics can be derived), nor do they provide reproducibility packages containing the results.
Therefore, we included additional sources where necessary. Yet, for methods where only a
few values have been compiled, the resulting estimates carry greater uncertainty.
Another simplification concerns the temporal interpretation of the reported performance
values. The literature values are rooted in individual experiments or sets of experiments
rather than observations over an entire year. Yet our models require the annual true positive
and false positive rates. We therefore assume the reported values are suitable approximations
for T P Ra and F P Ra , although in practice, diagnostic performance over a year may vary, e.g.,
due to data drift. We partially address these concerns by documenting the data extraction and
relying on minimum, maximum, and average values rather than individual values reported in
the literature.
Computational energy data As with the diagnostic performance data, we obtained the
computational energy values from prior literature. However, we did not limit our analysis to
19

HVAC applications, since application-specific energy measurements are sparse in the building
domain. Consequently, the reported values stem from different application domains, hardware platforms, software frameworks, model configurations, and experimental setups. We
acknowledge that all of these factors can substantially influence an FDD method’s energyconsumption. However, we retain the values and use their ranges to capture the variability.
In addition, suitable energy data were not available for all architectures. In particular, for
RB and MBD, we rely on estimates derived from reported runtimes. Although this aligns with
prior research indicating a relationship between runtime and energy-consumption [3, 76, 67],
these values remain approximations introduced to compare the various FDD architectures.
For LLMs, we add further assumptions. Since LLMs are not used exclusively for building
FDD, we allocate only a fraction of the overall LLM training cost to the diagnosis task. We
also approximate the additional energy required for fine-tuning an existing LLM. As a result,
the computational energy values should be interpreted as approximations.
Lastly, we do not account for all energy expenditures associated with developing and
maintaining the different diagnosis methods. For example, feature selection, hyperparameter
tuning, prompt design, rule creation, and creating models from first principles are not included
in the reported training energy. While these activities may require substantial effort and
computational resources, their energy demand is difficult to quantify consistently across the
considered architectures.

5.3

Generalizability

First, the generalizability of our simulation results is limited by the HVAC subsystems considered. In particular, we focus on chiller and AHU applications. Consequently, the numerical
results do not necessarily transfer directly to other HVAC systems or fault types.
Furthermore, the relative benefit of a diagnosis approach depends on characteristics of
the monitored systems. While we cover a selected range of parameters (e.g., building area),
we do not consider other potentially relevant properties, including occupancy patterns, environmental conditions, or usage profiles. Accounting for such factors may change the relative
benefit and ranking of the FDD methods considered.
Finally, our current experiments rely on literature values obtained from specific implementations of the considered architectures and methods. A highly optimized implementation of
a particular method may exhibit substantially different diagnostic performance or computational energy demand than the ranges we currently report. Therefore, the rankings observed
in our simulations should not be interpreted as universal rankings of the FDD methods, but
rather as an illustration of the underlying relationship: the preferred architecture depends on
diagnostic performance, computational energy demand, and building scale.

6

Conclusions

In this paper, we investigate a selection of software architectures for FDD in building systems.
We introduce an energy-consumption model that relates the performance of an FDD architecture/method to the energy consumption of the monitored HVAC system. In an extended
model, we also account for the energy-demand of model creation/learning and inference. We
utilize the models in a set of initial simulation experiments to compare the various architectures and methods based on performance and energy expenditure values collected from prior
literature.
20

The results indicate that for office buildings, the energy required for training and inference
is negligible, since the energy demand of the HVAC system itself accounts for a substantial
portion of the energy-consumption. In this case, fine-tuned LLMs provide the largest net
energy savings. For single-family houses, the architecture’s energy-demand becomes more
relevant as the ecological footprint of heating and cooling is noticeably smaller. Nevertheless,
fine-tuned LLMs also achieve the largest energy savings in this setup. Our experiments further
show that inference frequency can influence the ranking of software architectures.
Overall, there is no universally most energy-efficient FDD architecture. Rather, the superiority of an approach depends on the relationship between diagnostic performance, energyconsumption for training and inference, and the characteristics and operation of the monitored
building.
While our experiments allow us to draw some initial conclusions, the values collected from
prior literature should be interpreted as estimates rather than universally precise figures.
Future work should therefore evaluate the considered diagnosis architectures and approaches
based on a common benchmark to collect diagnostic-performance and energy values under
the same conditions. Furthermore, we want to apply our energy-consumption models to
real-world building data to assess the practical usefulness of our approach.
