Doing Less with More: A First-Principles Exploration of the Suitability of Agentic
Computing over Alternative Architectural Choices

Abstract
There is growing interest in automating business activities
with Agentic Artificial Intelligence (AI) due to latter’s seeming ease of use. Never has it been easier, or costlier, to do
less with more. However, little is known about when agents
are preferable to established alternatives such as local computation, Representational State Transfer (REST), the Simple
Object Access Protocol (SOAP), and the Model Context Protocol (MCP), particularly when development speed, performance, and operational cost are considered. We investigate
this question using a controlled mathematical task that compares seven methods on a benchmark of 1,000 arithmetic expressions where semantics of operator precedence has to be
preserved. We ran this setup across a native Function Calling baseline (Python library), REST, SOAP and MCP microservice architectures, direct responses from large language
models (LLMs) using a Groq cloud API and five locally deployed Ollama models, and agentic systems in which five
local LLMs invoke MCP tools. The deterministic methods
achieve 100% accuracy with negligible latency. Direct LLM
evaluation achieves at most 92.9% accuracy at over 22,000×
the latency of a native function call. With MCP tools, the best
agent reaches 99.9% accuracy, but incurs nearly 1,000,000×
the baseline latency. These results demonstrate that LLMs
approaches are poorly suited for exact arithmetic evaluation and motivate an informed architectural decision framework (ADF) for choosing an architecture based on accuracy
and throughput requirements. We recognize that an actual interaction with an agent may span both reasoning and linguistic tasks. Hence our recommendation is to explore multiple
architectural choices (based on task type) rather than onesize-fits-all solution (LLMs).

Introduction

The prevalent approach of building large software systems is to modularize code as functions and run them locally or remotely (Woodfield, Dunsmore, and Shen 1981).
The mechanisms to implement code modularity, however,
has evolved over time with technologies ranging from local function call, Representational State Transfer (REST),
Simple Object Access Protocol (SOAP), the Model Context Protocol (MCP) (Anthropic 2024) to calls now orchestrated and executed using Large Language Models (LLMs)
(Sarkar, Rama, and Kak 2007; Fielding 2000; Rodriguez
2008; Weerawarana et al. 2005; Halili, Ramadani et al. 2018;

Schick et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2022). Each generation of this
evolution has brought both opportunities and trade-offs with
respect to development speed, runtime performance, and operating cost. However, surprisingly, these trade-off aspects
are rarely analyzed and quantified under similar tasks and
conditions.
Even when a comparison may be done, care is needed
as simplistic comparisons risk conflating the computation’s
outcome (functional effectiveness like accuracy) itself with
the operational performance of mechanism used to invoke it.
For example, the choice between REST and SOAP or MCP
typically does not expose an underlying deterministic implementation, whereas an LLM asked to produce an answer replaces exact execution with probabilistic inference. Furthermore, an orthogonal performance issue with code modularity is when to call the functions leveraging potential parallelism leading to various strategies for composition, orchestration and execution (Agarwal et al. 2008).
In this paper, we do a comparison of architectural choices
for functional invocation in the context of arithmetic expression evaluation. Here, the semantics of operator precedence plays a direct role in accuracy of computation while
the choice of architecture impacts performance of (chosen
workflow’s) composition and execution. Illustrated in the
example below (bracket added for readability only):
Example Arithmetic Expression (from dataset)
Expression: (78.0 ∗ (186.0 − 172.0))
Expected Answer: 1092
Consider the semantics of the above situation, the operator precedence is crucial. If multiplication was done first, it
would result in an incorrect answer of 14336. Due to automatic verifiability of the answer and dependency on semantics of operator precedence (in this case, BODMAS - also
called PEMDAS rules), this task plays a useful control setting in isolating the reasoning problem. Our goal from this
work is not to argue that arithmetic expression evaluation as
a representation for reasoning tasks in general, but to use it
as a primer for a broader point i.e., every invocation mechanism has a place, there is no one-size fits all scenario (LLMs)
and choices should be made with consideration of tradeoffs
(described more in Section 5).

We compare seven representative strategies for implementing functionality (mathematical operations in our case)
spanning four architectural families: (i) direct function calling; (ii) service-oriented architectures (SOA) through REST,
SOAP and MCP; (iii) LLMs answering directly, hosted locally and remotely; and (iv) agentic LLMs that call the arithmetic operations as MCP tools. All strategies are evaluated
on the same set of 1,000 arithmetic expressions extracted
from SVAMP (Patel, Bhattamishra, and Goyal 2021), measuring exact-answer accuracy, execution time and processing cost (Section 3). Our results show that deterministic
methods (Function Calling, REST, SOAP and MCP) are
both the most accurate and the fastest. LLMs answering directly trail substantially on both measures, varying widely
by model and remaining insensitive to temperature. Giving
LLMs deterministic tools recovers accuracy only for models that reliably use them, at a further cost in latency. Direct
LLM calls were the easiest to assemble (in lines of code),
but this ease of integration does not guarantee the correctness that exact arithmetic demands (Section 4).
Key Contributions
• A controlled evaluation of leading alternative
choices for algebraic evaluation exploring accuracy and performance.
• An architectural decision framework for selecting an invocation strategy according to tradeoff
considerations.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the relevant computation and invocation
paradigms. Section 3 describes the experimental design,
benchmark construction, evaluated configurations, and metrics. Section 4 presents the empirical results. Section 5 – 6
discusses their architectural implications and the resulting
decision framework. Section 7 concludes the paper.

Background

Agentic AI is widely used to automate tasks that could
equally be handled by simpler, deterministic mechanisms
(Schick et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2022). Before going directly
into one-size-fits-all approach, it is worth asking to what extent can they actually cause an impact and whether local
existing mechanisms (local function call, REST endpoint,
SOAP service, MCP server) can serve as a better solution.
This section briefly reviews these mechanisms and the LLMbased alternatives (direct and agentic), the role of execution
order in composing them and why exact computation is not
guaranteed via LLM’s decoding process.
Invocation Paradigms. In-process function call transfer
control directly to locally available implementation with no
overheads. This makes them a natural baseline whenever an
implementation procedure is well known (e.g., PEMDAS
rules of arithmetic expressions).
REST and SOAP expose the same kind of computation across a process and network boundary over HTTP
(Fielding 2000; Rodriguez 2008; Weerawarana et al.

2005). REST uses lightweight, resource-oriented endpoints
whereas SOAP wraps calls in XML envelopes validated
against an explicit contract (WSDL). Because both approaches share the same transport, their overhead should be
comparable with SOAP’s envelope and schema validation
being a more structured definition (Castillo et al. 2011; Kumari and Rath 2015). SOAP also supports dynamic service
discovery, which we do not evaluate.
The Model Context Protocol (MCP) (Anthropic 2024)
standardizes how applications expose tools to clients. An
MCP server lists each tool with a name, a description and
a JSON schema for its arguments, and clients invoke tools
through JSON-RPC calls over HTTP. When called from deterministic code, MCP is simply another remote-invocation
protocol. Its tools-calling is optimized for LLM-based invocations where an LLM must discover and select tools at
runtime, a role similar to SOAP’s service discovery. In our
setup, REST, SOAP and MCP all decompose an expression
into a sequence of pairwise operations and dispatch them
one call at a time.
LLM-based computation replaces this pipeline with a single text generation. Instead of applying a fixed procedure,
the model receives a prompt that asks for the numerical answer alone, and correctness depends on its ability to reason
about operator precedence. Unlike an interpreter, this offers
no guarantee of correctness, since the answer is generated
token by token rather than derived (Boye and Moell 2025;
Shrestha, Kim, and Ross 2025). We refer to this direct answer generation as LLM-based computation, and to the setting in which the LLM instead selects and calls deterministic
tools (here, MCP tools) and only composes their results as
agentic computation (Schick et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2022).
Order-Preserving Computation. Arithmetic expressions make correctness easy to check but not trivial to preserve. A deterministic evaluator enforces the operator precedence (PEMDAS/BODMAS) through a parse tree. This requires the LLM to understand the semantics of the operator
precedence implicitly from the prompt text making it a nontrivial task.
Decoding and Failure Modes. Decoding strategy is assumed to be a highly influential variable in LLMs with Temperature (T ) used as the hyperparameter. Greedy decoding
(T = 0) always picks the highest-probability token whereas
most LLMs use a temperature T = 0.7 to introduce stochasticity (allowing for exploration and ideal for text generation)
(Renze 2024). Beyond wrong answers, an LLM can fail by
format (a response that is not a number alone), and an agent
can additionally fail by ignoring its tools, misusing them, or
never reaching a final answer.

Experimental Setup

In this section, we look into the setup used to compare the invocation strategies. We first describe the dataset and groundtruth extraction mechanism, then the implementation details
of each method, followed by the metrics used to evaluate accuracy, time and cost. Finally, we talk about the execution
conditions under which the methods were run to ensure a
fair comparison.

Dataset Preparation. The SVAMP benchmark dataset

Implementation Details
Each model receives an equation string and must return the
correct numerical result. We define seven methods as follows:
• Function Calling: Python’s built-in eval() function,
executing entirely in-process with no network overhead.
• REST: Equations are decomposed into sub-operations
dispatched as HTTP POST requests to a local Flask
server (localhost:5001).
• SOAP: We run both the functional services and discovery on the same server using XML-encoded SOAP
envelopes over HTTP (localhost:8000). Same decomposition strategy as REST is used.
• MCP: Same decomposition as REST, with each
sub-operation sent as an MCP tool call (JSONRPC over HTTP) to a server exposing add,

subtract, multiply and divide as tools
(localhost:8100).
• LLM-API: Each equation is submitted as a natural language prompt to the Groq cloud API
(openai/gpt-oss-20b, ∼20B parameters), instructed to return only a numerical answer.
• Ollama LLM: Identical prompting strategy is executed
over five locally hosted models llama3.2:1b,
llama3.2:3b, phi4-mini:3.8b,
granite4:3b, qwen2-math:1.5b
via
the
Ollama runtime (localhost:11434).
• LLM + MCP Tools: Local models receive the MCP
server’s four tools as function definitions and must
solve each equation by calling them. Each tool call is
executed on the MCP server and its result returned to
the model, until the model replies without a tool call
(its final answer) or six LLM turns pass. Each reply is
limited to 256 tokens.. Models: llama3.2:1b,
llama3.2:3b, phi4-mini:3.8b,
granite4:3b, qwen3.5:4b.
Qwen2-Math1.5B is excluded as it does not support tool calling,
instead substituted with Qwen3.5-4B.
All LLM calls use a sampling temperature of 0.7, a common temperature setting for NLU tasks. They were once
again run with temperature of 0 for greedy decoded response. Responses were parsed to only select numeric token;

Evaluation Principles
Each method was assessed on four dimensions: Accuracy,
Time, Cost and Development Effort. Each were measured as
follows:
• Accuracy: The percentage of exactly correct numerical
output from the invocations. For LLMs, this meant that
any non-numeric or explanatory text in the response (e.g.,
”The answer is...” ) was treated as a failure mode and
bucketed under format failure, resulting in the response
marked as incorrect answer.
• Time: Total wall clock time from the start of the program
to the end. This was then divided by number of samples
(1000) for each method to get avg. time per sample.
• Cost: The cost of the methods were calculated using
CPU-seconds for deterministic pipelines such as Function Calling, REST, SOAP and MCP (single delta using
psutil’s process-level counters). The local LLMs use
ollama-reported inference duration (prompt evaluation +
generation time), reflecting both CPU and GPU execution costs. These are the total amount of processor time
a process consumed during inference collectively called
Runtime Cost and measured in seconds. For cloud API,
we report token consumption instead. For LLM + MCP
Tools, this is summed over every LLM turn.
• Development Effort: We measure the effort taken to
develop software by lines-of-code. Although there are

many measures in software engineering literature for effort, code length is easy to calculate and relate to across
architecture styles.

All experiments were executed on the same host machine;
Cloud inference occurred remotely on Groq hardware (with
fixed temperature of 0.7), while local Ollama inferences ran
with two temperature settings (T = 0.7 and T = 0) to emphasize both general chatbot level behaviour and greedy decoding, which is often mistook as ”determinism” choice by
many (Atil et al. 2024). For LLM based invocations (API
and Ollama), a single prompt was used as a primer while
agentic (LLM + MCP Tools) used the second as shown below:

Accuracy. Function Calling, REST, SOAP and MCP
achieve 100% accuracy through deterministic computation. Every LLM-based method fell short of this ceiling, and by widely varying margins. Granite4-3B is
the strongest at 92.6–92.9%, followed by Phi4-mini-3.8B
(81.8–82.0%), Llama3.2-3B (73.1–74.3%) and Llama3.21B (42.5–44.6%). Qwen2-Math-1.5B scores 0.0% at both
temperatures despite being fine-tuned for math, which reflects a failure to follow the output instruction rather than
to compute (Table 2). The remote API (gpt-oss-20b) scores
15.5%.
Time. Wall-clock time spans six orders of magnitude,
so Figures 1 and 2 use a logarithmic scale. Function Calling completes all 1,000 equations in 6.9 ms (7 µs per equation). REST, MCP and SOAP are 326×, 326× and 347×

slower (2.25–2.40 s in total), mostly due to HTTP request
and response serialization. LLMs answering directly are another one to three orders of magnitude slower. Relative to
the baseline, Llama3.2-1B is 8,800–9,078× slower (61–

Qwen2-Math-1.5B is the slowest at 240,320–256,929×

Tool use adds up to another order of magnitude, since

a tool.

• For accuracy-critical and high-stake tasks. (e.g., financial calculations, safety-critical logic,), use deterministic methods (Function Calling, REST, SOAP or MCP).
These were the only methods to reach 100% accuracy.
• For cases with Strict protocol or formal contract requirements. (e.g., regulated, multi-party or auditable exchange), prefer SOAP. Its WSDL discloses the full action
space upfront and its fault format is fixed by the protocol, whereas REST leaves both to the implementation.
MCP sits between them, declaring every tool and its argument schema upfront but returning errors as free-text
messages, and is the natural choice when the callers are
LLM agents that discover tools at runtime. Performance
is comparable across all three (REST and MCP 326×,
SOAP 347× baseline), and code size grows with contract rigor (REST 136, MCP 165, SOAP 258 lines), so
the choice rests on how much formal disclosure the setting requires.
• For exploratory or prototyping work. REST and LLM
based settings work here. Direct LLM calls are the fastest
to build (40–44 lines) and handle open-ended tasks,
which makes them useful for exploration. But development speed does not buy output trust (max. accuracy at
92.9%). LLMs should not carry over into exploitation
(prototyping and system build). Using REST as the deterministic alternative would be a good start (due to less
requirements for WSDL definitions) once the task is understood would be a good start.
• Latency-critical tasks: avoid LLMs, which were
8,800× to 992,683× slower than the baseline. Among
deterministic services, REST & MCP are marginally
faster than SOAP (326× vs 347×), a negligible gap at
this scalex.
• Cost-constrained tasks: the right choice depends on
which kind of cost is being budgeted. Function Calling has effectively no marginal cost, and REST, SOAP
and MCP add a bounded, predictable infrastructure cost
(104–116× baseline processor time). A remote API’s

What to Consider in a Particular Situation

Based on the experiments, we found that deterministic methods (Function Calling, REST, SOAP) achieve high dominance in all three metrics of accuracy, performance and cost
optimization. LLMs answering directly are slower, less accurate and more compute-intensive regardless of model or
temperature (generalist vs greedy decoding). Given deterministic tools, the best LLM agents approach deterministic
accuracy, but only by delegating the arithmetic to those tools
and at five to six orders of magnitude more latency. The practical difference is that deterministic methods require the task
to already have an explicit implementation, as a library function or a deployed service, while LLMs can handle tasks
specified in natural language. As stated in Section 1, we
compare architectural choices for functional invocation on
arithmetic evaluation rather than on natural-language under-

Two further findings should inform this framework directly. First, domain specialization does not guarantee deployability: Qwen2-Math-1.5B, fine-tuned specifically for
mathematical reasoning, scored 0.0% in this benchmark
due to inability to adhere to strict number-only output instruction (Table 2), showcasing some pitfalls that can be
faced from fine-tuning. Model selection process should verify instruction-following behavior directly rather than inferring from domain benchmarks. Phi4-mini-3.8B supports
tool calling yet never called a tool, answering every equation
itself, and the Llama models called tools but then answered
in prose. Whether a model uses its tools must be measured,

not assumed from its documentation. Second, temperature
is not a meaningful lever for this class of task. Moving
from T = 0 to T = 0.7 changed accuracy by at most 2.1
percentage points for direct invocation and 3.7 with tools.
While the promise of ’determinism’ is sought by many with
T = 0, all it does is Greedy decoding (always picks the single token with the highest predicted probability) which still
is probabilistic rather than deterministic (Atil et al. 2024).
Architectural Decision Framework (summary)
1. Is the procedure known and exact? Use a deterministic method: Function Calling within a process, REST for a lightweight service, SOAP when
a formal contract is required, and MCP when LLM
agents will call the service.
2. Is the procedure unknown and some error acceptable? A direct LLM call is the fastest to build,
but it should be replaced by a deterministic implementation once the task is understood. Where an
LLM remains in the loop, it should call deterministic tools rather than compute the answer itself.

6

Discussion

We set out to show that established architectural alternatives remain viable and should be chosen by task type rather
than by defaulting to a single approach. We quantified this
via the proposal of the Architectural Decision Framework
(Section 5). The results support this premise. Deterministic methods reach 100% accuracy at negligible cost, while
every LLM answering directly falls short by wide and inconsistent margins at orders of magnitude more latency and
compute, regardless of model, hosting or temperature. This
points to a mismatch between autoregressive generation and
exact computation, since the model must preserve operator
precedence implicitly rather than by construction. This gap
holds regardless of the model, host environment, or decoding temperature. LLM agents narrow the accuracy gap only
after deterministic tool allocation but at cost of latency that
is orders of magnitude higher than baseline. The overhead
of REST, SOAP and MCP is acceptable for low-frequency
services, although high-volume use would call for batching
or persistent connections.
Failure composition. Table 2 separates wrong answers
from format failures. Answering directly, the models mostly
follow the output instruction but miscalculate. Wrong answers account for 540 of 554 failures for Llama3.2-1B at
T = 0, 249 of 257 for Llama3.2-3B, 177 of 182 for Phi4mini-3.8B and 66 of 71 for Granite4-3B. Qwen2-Math-1.5B
is the opposite extreme, with all 1,000 responses being format failures at both temperatures. Fine-tuning for math did
not help it follow an instruction that general-purpose models of similar size handled. With tools, the failures shift from
computation to interaction. The Llama models mostly called
tools and then answered in prose (641–755 format failures).
Phi4-mini-3.8B never used its tools, so its failures remain
wrong answers (318–347).
Temperature invariance. At both greedy decoding (T =

0) and commond default (T = 0.7)changed accuracy by
at most 2.1 points when answering directly (Llama3.2-1B,
44.6% to 42.5%) and 3.7 points with tools (Figure 3b),
with little effect on time or cost. Greedy decoding removes
sampling variation but not model error, so T = 0 makes
an LLM’s output more repeatable, not more correct. This
agrees with prior findings that “deterministic” settings do
not make LLMs deterministic (Atil et al. 2024).
These findings should be read within the scope of what we
tested and the assumptions made in the process. Our benchmark is limited to algebraic expressions with a fixed prompt
template, chosen for the ease of verifying answers and the
need for structured understanding of operator precedence semantics, isolating the reasoning problem from more popular
NLU-based math reasoning benchmarks (Hendrycks et al.
2021; Cobbe et al. 2021). We also evaluated only two temperature settings per model. Extending this comparison to
tasks that mix computation with language understanding, or
to cases with more complex semantics is a natural next step.
Related Work. Our work focuses on the appropriateness
of architecture choices (such as REST, SOAP, MCP, LLMs)
to get the same functionality. Others have started looking
at appropriateness of LLMs for different settings. (Assadi,
Muennighoff, and Lee 2026), for example, compare embedding models against LLMs on text-based tasks. Their study
finds near-identical aggregate quality (77.6 vs 77.2) but at
three orders of magnitude higher inference cost in dollars
for the best LLM ($154 vs $0.11, a 1,431-fold difference).
Embedding pipelines remain to stand as more efficient setting for classification, similarity and clustering based tasks.
Together, these results indicate that the right architecture
is a decision made under acceptable tradeoffs between accuracy, developer convenience, protocols adherence, performance and cost, and more (as mentioned in 5). We view this
as the central implication of our arithmetic case study for the
broader adoption of agentic systems in business automation.

7

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose an architectural decision framework (ADF) for selecting an invocation mechanism under
trade-offs between accuracy, developer convenience, protocol adherence, performance and cost, grounded in a comparison of seven strategies on 1,000 arithmetic expressions: native function calls, REST, SOAP, MCP, LLMs answering directly (cloud and local), and LLM agents calling MCP tools.
Deterministic methods scored 100% accuracy at negligible
cost, while LLMs answering directly reached at most 92.9%
at orders of magnitude more time and compute, regardless
of model or temperature. LLM agents reached 99.9% only
by delegating the arithmetic to deterministic tools, at nearly
1,000,000× the latency of a function call, and weaker models performed worse with tools than without. Direct LLM
calls were the easiest to integrate with least lines of code,
but this ease did not translate into correctness, underscoring
that no single axis of convenience, accuracy, or cost is sufficient on its own to justify an invocation choice. Extending
the framework beyond this single task class, to richer semantics and multi-step workflows, is the natural next step.
