HARL-A: An Extensible Benchmark Framework for Heterogeneous
Multi-Agent Adversarial Reinforcement Learning in IsaacLab

arXiv:2510.01264v2 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026

Isaac Peterson*1

Christopher Allred*12

Abstract— Progress in adversarial multi-agent reinforcement
learning (Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)) for
robotics has been hampered by a lack of shared, extensible
infrastructure that supports heterogeneous agent morphologies
in high-fidelity physics simulation. Existing frameworks either
focus on cooperative tasks, rely on simplified physics engines,
or provide isolated implementations that are difficult to extend.
We present HARL-A, an open-source, actively maintained
framework built on IsaacLab that enables scalable training and
benchmarking of adversarial policies across morphologically
diverse robot teams with any number of teams and any mix
of robot morphologies per team. HARL-A extends the HARL
algorithm library and IsaacLab with adversarial multi-agent
support and contributes three components: (1) a modular
software architecture that reduces the engineering overhead
of defining new heterogeneous adversarial environments, (2) a
suite of three benchmark environments—Sumo, Soccer, and 3D
Galaga—spanning contact-rich pushing, ball-skill competition,
and pursuit/evasion, (3) over ten pretrained policies spanning
homogeneous and heterogeneous team configurations, released
publicly on Hugging Face to enable immediate exploration of
adversarial learning dynamics without retraining from scratch.
We demonstrate the framework across multiple competitive
scenarios, showing that it reliably produces learned adversarial
policies and emergent role specialization. All code environments,
trained policies, and documentation are openly available at
https://github.com/DIRECTLab/IsaacLab-HARL.

I. I NTRODUCTION
The development of robust multi-agent policies for competitive robotics applications—pursuit-evasion, adversarial
manipulation, security—requires training infrastructure that
combines three properties rarely found together: high-fidelity
contact physics, support for heterogeneous robot morphologies, and scalable parallelized simulation that can train adversarially. Without the first, learned policies fail to transfer to
real mechanical systems. Without the second, benchmarks
cannot capture the strategic asymmetries that make realworld competition interesting. Without the third, the sample
complexity of adversarial training makes meaningful experiments impractical.
Reinforcement learning has matured rapidly for singlerobot tasks [1], [2], producing capable locomotion [3], [4]
and dexterous manipulation policies [5]. GPU-accelerated
simulators such as IsaacLab [6] have made large-scale training feasible, and recent work has extended this to cooperative
heterogeneous MARL [7]. However, adversarial settings have
received comparatively little attention in high-fidelity simulation. Prior adversarial work has largely relied on simplified

Reinforcement Learning – Adversarial), a framework that

programming interface (API) that cleanly separates observations, actions, and rewards by team. HARL-A implements
both, and wraps them in a modular environment interface that
allows new scenarios to be defined with minimal boilerplate.

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Adversarial Multi-Agent Learning
Early work demonstrated that self-play in physics-based
environments could produce emergent competitive behaviors
[8], and subsequent work showed that adversarial training
could exploit brittle policies that are undetectable under
standard evaluation [12]. Multi-agent extensions introduced
multi-agent deep deterministic policy gradient (MADDPG)
[16] and showed that large-scale population-based training
could reach human-level performance in complex games
[17], [18]. The MuJoCo hide-and-seek environment [9]
demonstrated rich emergent tool use from repeated self-play.
More recent work combined imitation learning, MARL, and
population training to produce competitive humanoid soccer
[15].
B. Benchmarks and Frameworks
The MARL community has produced several benchmark
frameworks, including VMAS [19], SMACv2 [19], and
Multi-Agent Particle Environments [13]. However, these rely

on simplified or 2D physics and do not support morphologically heterogeneous agents. In the high-fidelity space,
MARLaDona [10] demonstrated competitive robot soccer in
IsaacLab, but focuses on homogeneous teams and does not
provide an extensible framework for new environments or
morphologies. The cooperative counterpart to our work [7]
introduced heterogeneous multi-agent training in IsaacLab,
but was limited to cooperative tasks with a shared critic.
HARL-A extends this line of work to adversarial settings
and provides the benchmark infrastructure the community
currently lacks, as summarized in Table I.
C. Curriculum Learning for Complex Tasks
Training stability in competitive settings is a wellrecognized challenge [8], [20]. Procedural content generation
methods, such as PAIRED [21], adaptively modulate task difficulty, while techniques like domain randomization and progressive task decomposition [20] have demonstrated strong
performance in locomotion tasks. In this work, we extend the
zero-buffer curriculum framework proposed in [22], which
preserves a consistent observation space across curriculum
stages and facilitates smooth policy transfer between them.
III. F RAMEWORK D ESIGN
HARL-A is built on two existing systems: IsaacLab [6],
which provides GPU-accelerated physics simulation, and the
HARL library [11], which provides heterogeneous actorcritic algorithms. Our contribution is the adversarial extension connecting them, comprising three design components
described below.
A. Team-Indexed Environment API
HARL-A exposes a simple dictionary in the IsaacLab
configuration class that allows for the configuration of the
teams for adversarial training.
teams = {
"team_0": ["anymal_0", "anymal_1", ],
"team_1": ["leatherback_0", "leatherback_1"
...
"team_N": ["robot_A_N", ... "robot_N_N"
}

This central interface change in HARL-A is a shift from
agent-indexed to team-indexed environment interaction. In
cooperative MARL frameworks, observations, actions, and
rewards are typically organized as flat per-agent arrays. In
adversarial settings, this is insufficient: teams require isolated
reward signals and critics, and the environment must expose

This modular abstraction enforces a clear separation between task specification and learning infrastructure, reducing coupling between environment logic and optimization
components. As a result, modifications to morphology or
task dynamics remain localized to a small portion of the
codebase, lowering the risk of unintended side effects in
the training pipeline. By reusing the same parallelization
backend, logging utilities, and curriculum mechanisms across
tasks, the framework minimizes duplicated implementation
effort and improves experimental reproducibility. In practice,
this substantially reduces development overhead and iteration
time, enabling rapid prototyping of new competitive scenarios while maintaining a consistent evaluation and training
protocol.
B. Team-Specific Critics
A cooperative framework’s shared critic is insufficient for
adversarial settings. When team rewards are zero-sum (i.e.,

HARL-A addresses this by assigning each team its own

C. Training Regime Support
HARL-A supports three adversarial training regimes out
of the box, selectable via configuration:
• Alternating (“leapfrog”): One team’s actor is frozen
while the other updates for a fixed number of timesteps,
then roles switch. This mirrors the regime used in [23]
and generally produces stable learning curves.
• Simultaneous: Both teams update concurrently. While
less stable theoretically, we find that it also produces
meaningful policies in practice, suggesting that the
framework is robust to this choice.
• Fixed-opponent: One team is held at a fixed checkpoint
while the other trains. Useful for measuring improvement against a defined baseline.
All three regimes use the same environment and critic
infrastructure, making it straightforward to compare them for
a given task.

B. Emergent Adversarial Behaviors
Beyond win rates, training produced qualitatively distinct
emergent behaviors across morphologies. In the heterogeneous Sumo environment, Leatherback rovers learned to target the legs of opposing Anymal robots—exploiting their low
ground clearance—while Anymal robots developed dragging
maneuvers that used their limbs to pull rovers outside the
arena boundary (Figure 7). Critically, these complementary
roles emerged without any explicit role assignment mechanism: the only signal was the team reward in Equation 1. This
suggests that morphological asymmetry alone is sufficient to
induce division of labor in adversarial training, a finding of
independent interest for multi-agent learning research.
These behaviors parallel emergent phenomena observed
in large-scale self-play systems [9], but arise here in a
high-fidelity contact-rich setting where physical constraints
actively shape which strategies are viable.

C. 3D Galaga
The 3D Galaga environment demonstrates a third task
category: pursuit and interception in three-dimensional space.
A team of MiniTanks attempts to intercept a drone navigating
to waypoints using arm-aligned laser-tag rays. The drone
receives a reward for reaching within 0.2 meters of its target
waypoint; each tank receives a reward for pinning the drone
within a 0.02-radian cone off its arm.
In this environment the drone enters adversarial training with an existing waypoint-navigation policy, and the
tanks are initialized with pointing policies rather than being
trained from scratch. This illustrates one of HARL-A’s design
goals—supporting the composition of existing robot skills
into new adversarial tasks with minimal engineering overhead. It also represents a qualitatively different competitive
structure from Sumo and Soccer, as the asymmetry between
offense (evasion) and defense (interception) is built into
the task definition rather than emerging from morphological
differences.

VII. E XTENDING THE F RAMEWORK
A core design goal of HARL-A is to impose as few
constraints as possible on team structure, morphology, and
algorithmic choice. Here we describe the extensibility concretely across four dimensions.
Any number of teams. The team-indexed API places no
upper bound on the number of competing teams. The threeteam configuration shown in Figure 1 was implemented
without any changes to the framework core—only the environment configuration file required modification. N -team
competition, free-for-all scenarios, and asymmetric team
sizes (e.g., 2-v-1-v-3) are all supported. It is important to
note, as highlighted in figure 8, that the speed of the training
is heavily dependent on the number of articulations in the
scene.
Any morphology mix. IsaacLab’s articulation system handles physics simulation for any robot described by a USD
asset file. To add a new robot to an existing environment, a
user specifies the asset path and defines the observation and
action dimensions in the team configuration. The framework
automatically constructs the corresponding actor network
with the correct input/output dimensions, and the teamspecific critic is sized accordingly. The 16 environments
in Table II span quadrupeds, wheeled rovers, humanoids,

drones, and tanks—all trained within the same framework
without modifications to the core codebase.
New environments with minimal boilerplate. Isaaclab
provides most of the scaffolding for implementing new environments and we provide our environments as open source
resources for further adversarial environment development.
Algorithm integration. The current release ships with
HAPPO as the primary training algorithm. The team-indexed
critic interface is compatible with any CTDE algorithm that
accepts per-team value functions, meaning other algorithms
from the HARL library (HATRPO, HAATRPO) and external
implementations (value-decomposition [24], graph-attention
critics [25]) can be tested against HARL-A benchmark
environments by implementing a thin adapter layer.
VIII. L IMITATIONS AND F UTURE W ORK
HARL-A’s current release has several limitations that represent directions for future development. The framework currently integrates only HAPPO; broader algorithm coverage—
including off-policy methods [23] and value-decomposition
approaches [24]—would make it more useful as an algorithm
benchmark. Evaluation methodology is currently limited to
win rate against a fixed baseline; richer metrics such as exploitability, cross-play performance, and robustness to novel
opponents would provide a more comprehensive picture of
policy quality. The Soccer and 3D Galaga environments are
at earlier stages of development than Sumo, and their benchmark status will strengthen with additional trained variants
and multi-algorithm comparisons. Finally, the framework
currently operates in simulation only; bridging to sim-toreal transfer for competitive tasks is an open challenge with
significant practical importance.
IX. C ONCLUSION
We presented HARL-A, an open-source, actively maintained framework for scalable heterogeneous adversarial reinforcement learning in IsaacLab. By extending the HARL

algorithm library with team-isolated critics and a teamindexed environment API, HARL-A supports competitive
training across any number of teams with any mix of robot
morphologies—with no changes to the framework core required as team structure or morphology changes. The accompanying benchmark suite—Sumo, Soccer, and 3D Galaga—
provides standardized environments spanning contact-rich
pushing, ball-skill competition, and pursuit/evasion, and over
ten pretrained policies on Hugging Face allow researchers
to immediately explore adversarial dynamics, run cross-play
evaluations, and use trained agents as opponents without
retraining from scratch.
Our experiments confirm that the framework reliably produces learned adversarial policies across all three task types,
and that morphological asymmetry alone induces emergent
role specialization without any explicit role mechanism. GPU
parallelization yields throughput sufficient to train competitive policies at 1010 -step scale on commodity hardware.
We hope HARL-A serves as both a practical starting point
and a community benchmark for adversarial MARL research
in physically grounded settings, and that the combination of
flexible infrastructure, open pretrained policies, and active
maintenance lowers the barrier to entry for the broader
robotics and multi-agent learning community.
