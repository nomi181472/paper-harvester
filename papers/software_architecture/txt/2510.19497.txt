A BSTRACT
Modeling realistic human behaviour to understand people’s mode choices in order to propose
personalised mobility solutions remains challenging. This paper presents an architecture for modeling
realistic human mobility behavior in complex multimodal transport systems, demonstrated through
a case study in Toulouse, France. We apply Large Language Models (LLMs) within an agentbased simulation to capture decision-making in a real urban setting. The framework integrates the
GAMA simulation platform with an LLM-based generative agent, along with General Transit Feed
Specification (GTFS) data for public transport, and OpenTripPlanner for multimodal routing. GAMA
platform models the interactive transport environment, providing visualization and dynamic agent
interactions while eliminating the need to construct the simulation environment from scratch. This
design enables a stronger focus on developing generative agents and evaluating their performance in
transport decision-making processes. Over a simulated month, results show that agents not only make
context-aware transport decisions but also form habits over time. We conclude that combining LLMs
with agent-based simulation offers a promising direction for advancing intelligent transportation
systems and personalised multimodal mobility solutions. We also discuss some limitations of this
approach and outline future work on scaling to larger regions, integrating real-time data, and refining
memory models.

Introduction

Understanding human mobility behavior in multimodal transport systems is essential for advancing traffic planning and
the design of efficient transport networks. The challenge lies not only in representing travel patterns but also in building
a simulation framework that supports the study of individual behavior, the testing of behavioral hypotheses, and the
development of improved multimodal transport and urban traffic planning strategies.
The emergence of personalized multimodal transport systems reflects a broader shift toward user-centric mobility,
where public transit, walking, cycling, and shared services are integrated to provide journeys tailored to individual
preferences [Smith et al., 1995, W Axhausen et al., 2016]. Capturing such systems in simulation, however, is particularly
challenging because mobility choices are shaped by diverse socioeconomic profiles, accessibility constraints, and
personal experiences [Grignard et al., 2018]. As highlighted by [Oberoi, 2024], personalization in Mobility-as-a-Service
requires addressing the heterogeneity of users—including their preferences, contexts, and accessibility needs—which
remains a significant challenge for developing realistic transport models. Modeling, therefore, requires both flexibility
and realism to capture heterogeneous and adaptive travel decisions across populations.

Existing transport simulations often struggle with this complexity and with the availability of data needed to calibrate
models. In particular, detailed trajectory and itinerary datasets—which are critical for capturing the nuances of
multimodal travel behavior—are extremely scarce [Fourez et al., 2025]. Such datasets exist for a limited number of
major metropolitan areas, for example, Tokyo, Japan, but are largely unavailable elsewhere [Feng et al., 2024]. This
dependence on scarce calibration data restricts the applicability of existing models in smaller or less data-rich regions.
Moreover, integrating multiple external data sources requires extensive rule engineering, producing brittle models that
are difficult to scale and prone to failure in unforeseen conditions.
Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and generative AI offer a promising alternative [Park et al.,
2023]. Generative agents can perceive their environment, process contextual information, learn from experience, and
adapt their behavior over time. Beyond transport applications, LLM-based agents have already demonstrated their
potential in diverse domains. For example, they have been applied to tasks such as software testing, advertising analysis,
and brainstorming in the Tiny Troupe framework [Salem et al., 2025], as well as automating research through Agent
Laboratory [Schmidgall et al., 2025]. These approaches create ensembles of agents with different roles, capabilities,
and personas that collaborate to achieve common goals. Additionally, other studies have evaluated the ability of LLM
agents to proxy human emotions by testing their performance on psychological tasks grounded in the OCEAN Big Five
personality traits [Serapio-García et al., 2025]. Results show that these agents can simulate emotional intelligence and
empathy, which highlights their potential to proxy human traffic decisions by considering context, preferences, and
even inheriting personal traits. At the same time, the GAMA platform provides a powerful environment for spatially
explicit agent-based modeling, with strong support for GIS integration and real-world datasets such as GTFS2 and
OpenStreetMap3 [Taillandier et al., 2019].
In this work, we build on these developments with two main objectives: (i) to integrate generative agents with the
GAMA4 platform in order to create a scalable, data-driven simulation environment, and (ii) to develop generative
agents that act as proxies for human decision-making in multimodal transport systems. Results demonstrate that
generative agents can not only adapt their travel choices but also learn and form habits over time, offering a more
realistic representation of human mobility behavior. Nevertheless, the well-known limitations of LLMs—such as
inconsistency, hallucination, grounding in real-world data, and scalability—remain significant challenges [Taillandier
et al., 2025]. Addressing these issues constitutes an important direction for our future research.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on the integration of LLM-based
generative agents into urban mobility simulation. Section 3 presents the proposed methodology, including the software
architecture, generative agent components, and GAMA platform integration. Section 4 describes the experimental
setup and presents the results. Finally, Sections 5 and 6 discuss the contributions, limitations, and directions for future
research.

Related Work

2.1

The Conceptual Framework

The notion of generative agents has gained traction from the influential work of [Park et al., 2023], which describes a
core architecture for artificial agents endowed with memory, planning, observation, and reflection modules. In this
architecture, agents record experiences in a memory stream, manage and summarize memories through reflection,
and reference these memories to guide future behavior via planning and reasoning. These core modules—perception,
memory (both short-term and long-term), planning, and reflection—form the backbone of autonomous, context-aware
agent behavior. In this work, generative agents leverage LLMs to generate behavior and interact with one another in
a human-like manner. Building on this foundation, subsequent studies have extended the generative agent paradigm
to various domains. For instance, Voyager is an agent that continuously explores the world, learns, and makes novel
discoveries without human intervention, demonstrated within the Minecraft environment [Wang et al., 2023]. Similarly,
TinyTroupe and XAgent enable the simulation of groups of people collaborating to achieve shared goals [Salem et al.,
2025, Team, 2023].

Extending this architecture into the domain of human mobility, [Liu et al., 2025a] propose a conceptual framework
tailored to transportation systems, grounded in the generative agent paradigm. Their framework augments traditional
modules with transport-specific components, including general planning, travel planning, and dual memory layers
(long-term and short-term memory). It also incorporates personal identity and traits as inputs, allowing agents’ decisions
to be influenced by their unique socio-demographic and behavioral profiles. In this setup, LLMs are prompted with
identity- and trait-based inputs, enabling them to act as proxies for human decision-makers. The models generate
textual outputs that represent decisions, which are then executed by agents in the simulation environment. In their
demonstration on a simplified transport network divided into four zones, agents use multi-layer travel planning and
experiential learning to iteratively optimize travel routes, which helps alleviate traffic congestion in that small network.
This framework effectively adapts the generative agent architecture to mobility contexts, enabling agents to reason
over high-level intentions (general plan) and concrete route choices (travel plan), while learning from experience and
adjusting behavior over time. Provides a conceptual baseline for integrating generative agents into transport modeling
by embedding agent cognition (memory, planning, trait-driven decisions) within a networked mobility environment.
2.2

City-scale Mobility Simulation

One promising direction in mobility modeling is using generative or agentic frameworks to predict or simulate city-scale
movement. A recent effort, called AgentMove [Feng et al., 2024] combines LLM reasoning with spatial modeling
to forecast agents’ next destinations. This work casts the city as a multi-layer graph to encode spatial context and
decomposes the prediction task into modules for spatio-temporal memory, world knowledge generation, and collective
pattern extraction. While this approach achieves strong predictive accuracy on benchmark datasets, it relies on rich
trajectory and survey data that are rarely available in most cities, which limits its general applicability.
A more traditional but practical approach is demonstrated by Ile-de-France’s synthetic itinerary generation (via the
EQASIM framework) [Hörl and Balac, 2021]. This method uses population data, origin-destination matrices, and
statistical matching techniques to generate daily itineraries for synthetic agents. Although less expressive in modeling
individual cognition, it is grounded in widely available demographic and commuter data and can be applied even in
regions lacking fine-grained trajectory logs.
At a larger scale, the AgentSociety framework is designed to simulate 10,000 agents in an urban environment [Piao
et al., 2025]. This work primarily focuses on optimizing the underlying architecture to support large-scale populations,
enabling agents to form daily routines, interact, and influence one another in socially meaningful ways. However, it
does not go into the complex of transport systems.
Another recent contribution is GATSim[Liu et al., 2025b] which introduces a generative-agent architecture over a
simplified transport network consisting of 16 fully connected nodes in a single-modal setting. Agents in GATSim are
equipped with memory hierarchies, planning and reflection mechanisms, and learning capabilities, which allow them
to generate realistic travel behaviors. The framework demonstrates that emergent traffic patterns align with expected
macroscopic dynamics (such as peak spreading), and that agent trajectories reproduce aspects of human-like decisionmaking. However, its reliance on a small, simplified network and single-mode transport limits its representativeness for
complex urban environments.
Taken together, these works map out a design space between pure prediction (AgentMove), synthetic generation
(Ile-de-France), and full simulation (GATSim, AgentSociety). They illustrate both the potential and the barriers of
applying the human traveling behavior simulation at city scale. In the next section, we will position our approach in this
space, aiming to bridge toward multimodal, large-scale transport simulation with generative agents.
2.3

Research Gap and Opportunities

Despite recent advances, current generative-agent approaches remain limited to simplified or small-scale transport
networks, restricting their relevance for real multimodal systems. To be practically useful, agents must operate in
complex environments where multiple modes and dense urban structures interact. For example, the public transport
system of Toulouse— combining metro, tram, bus, and cable car — illustrates such a setting, where realistic decisionmaking requires agents that adapt travel choices to context and experience (Figure 1); the transport network is far
more complex than the 16 fully connected nodes used in GATSim, as it features a dense structure with numerous stops
and routes, integrates multiple modes, and can be represented as a large multi-layer graph, where each stop and route
corresponds to a node and an edge, respectively.
Another gap lies in the construction of simulation environments. Many studies develop platforms from scratch, which
demands significant coding effort and hinders scalability. Integrating generative agents with the established GAMA
platform provides a more efficient path, allowing researchers to focus on agent performance while leveraging GAMA’s

Methodology

3.1

Overall Architecture

The proposed architecture builds upon the conceptual framework introduced by [Liu et al., 2025a]. Its core components
are: (i) a multimodal transport system modeled in the GAMA Platform, (ii) Generative Agents, and (iii) a Data Exchange
Integration layer that connects these two modules. Through this integration layer, the Generative Agents control and
coordinate the behavior of agents within the GAMA simulation, while continuously adapting their future decisions
according to evolving contexts and feedback. An overview of the architecture is illustrated in Figure 2.
The architecture relies on a diverse set of input data. At the individual level, each agent is initialized with a personal
identity and traits as well as a set of daily activities represented as a sequence of actions that specify the time, location,
and purpose (e.g., attending school, commuting to work, engaging in leisure, or returning home). At the system level,
the architecture integrates geographic data and GTFS data, the latter providing the schedules of the city’s public
transport system.
Decision-making is supported by the Generative Agents, which query LLM models to generate context-aware plans
and adapt them to real-time conditions. A dedicated Daily Activity Planning module (i) translates each agent’s activity
sequence into a structured schedule, (ii) triggers activities at their designated times, and (iii) adjusts departure times to
ensure timely arrivals. For example, if an agent departs for work at 8:00 AM on the first day but arrives 20 minutes
late, it is likely to adjust its departure time to 7:40 AM on the following day. The Daily Activity Planning module then
invokes the Travel Planning module, which decomposes each activity transition into a detailed journey, specifying travel
legs, selecting mobility modes, and determining the routes to be followed. Within this setup, perception data—including
travel outcomes such as delays, waiting times, in-vehicle travel times, and arrival deviations—are transmitted to the

In this architecture, each agent in the GAMA platform simulation corresponds to a generative agent. We extend the
original framework of [Liu et al., 2025a] by incorporating an additional OpenTripPlanner module. The module provides
access to detailed transit data and multimodal routing information, enabling Generative Agents to evaluate alternative
journeys and adapt their travel plans dynamically. More importantly, it allows agents to operate within the complex
topology of transport networks, represented as large multi-layer graphs with numerous stops, routes, and modes of
transport. By embedding this capability into the architecture, agents are able to replicate human decision-making more
realistically: consulting route information, responding to disruptions, and selecting journeys that best align with their
preferences and context.
To summarize, Generative Agents, supported by various modules and data sources, leverage LLMs to emulate human
behavior based on personal identity and traits. With the integration of OpenTripPlanner as a travel assistant, agents
can navigate the simulation environment and complete their daily activities without being constrained by the inherent
complexity of transport networks. Finally, the GAMA platform provides the simulation environment in which agents
learn, interact, and act across diverse experimental scenarios.

3.4

Generative Agent Memory

The memory system is central to the agent’s ability to learn from experience and adapt behavior over time. This
module takes a crucial role in storing, retrieving, and managing knowledge at both the naive and the reflective levels,
joining both reflection and decision-making phases. How the agent performs depends heavily on the design of this
memory system. Like other related works, we implement a dual-layer memory architecture consisting of short-term and
long-term memory components.
3.4.1

Short-term Memory

The short-term memory is designed to store recent experiences and observations in a relatively simple form. It captures
the agent’s immediate context and the events occurring in its surroundings. For example, in the current implementation,
the short-term memory includes details of recent trips, such as the delays, waiting times, travel durations, and arrival
late time. In future work, this memory could be extended to include additional contextual factors—such as weather
conditions, route disruptions, or traffic incidents—to support experimentation under varied scenarios. Short-term
memory is volatile and primarily serves as input to the reflection process at the end of each simulated day. Structurally,
it is organized as a chronological stream of events, allowing agents to review and reason over daily experiences in
sequence.
3.4.2

Long-term Memory

The long-term memory is responsible for storing knowledge consolidated from the short-term memory over time. This
includes successful strategies, learned preferences, and other insights that inform future decision-making. Unlike
short-term memory, it is less volatile and designed to retain information across multiple simulation runs, enabling the
agent to develop a richer understanding of its environment and progressively improve performance.
To maximize the utility of information derived from short-term memory, we define two distinct types of long-term
memory: (i) concepts, which represent specific events or facts independent of personal traits or identity, and (ii)
reflections, which summarize broader insights and lessons learned, often incorporating the agent’s traits and identity.
Concepts serve as concise representations of short-term memory, optimizing token usage in large language model
prompts, while reflections capture higher-level abstractions and patterns that can guide future behavior in alignment
with the agent’s characteristics.
For example, a concept might be “Bus 67 tends to be slow during the evening commute”. A reflection, however, could
vary depending on the agent’s traits: one individual might prefer this bus because of its predictability, while another with
high conscientiousness might avoid it due to its slowness, even though both agents have learned the same underlying
concept.
Another type of long-term memory involves abstracting the reflections into higher-level generalizations, which are
discussed further in Section 4.

Memory Retrieval

The memory retrieval process (Figure 4) is crucial for the agent’s decision-making capabilities. To make it realistic, we
aim to mimic the human cognitive process of recalling relevant information from memory when faced with a decision.

For contextual relevance retrieval, we use Chroma6 - a vector database to store long-term memories as high-dimensional

based on the current context and searches the vector database for the most similar entries. After that, the retrieved
memories are ranked based on time decay and seasonal relevance criteria to ensure that the most pertinent information
is prioritized.

experiences are weighted more heavily than older ones.
3.5

GAMA Platform Integration

The integration of LLM–based Generative Agents with the GAMA simulation environment is achieved through
a combination of synchronous and asynchronous communication mechanisms, specifically HTTP and WebSocket
protocols. The details of this integration are shown in Figure 5. Since GAMA platform has built-in support for these
protocols, data can be exchanged between the two systems in real time. Within GAMA, a dedicated model, referred to
as the LLM Agent Interface, serves as the communication bridge to the Generative Agent Server, which is implemented
as a Python-based HTTP and WebSocket server.
Building on this integration layer, the Generative Agent (GA) itself is implemented outside of the GAMA simulation
platform in order to take advantage of the rich Python ecosystem. While GAMA provides strong capabilities for
modeling and simulating spatial environments, its native language (GAML) does not offer support for essential libraries
commonly used in large language model development, such as retrieval-augmented generation systems, vector databases,
and advanced machine learning frameworks.
By hosting the Generative Agent as a separate Python-based server application, we encapsulate all its functionalities and
expose them through a set of application programming interfaces for communication with the GAMA environment. This
design ensures that the simulation benefits from GAMA’s strengths in spatial modeling while still enabling advanced
reasoning, memory management, and decision-making capabilities powered by the Python ecosystem. The integration
process involves several key steps:

• Action Sending: The first step is to send the actions - the sequence of transfer and transit actions (see
Section 3.2) defined in the LLM Generative Agents to the GAMA simulation. Whenever an inhabitant agent
in GAMA receives a new action command from its corresponding LLM GA, it executes the action within the
simulation environment.
• State Sharing: Because the agents in GAMA have a one-to-one mapping with their corresponding agents
in the LLM GAs, the state of each agent in GAMA is shared with its counterpart in the GAs. This allows
the GAs to have a consistent view of the simulation state and make informed decisions based on the current
context. The shared state includes (i) the simulation timestamp and (ii) the spatial location of each agent. A
critical aspect of this process is timestamp synchronization: because it takes time for the GAs to process the
information and respond to the simulation, so we need to delay GAMA to force it to wait.
• Feedback Loop: A feedback loop is established between the GAs and the GAMA simulation, allowing the
GAs to continuously receive updates on the outcomes of their actions. This feedback is crucial for the GAs to
learn and improve their decision-making over time. Specifically, the feedback corresponds to the observations
described in Section 3.4.1.
State Sharing step is performed in a synchronous manner, because the GAs need to have the most up-to-date state of the
agents in GAMA before making any decisions. On the other hand, the Action Sending and Feedback Loop steps are
handled asynchronously, as the GAs can process actions and feedback independently of the simulation’s main loop.
Therefore, State Sharing step is implemented using HTTP protocol, while Action Sending and Feedback Loop are
implemented using WebSocket protocol to improve the speed of the simulation.
3.6

Evolving Learning and Reflection

The reasoning flow of the Generative Agent, illustrated in Figure 6, operates through two interconnected phases that
enable agents to adapt their travel behavior over time:
• Reflection phase — At the end of each simulated day, the agent reviews perception data (such as delays,
waiting times, and travel outcomes) stored in short-term memory and integrates this information into longterm memory. This process consolidates daily experiences into structured knowledge that can be reused in
subsequent decisions.
• Decision-making phase — At the beginning of the next simulated day, the agent retrieves relevant knowledge
from long-term memory to inform travel planning. The plan is shaped by daily activities, personal identity,
individual traits, and accumulated experience. To select among alternatives, the agent queries the OpenTripPlanner module to obtain multimodal route options across the transport network; these candidate itineraries
are then evaluated against the agent’s preferences and constraints. The finalized itinerary is then transmitted to
the corresponding inhabitant agent in the GAMA simulation environment for execution.

Figure 6: The reasoning flow of the Generative Agent.
This iterative cycle allows agents to refine their choices across simulation days. For example, as depicted in Figure 6, an
agent may learn on day Dk−1 that waiting times at the Jean Jaurès stop average 18 minutes for Bus 16. On day Dk , the
agent applies this knowledge by avoiding Bus 16 and selecting an alternative route. The mechanism is conceptually
similar to a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) in natural language processing, where outputs from the step Dk−1 serve
as inputs to the next step Dk . Here, however, continuity is maintained by the explicit memory management system, and
the objective is to generate adaptive travel plans rather than text sequences.
Figure 7 illustrates the data flow for constructing a prompt used with the LLM—the core engine of the Generative
Agent. The process begins with an activity that is triggered at a specific time. Next, OpenTripPlanner is queried to
provide several candidate travel options. The activity and travel options are then combined and sent to the memory
stream (long-term memory) to retrieve related memory entries, as discussed in Section 3.4.3. Finally, four elements are
integrated to build the prompt: (i) the activity, (ii) the agent’s personal identity, (iii) the related memory entries, and (iv)
the candidate travel options. This prompt is then passed to the LLM, which selects the most suitable travel route for the
agent in the GAMA simulation.

consequently the token count—also decreases. The reduction in the number of tokens also results in a corresponding
decrease in the number of model calls during the same periods.

5

Discussion

The proposed framework provides a foundational step toward integrating large language model-based generative agents
with agent-based transportation simulations in complex multimodal systems. Unlike previous studies that focus on
simplified or small-scale networks, this work demonstrates integration with the complex multimodal transport network.
18

Modeling realistic human behavior using generative agents in a multimodal transport system

Future research should extend the framework to incorporate additional complexities such as traffic incidents, social
interactions, and inter-agent influences, which are critical in realistic multimodal environments.
In theory, the integration of generative agents addresses two longstanding challenges in transportation modeling:
reliance on extensive calibration data and the limited adaptability of rule-based agents. However, this flexibility comes
with trade-offs. Unlike rule-based systems, generative agents function as a “black box,” making their decision-making
difficult to interpret or control. The hallucination problem further complicates reliability, requiring careful validation
and mitigation.
Prior research has employed human-in-the-loop methods, where experts evaluate generative agent decisions at a
microscopic level. While this improves reliability, such methods are resource-intensive and difficult to scale when
applied to large populations in city-scale simulations.
Finally, computational cost and runtime present practical limitations. Running simulations with large language
model-based agents is both expensive and time-consuming, constraining scalability.
Looking ahead, several directions may help address these challenges.
• Validation: The validation issue can be partially mitigated by exploiting existing transportation surveys. In
Toulouse, survey data could be used to validate the behavior of agents at the city scale. Although these datasets
lack detailed information on personal traits, they nonetheless provide a feasible basis for testing whether
simulated mobility patterns align with observed behaviors.
• Cost effective approaches: To address the cost of large language models, future research should investigate
resource-efficient techniques such as agent clustering, batch prompting, or hybrid methods that combine
generative reasoning with lightweight rule-based components. These approaches may allow large-scale
simulations while keeping costs manageable.
• Hybrid models: Incorporating soft rule-based constraints into generative agent behavior could reduce unpredictability without undermining flexibility. By bounding the outputs of large language models within
reasonable ranges of expected behaviors, such hybrid methods may preserve adaptability while avoiding
unrealistic decisions.

6

Conclusion

This paper introduced a simulation framework integrating large language model-based generative agents with GAMA
to model adaptive decision-making in multimodal transportation. By separating spatial simulation from intelligent
reasoning, agents leverage perception, memory, and reflection to plan routes using real-world GTFS and OSM data,
producing behavior patterns resembling human decision-making. Compared to rule-based approaches, the framework
reduces reliance on calibration and enables more human-like responses.
Results show that the agent is capable of converging to a habit over the course of the simulation, while simultaneously
optimizing its travel decisions to arrive on time for daily activities. However, this convergence occurs slowly due to
weekly variations in the GTFS data. In addition, we need to iteratively evaluate the process to identify the most effective
setup and gain a deeper understanding of the behavior of the LLM models.
Future work will focus on scaling simulations to larger populations through clustering or batch prompting, incorporating
social interactions to capture collective behavior; and validating agent behavior against real-world survey data to ensure
realism; and combination with rule-based methods to enhance interpretability and control. Overall, this work lays the
groundwork for more flexible and adaptive transportation modeling using generative agents.
