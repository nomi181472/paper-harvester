PhoneBot: A Low-Cost Open Humanoid Robot Platform Reusing
Smartphones

arXiv:2610.08737v1 [cs.RO] 6 Oct 2026

Ruochen Hou1 , Quanyou Wang1,† , Daniel Koh1,† , and Dennis W. Hong1
https://phonebot.dev
Abstract— The adoption of humanoid robots in education and
research remains limited by high hardware costs, complex sensing systems, and substantial computational requirements. This
paper presents PhoneBot, a low-cost, open-source humanoid
robot platform that repurposes commodity smartphones as its
primary sensing and computing unit. By using a smartphone’s
integrated inertial measurement unit (IMU), camera, wireless
connectivity, and onboard processing capabilities, PhoneBot
reduces hardware costs and simplifies the system architecture.
The robot combines a modular lower-body structure driven by
13 low-cost actuators with a torso-mounted smartphone that
supports perception, control computation, and user interaction.
We describe the mechanical design, software architecture,
and real-time communication framework that support stable
locomotion and capabilities including vision-based human following, conversational interaction, filming, and mobile telepresence. Experimental evaluations demonstrate reliable walking,
perception-driven interaction, and straightforward deployment
using off-the-shelf consumer smartphones. With fully opensource hardware and software designs, PhoneBot provides
an affordable, reproducible platform for education, research,
and rapid prototyping. More details are available at https:
//phonebot.dev.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Compact humanoid robots provide accessible platforms
for studying locomotion, physical interaction, and embodied intelligence. Their reduced size supports experimentation in laboratories and classrooms with limited space and
infrastructure. Recent platforms have improved accessibility through open-source hardware, accessible manufacturing
methods, and commercially available components [1], [2].
Nevertheless, hardware costs and system integration effort
remain barriers to broader adoption in education and research. Beyond mechanical structure assembly, users must
connect and configure components for motion control, perception, communication, and interaction. Further reducing
these barriers requires simplifying the integration of sensing,
computing, and communication capabilities.
As the widely available consumer devices, smartphones
offer a practical basis for this approach, where they combine
a camera, an IMU, wireless connectivity, onboard processing,
and a user interface. Reusing these capabilities can reduce
the need for separate sensing and computing hardware while
providing an interface for robot operation and interaction.

Contributions. The contributions of this work are: (i)
a low-cost, modular, open-source humanoid that reuses

Humanoid locomotion has been studied through both
model-based and learning-based approaches. Model-based
methods use robot dynamics for motion planning, tracking,
and balance control [4], [7], [8]. Reinforcement learning
has enabled diverse bipedal locomotion behaviors, including
walking, running, and jumping [9]. Successful simulationto-real transfer depends on both policy design and the
correspondence between simulated and physical robots [10].
Existing approaches include transformer policies trained in
randomized simulation [11], hardware designs that reduce
simulation complexity [12], and explicit modeling of parallel
actuation mechanisms during policy training [13].
GPU-accelerated simulation improves training efficiency
by enabling large-scale parallel rollout collection [14]–[16].
Domain randomization and asymmetric actor–critic training
have been used to support sim-to-real transfer [17], while
symmetry-based data augmentation can improve learning
efficiency and gait symmetry [18], [19].

A. Kinematic Structure and Joint Configuration
The kinematic topology and center-of-mass (CoM) location are illustrated in Fig. 2. PhoneBot has a total of 13
degrees of freedom (DoF), comprising 6 actuated joints per
leg arranged in a conventional serial biped configuration:
hip yaw, hip roll, hip pitch, knee pitch, ankle pitch, and
ankle roll. The right and left legs are actuated by joints
J1–J6 and J7–J12, respectively, as summarized in Table II.
This symmetric arrangement provides 3 rotational DoF at
each hip, 1 DoF at each knee, and 2 DoF at each ankle,
enabling control of foot placement and orientation in threedimensional space. An additional torso yaw joint, J13, connects the lower-body assembly to the smartphone mounting
structure. This joint allows the upper sensing platform to
rotate independently of the stance direction, enabling active
visual tracking, camera reorientation, and perception tasks
without requiring the robot to rotate its entire body.
As shown in Table II, most joints are mechanically
constrained to an operating range of [−90◦ , 90◦ ]. The hip
pitch joints, J3 and J9, have an asymmetric extended range.
The larger hip-pitch excursion increases the available leg
workspace in the sagittal plane and permits greater thigh
flexion during configurations such as deep crouching, large
forward leg swings, and recovery motions. All 13 joints are
actuated using DYNAMIXEL XL430-W250-T servo motors,
with key specifications summarized in Table III. At $23.90
per unit, this actuator minimizes platform cost despite being
the system’s primary expense.
B. Electronic and System Integration
Fig. 3 illustrates the system structure of PhoneBot.
PhoneBot uses the smartphone for perception, locomotion
policy inference, and access to cloud-connected services,
while the onboard mini single-board computer (SBC) handles

communication with the actuators and execution of jointposition commands. Utilizing the smartphone’s integrated
camera, IMU, and wireless interfaces eliminates the need to
source standalone modules that require precision mounting
and calibration.
The smartphone has no physical electrical connection with
the robot hardware. Instead, high-level control commands,
joint telemetry, and gamepad inputs are exchanged bidirectionally over Wi-Fi between the smartphone and the SBC.
This is an intentional design choice to overcome hardware
limitations in wired connections, and allow for compatibility
with different smartphone models capable of running the
software application.

B. Observation, Action, and Reward
We train an omnidirectional walk policy with asymmetric
actor–critic PPO. The actor receives only signals that exist on
the phone (noisy IMU and joint feedback). The critic receives
a larger privileged state that exists only in simulation, which
improves value estimation without changing the observation
used at deployment.
a) Observation space.: The actor observation ot concatenates noisy gyroscope ωt , projected gravity gt , the velocity command ct = (vx , vy , ωz ), joint positions relative to
the offset home pose, joint velocities, the previous action,
and a two-foot gait phase (cos ϕL , cos ϕR , sin ϕL , sin ϕR ).
Joint positions include the load-dependent backlash of (6).
When ∥ct ∥ is below a deadzone of 0.05, the phase provided
to the networks is replaced by (π, π) so the same policy
can stand still. The critic input is this proprioceptive vector
plus a noisy base linear velocity, a noiseless copy of IMU
and joint state, and quantities available only in simulation:
base height, actuator force, binary foot contacts, foot linear
velocities, and swing air time. Table V lists this partition.
Actor dimension is 52; critic dimension is 120.
b) Action space.: The policy outputs a 13-dimensional
joint-position residual at at 50 Hz. Desired positions follow (1) and are converted to torque by the XL430 model
of Sec. IV-A, not by an unsaturated position actuator.
Commands are sampled in vx , vy ∈ [−0.5, 0.5] m/s and
ωz ∈ [−1.0, 1.0] rad/s, with probability 0.1 of a zero (stand)
command.

C. Sagittal Mirroring
On a torque-limited platform the walk policy can converge
to an asymmetric local optimum: one foot remains forward,
the other remains behind, and most of the load and XL430
torque are applied through a single support leg. We observed
this asymmetric gait, with the left foot leading and the right
foot supporting, on checkpoints trained without mirroring.
PhoneBot is left–right symmetric, so that gait is an avoidable
bias rather than a mechanical necessity.
We therefore augment each PPO update with a sagittalplane mirror of the collected batch. One physics step produces the real transition; the trainer then concatenates a
mirrored copy ( o′t , a′t ) for the actor loss. Advantages and
returns are computed only on the real trajectory and repeated
for the mirrored policy samples, so the critic does not
estimate values for synthetic trajectories. The value loss is
computed only on real samples: the privileged critic contains
world-frame signals that cannot be mirrored without the
floating-base yaw.

frame exceeds 55◦ and stops it below 30◦ , so the robot steps
only when trunk yaw cannot finish the turn.
Table VII lists the standup rewards. Pose-to-home terms
are weak so that a recumbent robot is not paid for folding
the legs while still on the floor. Height, upward velocity, and
upright orientation lead the get-up. A stable-stand bonus is
paid only when height, tilt, pose, and base velocity are all
near the standing target. Extra action-rate and foot-slip costs
apply only near that stand. Trunk-to-floor contact is always
penalized. Table VIII lists heading-specific terms. While the
heading policy is stepping, it also uses the walk gait terms
in Table VI at the same weights; those gait terms are zero
while the robot is planted.

V. S MARTPHONE A PPLICATION
PhoneBot uses a commodity smartphone as the torso, the
sensor suite, and the onboard computer. OpenBot showed
that a standard Android phone already provides cameras, an
IMU, CPU and GPU compute, and wireless communication,
and that this hardware is sufficient for robotics workloads
such as person following and navigation when it is attached
to a low-cost wheeled body [6]. PhoneBot applies the same
idea to a biped. The phone is fixed to the trunk, so its IMU
is the body IMU used by the locomotion policies, and its
camera is the robot camera.
A smartphone also provides a maintained software platform (Android or iOS), yearly hardware updates, and a
second-hand market. The electromechanical legs do not need
a separate vision computer or a custom IMU board. The
current PhoneBot application is an Android app that uses
these built-in devices directly.
a) Inertial sensing and locomotion.: The app reads
the phone IMU at the policy rate and forms the actor
observation in Sec. IV-B: angular velocity, projected gravity,
joint state received from the motors, and the user command.
A TensorFlow Lite graph of the trained actor runs on the
phone CPU or GPU. The app sends joint-position targets over
Wi-Fi (UDP) to a small onboard computer that forwards them
to the XL430 bus. The same path supports walk, standup, and
heading.
b) Communication and language.: Wi-Fi carries motor
state and commands. It also connects the phone to a laptop
AI backend: the phone records speech on the microphone,
the backend runs local speech-to-text (whisper.cpp) and a
large language model, and the reply is spoken on the phone
speaker with Piper text-to-speech. The API key stays on the
laptop, not in the Android package.
c) Vision and human interaction.: The rear camera runs
on-device object detection and human pose estimation (ML
Kit and MediaPipe). These detections support person following and other look-at behaviors together with the heading
policy. The same camera and IMU also feed ARCore visual–
inertial SLAM for onboard localization. The touchscreen
displays status, camera overlays, and controls, so a user can
operate the robot without a separate operator computer.
VI. E XPERIMENTS
This section reports two groups of tests: locomotion training from scratch, and smartphone-application behavior on
four phones.
A. Locomotion
We compare the torque-aware walk policy trained with
sagittal mirroring to the same policy trained without mirroring. Both methods use the environment, XL430 actuation
model, observation split, action space, and reward weights of
Sec. IV-B. The only intended difference is whether each PPO
update concatenates a mirrored actor sample (Sec. IV-C).
Each method is trained from scratch on flat terrain for five
independent seeds {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}. The plots in Fig. 4 show
the first 1.5 × 108 environment steps. At every evaluation

point the current policy is rolled out in 256 evaluation
environments. The solid line is the mean across the seeds
that exist for that method. The shaded band is ±1 standard
deviation across those seeds. The band is not the standard
deviation inside a single evaluation.
The logged episode return is the accumulated per-step
reward of Sec. IV-B: the weighted sum of the terms in
Table VI, multiplied by the control timestep ∆t = 0.02 s
and then summed over the episode. The component panels
show those same weighted terms before the ∆t factor. A
larger value is better for a positive term. A value closer to
zero is better for a cost term.
Fig. 4a is the overall episode return. Mirroring raises
this return earlier and to a higher final level. Its band is
also narrower. The policy therefore finds a high-return gait
with less dependence on the random seed. Training without
mirroring is more seed-dependent and can remain in different
local gait optima.
The two tracking terms explain much of that gap.
Linear-velocity tracking (Fig. 4b) and yaw-velocity tracking
(Fig. 4c) both increase earlier with mirroring, so the robot
learns to follow the joystick command in fewer environment
steps. The alive term (Fig. 4d) increases as well, which means
the mirrored policy spends more of the episode upright and
terminates less often by falling.
The swing terms show how that command tracking is
realized. Foot air time (Fig. 4e) and foot-height cost (Fig. 4f)
rise (or recover toward zero) quickly and with a narrow band
when mirroring is enabled. Without mirroring the same terms
have a wide band: some seeds learn to lift the swing foot,
while others keep the foot near the floor and drag it. That
spread is a direct sign of seed-sensitive local optima.
Once the foot leaves the ground, mirroring also follows the
scheduled gait more closely. Foot-phase tracking (Fig. 4g)
and foot-corner phase tracking (Fig. 4h) are higher and more
consistent with mirroring. Support is more stable as well: the
slip cost (Fig. 4i) returns closer to zero, so the stance foot
slides less on the ground. The feet-parallel cost (Fig. 4j) also
recovers closer to zero, which means the soles stay flatter
to the floor. The pose cost (Fig. 4k) remains nearer zero,
which means the joints stay closer to the standing home pose
instead of collapsing into a crouched or twisted stance.
Action rate is the one term that is not uniformly better
at every step (Fig. 4l). Early in training the mirrored policy
changes its action more than the baseline, so its action-rate
cost is briefly larger. After that transient it reduces the action
rate and finishes below the non-mirrored curve. The extra
early motion is therefore paid back by a quieter late gait,
not by a permanently more aggressive controller.

domain randomization, noise, delay, backlash, joint offsets,
and pushes disabled. Each policy receives a 0.5 m/s forward
command and is rolled out for 20 s from the phase pairs
[0, π] and [π, 0] at 1.5 Hz; the first second is discarded.

B. Smartphone Application
The PhoneBot application is tested on four Android
phones. All complete the same onboard functions: IMU
sensing, UDP motor communication, locomotion-policy inference, on-device vision, and human–robot interaction. The
set includes a 2017 Honor 9 and three prepaid phones bought
for about $50–$80 and locked to Tracfone or Total Wireless
(moto g 2025, Galaxy A16 5G, and moto g 2024). The
Honor 9 is an old flagship; the prepaid units are current low-

cost phones. Both classes run the app without a separate
vision or SLAM computer. That is the point of using the
phone as the robot computer: even a cheap or old handset has
enough CPU, camera, and IMU for these tasks. The current
stack is CPU-bound, so GPU details are omitted.
Table IX lists one phone per row. Hardware fields occupy
the left block. The right block is CPU usage. Idle is the app
with no function running. The other columns are IMU sensing, policy inference, vision detection, SLAM, and human–
robot interaction. ARCore SLAM on the Honor 9 is marked
× because Google services are unavailable on that Huawei
device in US. In qualitative walking trials, ARCore SLAM
remained stable despite the periodic body motion, with no
evident degradation caused by the bipedal gait.
VII. C ONCLUSION
This paper presented PhoneBot, a low-cost, open-source
compact humanoid platform that integrates a commodity
smartphone with a modular 13-DoF body for sensing, onboard computation, and interaction. Its locomotion pipeline
combines a torque-aware actuator model with reinforcement
learning in MJX. Evaluations across five random seeds
showed that sagittal mirroring improved sample efficiency
and training consistency compared with training without mirroring. Hardware demonstrations included walking, standing up, and camera-heading control, while application tests
across four Android phones demonstrated the feasibility
of reusing consumer smartphones for robot sensing, policy
inference, and interaction. The hardware and software are released to support reproducible experimentation in education
and research.

The current platform lacks arms, and its locomotion
policies were trained on flat terrain. The limited torque of
its low-cost servos also constrains payload and dynamic
motion. Future work will investigate locomotion on uneven
terrain and the integration of lightweight arms for object
manipulation. Extending the training pipeline to these tasks
will require accounting for changes in whole-body dynamics,
contact conditions, and actuator demands. We also plan to
explore language-model-based task planning that translates
spoken instructions and visual observations into commands
for learned skills. Deploying suitable language models on
the smartphone could further reduce reliance on the external
backend currently used for dialogue.
