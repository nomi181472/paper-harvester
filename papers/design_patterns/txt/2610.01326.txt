Abstract. This paper presents the European Crisis Management Ontology (ECMO), a modular OWL-based ontology intended as a crosssectoral reference for disaster risk reduction and response. ECMO is
designed to be organised as a network of ontological modules. Among
the modules, ECMO-CORE captures fundamental crisis management
concepts such as hazard, event, exposure, impact, and response measure and uses ontology design patterns and the OWL2 punning technique to resolve ambiguities between hazard types and event manifestations. In addition, domain-specific modules are defined as in the case
of the public health module aligned with SNOMED CT and ICD-11.
To demonstrate the resource’s utility, we used ECMO to represent the
data of the Epidemic Intelligence from Open Sources system of the
Joint Research Centre to generate an end-to-end pipeline that populates an ECMO-compliant knowledge graph from unstructured epidemiological news. Initial results demonstrate that ECMO provides the formal guardrails necessary for consistent and unified knowledge representation and integration. The ontology is publicly available at https:
//doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20070268 and is released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license.
Keywords: Ontology engineering · Semantic interoperability · Crisis
management · Cross-sectoral coordination · Knowledge representation
and integration.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, increasingly frequent extreme weather events due to
the climate crisis, the conflicts in Ukraine and Middle East are all recent and
ongoing complex crises that affect multiple policy sectors. In such a challenging context, the need for a cross-sectoral and whole-of-government approach to
preparedness and crisis management is emerging, advocating for more coordinated and effective policy interventions [14]. The European Preparedness Union
Strategy [7] is one outstanding example of such a trend. In the scope of this policy strategy, the European Crisis Management Platform (ECMP) is an initiative
led by the European Commission’s Secretariat General to enable interoperability
and harmonisation across existing early warning and crisis management systems.
The main aim is to ensure cross-sectoral information exchange and coordination
during emergencies.
However, interoperability cannot be achieved without a shared conceptual
understanding among the communities of practice involved in crisis management,
including domain experts, policy makers, and operational actors. A key challenge
lies in the heterogeneity of terminology and their intended meaning and context
of usage (different "perspectives") used across sectors and competence levels
(e.g., local, national, and international). Furthermore, the adoption of different
standards by crisis management practitioners is often complicated by their bound
to distinct legislation, mandates, consolidated practices, or by linguistic barriers.
Ontologies are suitable to address these challenges, but existing models target specific sectors individually (most notably health), while crisis management
is overlooked. Shared terminologies, such as the United Nations in the context
of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) [1], are released as
glossaries within reports or annexes to legislation, making them subject to ambiguity. For example, in contrast to the United Nations terminology, the definitions
provided in the European regulation on serious cross-border threats [8] use the
term "threat" interchangeably with "hazard" (the potential source of harm)
and "hazardous event" (the actual manifestation of that hazard). Another issue
is that existing ontologies typically adopt a non-modular design, making them
difficult to extend and adapt to new crisis scenarios (cf. Section 2).
To address these challenges in support of the ECMP initiative, the Joint
Research Centre (JRC) of the European Commission was requested to develop a
novel European Crisis Management Ontology (ECMO)7 , which we present in this
document as a first prototype version. Funded by the European Commission’s
Health Emergency Preparedness and Response Authority (DG HERA)8 , the
effort has been carried out in conjunction with the ontology experts who are
co-authoring this paper, as well as with the EU policy services that are involved
in the joint crisis management initiative.
ECMO is conceived as a modular ontology network for crisis management,
grounded in foundational ontology design patterns and aligned with a number

Beyond addressing good practices in ontology engineering, such as ontology
modularisation, reuse, and alignment with standards, ECMO is designed with
a strong policy and societal orientation. It aims at enhancing cross-sectoral interoperability by capturing shared concepts and relationships relevant to crisis
preparedness, response, and recovery. Its scope spans multiple domains to reflect
the interconnected nature of contemporary crises, including but not limited to:
– Public health threats/emergencies and health security;
– Food safety and food security, animal and plant health;
– Weather extremes and climate change-related threats;
– Human-induced environmental degradation and disasters;
– Critical infrastructure disruption (including from extraterrestrial hazards
such as solar flares);
– Civil protection and humanitarian aid;
– Security, hybrid threats, and cybersecurity;
– Nuclear hazards and incidents;
– Armed conflicts and geopolitics;
– Migration and refugee crises;
– Financial shocks and economic crises;
– Societal hazards, human rights, and the rule of law.
This broad coverage reflects the ontology’s intended applicability across heterogeneous domains and stakeholders, supporting its reuse beyond the original
development context and reinforcing its potential impact in real-world crisis
management settings.
In addition, ECMO is positioned to support neuro-symbolic approaches (see
Section 7). Its explicit and curated semantics are designed to steer the extraction
capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). Whereas LLMs can be prompted
to generate any kind of content, from text to software code and knowledge
graphs, ECMO acts as a qualitative, logically verified prior that grounds LLM
outputs in accountable vocabularies and formal axioms. This aligns with emerging neuro-symbolic conditioning approaches [5], in which formal ontologies serve
as forward pass (the contextual "inference" of LLMs) guardrails for knowledge
extraction, and reasoning tasks, especially in high-stakes settings where semantic
ambiguity carries operational risk.
The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews existing crisis management ontology, identifying key limitations that motivate ECMO. Section 3
outlines the requirements and envisaged coverage of the ontology. Section 4
presents ECMO-CORE, covering its foundational alignment with DOLCE, the
OWL2 punning pattern for hazard and disaster-risk subtypes, and the frame
cluster organisation. Section 5 describes the ontology network structure and its
domain-specific extensions, namely for public health. Section 6 details the alignment methodology and mapping to existing standards, including SNOMED CT,

Related work

The literature on crisis management ontologies is growing but fragmented, reflecting the diversity of application domains and modelling approaches. Existing
work can be broadly categorised into two categories: (i) cross-sectoral ontologies,
and (ii) domain-specific ontologies.
The Emergency Management Ontology (EMO) [10] is one of the most prominent attempts to provide an ontology for crisis management. EMO targets the
general crisis management lifecycle and its actors. Although EMO is described
as a first step into a robust solution, it is limited in some aspects. First of
all, the scope of the ontology does not properly account for the case of international organisations that monitor ongoing and potential events to intervene
(based on their mandate) when the local capacity is exceeding. Moreover, EMO
relies on labour-intensive manual modelling, lacks automated filtering of the
massive data streams that responders face, has no fully-functional prototype for
real-time use, requires constant updates during emergencies, and involves complex multi-method mapping and possible extra ontologies to achieve the desired
semantic interoperability. Crisis Response Ontology (CROnto) [3] focuses on
supporting decision-making processes during emergency response operations. It
provides a structured representation of crisis situations, actors, and response actions, with particular emphasis on operational coordination. However, its scope
remains relatively narrow and primarily oriented towards specific response scenarios. In [13], an ontology for climate crisis management is presented, targeting
environmental hazards such as wildfires and extreme weather events. This approach does not generalise easily to other types of crises, such as health emergencies or security threats. Similarly, the work presented in [2] proposes an ontology
for information integration in disaster management covering the description of
damages caused by the disaster and the resources available to organisations that
are relevant for each type of damage. Although it addresses important aspects
of semantic integration, the approach is coupled to specific use cases.
Several ontologies target specific domains, namely public health and epidemiological, relevant to crisis management. SimPHO ontology [15] is an ontology
developed for simulation modelling of population health. It aims to reuse medical concepts from existing ontologies and to add health-specific classes (e.g., risk
factors and health-policy interventions). As main limitations, SimPHO authors
identify a lack of domain coverage and formal evolution. The Simple Event Model
+ Clinical Element Model (SEM-CEM) ontology [6] aims to solve the chronic
problem of linking patient-specific records across health-care and public-health
systems when demographic identifiers are inconsistent, incomplete, or change
over time. The main limitations of SEM-CEM concern the narrow geographical and institutional scope, the use of simulated data, and the limited coverage

of life-course events. The Epidemiology Ontology (EPO) [16] aims to address,
as the authors describe, the previous incompleteness of biomedical ontologies
in covering epidemiologically specific concepts (e.g., exposure ratios and demographic and epidemiological parameters), by modelling missing terms and linking
them to established ontologies. Some of the challenges with EPO identified by
the authors include incomplete coverage, insufficient granularity, and no formal
evaluation of annotation performance.
Beyond domain-specific ontologies, the Sendai Framework Terminology on
DRR [17] provides a normative vocabulary of 38 terms that serve as a de-facto
cross-sectoral reference for DRR policy. However, this terminology is published
only as a glossary - not as a formal ontology - and therefore lacks machinereadable axiomatization.
Overall, the existing ontologies and domain-specific resources are inefficient
to deal with a pan-European high-stakes crisis management setting given the
fragmented domain coverage. Each resource aims to tackle a single sector, missing the cross-sectoral concepts we aim to unify (e.g., legal frameworks, finance,
humanitarian aid, etc.); lack of alignment with European standards (i.e., not
mapping clearly to EU-specific normative frameworks); and insufficient provenance, modularity, and scalability (i.e., lack of governance). These shortcomings
motivate the creation of ECMO, which deliberately combines explicit, curated
OWL semantics, modular cross-sectoral design, and direct alignment with European standards to overcome the inefficiencies observed in the existing landscape.

Requirements and coverage

The crisis management landscape is characterised by a wide range of specialised
and critical information systems, each a valuable source of heterogeneous information. A key requirement for ECMO is therefore to capture the knowledge
embedded in these systems, which are widely used by European stakeholders
across different sectors. In this context, the initial version of the ECMO ontology is guided by an analysis of the following platforms that support crisis preparedness, monitoring, and response and will be integrated or connected with
the ECMP "system of systems":
– The ECMP Core Platform, a suite of tools aimed at providing harmonised
functionalities and workflows for incident management.
– DG HERA’s Advanced Technology for Health INtelligence and Action IT
system (ATHINA), an information system developed to strengthen Europe’s
ability to prevent, detect, and rapidly respond to serious cross-border threats
to health, particularly focused on Medical Countermeasures (MCMs).
– The Early Warning Response System (EWRS)9 , a secure web-based platform
that ensures rapid information exchange and coordinated risk management
among EU Member States, enabling real-time sharing of situational awareness to support a unified response to serious cross-border health threats as
defined by Regulation (EU) 2022/2371.

In addition to those technical systems, the ontology design is informed by
the analysis of legislation and science-for-policy recommendations, which define
the regulatory and operational context in which crisis management systems are
deployed. However, they also introduce challenges due to semantic ambiguity. In
particular, terminology is often used interchangeably to refer both to abstract
concepts and to their concrete manifestations. For example, distinctions such
as "slow-onset" versus "sudden-onset" disasters typically describe the dynamics of underlying hazards rather than intrinsic properties of the disaster events
themselves.
Based on this analysis, the ontology is designed to meet key requirements.
As an initial foundation, this paper introduces the ECMO-CORE module together with selected extensions, and provides a first approach to populating the
ECMP knowledge graph using news sources. This demonstrates the feasibility of
integrating heterogeneous data sources within a unified semantic framework and
highlights the potential of ECMO for supporting real-world crisis management
applications. To evaluate the expressiveness and practical relevance of the ontology, a set of competency questions are defined that reflect typical information
needed across domains.

ECMO-CORE

To bridge the different systems, a higher level of abstraction is necessary. We
rely on ontology design patterns that address foundational concepts and relations and can be specialised according to the terminological and possibly more
restrictive rules that characterise the addressed knowledge domains. Following
this approach, we designed the ECMO-CORE Ontology, the foundational module of the network of ontological modules, that describes the structure of a crisis
pipeline, independent of any specific domain.

8

A. Gangemi and R.T. Sousa et al.

ECMO-CORE formalises crisis management concepts as OWL2 classes and
named individuals, organised into 10 conceptual frames (Risk, Hazard, Disaster,
Exposure and Vulnerability, Capacity and Resilience, Governance and Management, Response and Recovery, Risk Reduction Instruments, Underlying Risk
Drivers, Impact and Loss), and grounded in the DOLCE foundational ontology
via semantic alignments (Figure 1).
A further methodological contribution of ECMO-CORE concerns the treatment of hazard types and their event-level counterparts. In ontology engineering,
domain taxonomies of types (e.g., "Biological Hazard" as a general category) and
their spatio-temporal instances (e.g., "the 2026 Chicago meningococcal cluster"
as an actual event) pose a well-known modelling tension. ECMO-CORE resolves this by adopting OWL2 punning: each hazard subtype (such as subtypes
of BiologicalHazard, NaturalHazard, TechnologicalHazard) is simultaneously a
class of situations (providing the OWL-DL type for instances) and a named
individual (typed as an instance of an anchor class such as BiologicalHazard,
and linked to peers via fschema:subsumedUnder, a transitive property from the
Framester schema). This technique – combined with DOLCE’s Descriptions and
Situations (D&S) pattern – cleanly separates the intensional (definitional, framelevel) hierarchy from the extensional (instance-level) one, preventing the metaclass conflation that affects many DRR ontologies and enabling sound OWL2
DL reasoning.

Foundational alignment with DOLCE design patterns

ECMO-CORE is aligned with the DOLCE Ultra-Lite (DUL) foundational ontology. The alignment is not merely taxonomic, as ECMO-CORE classes reuse the
DUL’s design patterns to capture both the descriptive and situational aspects
of crisis management concepts. This design choice is particularly relevant for
modelling the distinction between general knowledge about hazards and their
concrete manifestations. As defined by the United Nations Office for DRR (UNDRR), a hazard is "[a type of] process, phenomenon or human activity that may
cause loss of life, injury or other health impacts, property damage, social and
economic disruption or environmental degradation", while a hazardous event is
"the manifestation of a hazard in a particular place during a particular period
of time". In ECMO-CORE, the alignment with DUL is operationalised through
the following modelling patterns:
– ecmocore:Hazard is typed as dul:Description and dul:ContextualDisposition
(a contextual attribute of a local situation or event). As a Description,
a hazard frames the conditions that hazardous events must satisfy. As a
ecmocore:ContextualDisposition, it inheres in the environment as a latent
potential for harm. This dual typing operationalises the UNDRR definition,
which characterises a hazard as a type of process or phenomenon - i.e. a
general description rather than a specific occurrence.
– ecmocore:HazardousEvent is a subclass of dul:Event and dul:Situation, carrying existential restrictions on temporal extent (dul:TimeInterval), location
(dul:Place), and the hazard it manifests. The Event reading captures the occurrence, and the Situation reading captures the fact that certain conditions
are simultaneously satisfied.
– ecmocore:Exposure is modelled as a subclass of dul:Situation with restrictions requiring both an exposed element and a hazard, to which the element
is exposed (ecmocore:isExposedTo some Hazard).
– ecmocore:Mitigation, ecmocore:Prevention, ecmocore:Response, ecmocore:Recovery,
and ecmocore:Preparedness are subclasses of ecmocore:DisasterRiskReductionPhase.
Each carries a dual reading: (1) as event-specific actions enacted during a
particular hazardous event (Situation/Action reading), and (2) as normative
task types prescribed by a ecmocore:DisasterRiskManagementPlan (Description
reading, via ecmocore:isTaskOf). This eliminates the need for separate tasktype and task-instance class hierarchies.
– ecmocore:Intervention is a subclass of both dul:Action and dul:Situation,
with restrictions linking it to a Disaster Risk Management (DRM) phase
(ecmocore:inDRMPhase), a capacity (ecmocore:involvesCapacity), and a measure type (ecmocore:involvesMeasureType).
4.2

OWL2 punning pattern for hazard and disaster-risk subtypes

Following the classification of hazard information profiles, a number of subclasses are derived from Hazard, based on UNDRR Hazard Information Profiles (HIPs) taxonomy. The key modelling challenge in DRR is that terms like

10

A. Gangemi and R.T. Sousa et al.

ecmocore:BiologicalHazard function simultaneously as general types (with defi-

nitional properties) and as classifiers for specific events. ECMO-CORE addresses
this issue through OWL2 punning, where the same IRI serves as both a class and
a named individual. This allows the ontology to distinguish, without conflation,
between the definitional and factual levels of representation.
At the definitional level, anchor classes such as ecmocore:DisasterRisk and
ecmocore:Hazard are modelled as subclasses of dul:Description, capturing general knowledge about types of phenomena. At the factual level, anchor classes
such as ecmocore:HazardousEvent and ecmocore:Disaster are modelled as subclasses of dul:Situation, representing concrete occurrences situated in space
and time. Within this framework, hazard sub-types are modelled in a dual manner. On the one hand, they are introduced as subclasses of the relevant situation
class, enabling standard OWL instance typing for events. On the other hand, the
same entities are represented as named individuals of the corresponding anchor
description class, thereby participating in an intensional taxonomy.
These two perspectives are explicitly linked through fschema:subsumedUnder,
which encodes specialisation relationships at the level of descriptions. This separation prevents metaclass conflation: rdfs:subClassOf carries the extensional
DUL typing for instances, while fschema:subsumedUnder carries the frame-level
specialisation hierarchy. The pattern is documented in the ontology’s editorial
notes and follows the D&S design pattern from the Ontology Design Patterns
community.

4.3

Frame cluster organisation

To provide a coherent organisational structure, ECMO-CORE groups the terms
into ten named frame clusters (F1–F10). Each frame cluster is modelled as a
dul:Description, thereby capturing a shared semantic context that brings together conceptually related terms. Membership of a given term in a frame cluster is expressed through the property ecmocore:frameCluster, defined as a subproperty of dul:isDescribedBy. This frame-based organisation supports both
human navigation and systematic coverage verification during ontology engineering. The resulting frame clusters are presented in Table 1.

4.4

Observation and measurement pattern

A shared observation infrastructure is implemented with reified observation/estimation patterns. In addition to directly asserting, for instance, that a hazardous event has a certain intensity as a literal value, the ontology provides
classes such as ecmocore:IntensityObservation, ecmocore:MagnitudeObservation,
and ecmocore:SeverityAssessment, each linked to a ecmocore:MeasurementScale
and an ecmocore:ObservationSource. This reification supports provenance tracking, multi-source assessment, and temporal evolution of severity assessments
during a crisis.

5.2

EIOS module

The EIOS module provides a SKOS-aligned ontology for the EIOS system. At
its core, the module defines:
– eios:Vocabulary as a subclass of skos:ConceptScheme representing high-level
thematic domains (e.g., Human Health, Chemicals and Food Safety).
– eios:CategoryGrouping as a subclass of skos:Collection grouping related
categories within a vocabulary. Groupings are linked to their members using
skos:member.
– eios:Category as a subclass of skos:Concept, representing specific entities or
phenomena (e.g., Anthrax, Taenia, Adenovirus).

In addition to this SKOS layer, the EIOS module also models the eventdetection workflow. The class eios:OpenSourceData captures information objects

5.3

DG HERA module

The DG HERA module in ECMO provides a model for evaluating medical countermeasures and pathogen risk in the context of public health emergencies. The
key features of this module include:
– hera:PathogenRiskAssessment is the core class and represents the risk assessment of a pathogen according to specific criteria. Subclasses define the main
assessment dimensions, namely hera:PHEICPotential, hera:Transmissibility,
hera:PopulationImmunity, hera:Pathogenicity, hera:AvailabilityOfVaccines,
hera:AvailabilityOfTherapeutics, and hera:EUCrossBorderThreatPotential.
– Each assessment dimension is linked to a hera:AssessmentCriterion (e.g.,
hera:PHEICCriterion).
– For each dimension, it is defined a scale class (e.g., hera:PHEICPotentialScale)
and a set of ordered qualitative values (e.g., hera:Low_PHEICPotential).
– Object properties such as hera:hasPHEICPotential, to link a hera:Pathogen
to its risk assessment values.
The DG HERA module enables the prioritisation of health threats based on
multiple risk dimensions, supporting decision-making for medical countermeasures deployment.

6

Alignment with existing standards

ECMO adopts a semi-automatic mapping strategy aimed at facilitating the integration of external standards and sources of contextual information for crossdomain reasoning. The adopted methodology follows a multi-step workflow,
where automated ontology alignment tools generate candidate correspondences,
which are subsequently curated and validated by domain experts. The process
begins with the selection of ontologies and terminological resources to be aligned.
This step considers domain relevance and conceptual coverage. Following ontology selection, automated ontology matching systems are employed to generate initial candidate correspondences between concepts across the selected standards. The candidate mappings produced by different systems are subsequently
aggregated. The use of multiple alignment tools increases mapping coverage and

14

reduces reliance on a single matching system, allowing complementary matching
techniques to capture a broader range of potential semantic relations. During
this step, mappings with higher confidence scores or stronger agreement among
systems are prioritised.
The resulting candidate alignments are then subjected to expert validation.
Expert assessment plays a critical role in the process, as incorrect mappings
may lead to inappropriate operational decisions in crisis scenarios. Validated
mappings can be represented using standardised semantic web mechanisms such
as owl:equivalentClass, owl:sameAs and rdfs:subClassOf, as well as SKOS relations including skos:exactMatch, skos:closeMatch, and skos:relatedMatch. Finally, logical consistency verification is performed using reasoning tools to ensure
that the identified mappings do not introduce contradictions into the ontology.
6.1

HIPS, SNOMED, ICD-11

Three resources are identified as particularly relevant in the public health domain: (i) HIPs [17] developed to support the implementation of DRR and riskinformed investment, aligned with the Sendai Framework for DRR 2015–2030;
(ii) SNOMED CT [4] as a comprehensive clinical terminology; (iii) ICD-11 [11]
as an international standard for disease classification. Together, these resources
cover complementary perspectives on hazards and health conditions.
The public health module models pathogens and diseases as individuals, each
annotated with preferred labels and biological taxonomy, and links them to
SNOMED CT, EIOS, and HIPS. The mappings between these resources were
obtained by applying LogMap [12]. The following turtle snippet illustrates this
approach with the case of Hepatitis B, represented as an ECMO individual and
aligned with corresponding entities in ICD-11, SNOMED CT, and HIPS through

Automated extraction from news sources

A key functionality of ECMO lies in its role as a prior for knowledge graph
extraction from an actual EIOS news item. E.g., a source article reports that the
Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH) identified four cases of Neisseria
meningitidis infections in adults since January 15, 2026, of which two were among
people living in a temporary homeless shelter. All patients developed septicemia,
and one additionally had meningitis.
First, the text is parsed by Text2AMR2FRED [9] to produce an initial graph
of entities, events, and role structures. The base graph is then subjected to ontological verification and domain alignment against the ECMO ontology network
using a set of systematic heuristics from the POLANYI++ methodology [5].
The article itself is represented as eios:OpenSourceData, generating an eios:Signal
for a eios:PublicHealthEvent, thereby linking the extracted knowledge to the
EIOS vocabulary. As an example of non-trivial extraction, the ontology-augmented
pipeline resolves the frequent conflation between pathogen and disease by distinguishing N. meningitidis as a ph:Pathogen and Invasive Meningococcal Disease
as a ph:InfectiousDiseaseOrPathogen. More details are in the GitHub13 .

8

Conclusions and future work

This paper presents ECMO, a modular OWL2-based ontology network designed
to support semantic interoperability across heterogeneous crisis management information systems. Built on foundational ontology design patterns and aligned
with DOLCE, ECMO-CORE provides a cross-sectoral reference model. Its frame
clusters organise core crisis management concepts, from hazard and exposure
to governance, resilience, and impact. The public health extension, the EIOS
module, and the DG HERA module demonstrate the practical extensibility of
the modular architecture, while the semi-automatic alignment methodology enables scalable integration with SNOMED CT, ICD-11, and the UNDRR HIPs.
The end-to-end extraction pipeline, illustrated through a real EIOS surveillance
article, provides concrete evidence of the ontology’s utility as a guardrail for
knowledge extraction. ECMO is a relevant resource both to the Semantic Web
community and to European crisis preparedness policy.
Future work encompasses two main directions. First, an LLM-assisted validation workflow will support iterative and collaborative expert refinement of
ontological definitions. Experts will evaluate definitions in their domain, submit
alternatives where needed, and the resulting inputs will be aggregated to identify convergence and divergence across domains, with LLMs synthesising consolidated definitions that resolve detected ambiguities. Second, an end-to-end
