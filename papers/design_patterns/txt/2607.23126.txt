Vibe to Code: Elucidating Strategic Oscillation of Tacit Knowledge in
Generative AI Design Workflows — An Exploratory Qualitative Study
Daisaku Sato
Graduate School of Knowledge Science, Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology
(JAIST) / Sumitomo Mitsui Finance and Leasing Co., Ltd.
Corresponding author: dice310@gmail.com

Abstract
The rapid adoption of generative AI tools has created new literacy demands for designers who
must verbalize tacit knowledge through natural language prompts. Yet the micro-level cognitive
processes by which designers externalize implicit intentions during iterative AI dialogue remain
underexplored. This exploratory qualitative study examined five expert designers (11–20 years
UI/UX experience, M = 15.4 years) using think-aloud protocols. We identified “Strategic
Oscillation”—experts’ intentional return to vague language (Vibe) after progressing toward
operational specifications (Code), leveraging AI’s probabilistic nature for creative exploration.
We observed shifts from “instruction” to “consultation” mode, deep reflection triggered by
semantic collisions, and one participant’s disengagement revealing conceptual alignment as a
tentative boundary condition. We propose the ECRT cycle (Expectation-Collision-ReflectionTransformation) operationalizing the SECI model’s “externalization” phase for human-AI
collaboration. These findings yield three design patterns for metacognitive support and position
AI as a “probabilistic mirror” catalyzing tacit knowledge externalization.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background: Prompt-Driven Design and Emerging Literacy Demands
Generative AI tools have rapidly permeated design practice. Tools such as Figma AI, Replit AI,
and Cursor now generate UI components directly from natural language text, fundamentally
transforming how designers work. Whereas designers previously externalized mental images
through sketches and wireframes, these tools position natural language “prompts” as the starting
point for design.
This approach gained wide recognition when Karpathy (2025) coined the term “vibe coding” on
social media. This approach—in which programmers and designers convey vague impressions
(“vibes”) instead of specifying code or design details, delegating concretization to AI—can be
situated within the scholarly discourse on end-user programming and natural language
programming (Nardi, 1993; Lieberman et al., 2006). The implications are far-reaching: it
represents a fundamental paradigm shift in the human–machine relationship.
Scope of terminology: In this study, “vibe coding (vibe to code)” is employed not as a
transplantation of a programming technique from the software development context, but as an
analytical concept describing the verbalization process by which designers move from vague
impressions (vibe) to operational specifications (code). Accordingly, the findings of this study
are not directly applicable to programming practice.
Under the previous paradigm (deterministic intent negotiation), humans fully defined the
“correct answer” and machines faithfully executed instructions. Deviations from instructions

2

constituted “errors”—noise to be eliminated. By contrast, collaboration with generative AI
(probabilistic intent negotiation) involves AI probabilistically interpreting ambiguous human
instructions and sometimes producing outputs that deviate substantially from expectations.
Crucially, such deviations are not necessarily “failures.” Rather, they can function as “cues” for
recognizing the ambiguity of one’s own instructions and refining verbalization.
1.2 Problem Statement: The Black Box of Verbalization Processes
Polanyi’s (1966) famous proposition—“we can know more than we can tell”—captures a central
tension in design expertise: much of what designers know resists verbalization. Schön (1983)
conceptualized “reflection-in-action” and Cross (2004) identified “designerly ways of knowing,”
both acknowledging that design expertise resists explicit verbalization.
Nonaka and Takeuchi’s (1995) SECI model theorized that dialogue and metaphor facilitate
“externalization”—the conversion of tacit knowledge to explicit knowledge. However, their
framework presupposed human-to-human dialogue. What happens when the “other” is an AI?
As Collins (2010) noted, AI does not share tacit knowledge the way human interlocutors do. It
lacks embodied experience and social context. Yet precisely for this reason, AI can function as a
“probabilistic mirror” that objectively reflects one’s intentions. Existing research has primarily
focused on prompt skill acquisition outcomes and AI collaboration efficiency. Studies that
systematically trace the micro-level process changes—hesitations, rewrites, and realizations—
occurring during dialogue remain limited.
1.3 Research Objectives and Questions
This study aims to provide a microscopic description of how expert designers verbalize tacit
knowledge and transform their strategies through iterative dialogue with generative AI. As an
3

exploratory study oriented toward theory building rather than generalization, this study’s
contributions are threefold:
Observational contribution: Description of the “instruction-to-consultation” shift and lexical
granularity oscillation (Strategic Oscillation) exhibited by expert designers.
Methodological contribution: Proposal of a protocol analysis framework combining ECRT
with lexical granularity (L1–L3).
Design implications: Derivation of UI design requirements for metacognitive support in
generative AI tools.
Main Research Question (MRQ): In the externalization phase of the SECI model, how does
iterative dialogue with generative AI transform the process by which designers verbalize
ambiguous images?
SRQ1: How do designers leverage gaps between expectations and outputs as cues for
verbalizing latent intentions?
SRQ2: Through what lexical choices and rephrasings do designers linguistically reconstruct
their instructions to AI?
SRQ3: How do the granularity of linguistic expression and aesthetic evaluation criteria
structurally change through iterative dialogue?
This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work and identifies research gaps.
Section 3 presents the theoretical framework of the ECRT cycle and Strategic Oscillation.
Section 4 describes the research design, participant selection, and data collection and analysis
procedures. Section 5 reports three main findings, and Section 6 discusses theoretical and
practical implications. Section 7 presents conclusions.

2. Related Work
4

2.1 Generative AI in Design and Prototyping
The application of generative AI to the design domain is advancing rapidly. Koch et al. (2019)
demonstrated the potential of positioning AI as a creative partner, while Yang et al. (2020)
organized the challenges of AI utilization in UX design. A common characteristic of these tools
is that users describe “what they want to create” in natural language, and AI interprets that intent
to generate visual outputs. However, this “description of intent” itself poses a new cognitive
challenge for designers.
Limitations of existing research: Most research on generative AI design tools has focused on
output quality evaluation and usability assessment. Yet the micro-level cognitive processes of
“how designers verbalize” and “what happens during the verbalization process” have not been
sufficiently elucidated.
2.2 Human-AI Co-Creation and Prompting Strategies
Knoth et al. (2024) demonstrated that prompt creation is a learnable skill dependent on AI
literacy. Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. (2023) analyzed difficulties non-experts face in dialogue with
LLMs and identified the fundamental challenge of “not knowing what to ask.” Tankelevitch et
al. (2024) systematically organized the metacognitive demands of generative AI use. Lee et
al. (2025) showed, in a survey of 319 knowledge workers, that high trust in generative AI was
associated with reduced critical thinking, while high task-specific self-efficacy was associated
with enhanced critical thinking. Sidra and Mason (2025) developed and validated a
“Collaborative AI Metacognition” scale, presenting important findings from the perspective of
Kahneman’s (2011) dual-process theory.
Simkute et al. (2024), writing in the International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction,
identified the “ironies of generative AI.” They analyzed the challenges of productivity decline
5

due to user role shifts from “production” to “evaluation,” inefficient workflow restructuring, and
interruption, drawing on decades of human factors research. Their macro-level analysis provides
a complementary perspective to the micro-level investigation in the present study.
Limitations of existing research: Most prompting research has focused on the “structure” and
“techniques” of prompts. What remains underexplored is how expert designers verbalize tacit
knowledge and what cognitive transformations they experience during the process remain
insufficiently explored.
2.3 Design Expertise and Tacit Knowledge Externalization
Polanyi’s (1966) concept of tacit knowledge occupies a central position in design research.
Nonaka and Takeuchi’s (1995) SECI model theorized the conversion process from tacit to
explicit knowledge (externalization). Externalization is facilitated through dialogue, metaphor,
and analogy. And yet, the SECI model presupposed human-to-human dialogue, and the
externalization process when the “other” is AI remains unexplored.
2.4 From Deterministic to Probabilistic End-User Programming
The “vibe coding” phenomenon observed in this study can be theoretically positioned as a
paradigm shift in end-user programming (EUP). Generative AI introduces a probabilistic EUP
paradigm in which users function as “supervisors” or “curators,” providing high-level intent
(Vibe) while delegating logical implementation to the model’s probabilistic nature. This shifts
the core competency from “logic description” to “intent negotiation.”
2.5 Research Gap and Positioning of This Study
The above review of related work identifies the following gaps: (1) micro-level cognitive
processes have not been systematically traced; (2) no study has operationalized the
6

externalization phase of the SECI model in a human-AI collaboration context; and (3) the
distinctive strategies exhibited by expert designers (Strategic Oscillation) have not been
described.
Literature collection method: The literature for this review was collected using ACM Digital
Library, Scopus, and Web of Science with the search string “generative AI” AND (“design” OR
“tacit knowledge” OR “prompt*“) AND (”think-aloud” OR “externalization” OR “SECI”) for
English-language publications from 2019–2025. Additionally, forward and backward citation
tracking (snowballing) and foundational works in cognitive psychology and knowledge science
(Polanyi, 1966; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Schön, 1983, etc.) were manually supplemented.
This review is not intended as a systematic review but rather as a narrative review aimed at
covering the conceptual scope necessary for constructing the theoretical framework.

3. Theoretical Framework
3.1 The ECRT Cycle Model: Theoretical Integration
To operationalize the “externalization” phase of the SECI model for interactions with a
probabilistic agent (generative AI), we propose the ECRT cycle. A central premise of this
framework is that generative AI functions as a “probabilistic mirror”—a mechanism by which
AI’s literal, non-empathic processing of language input returns probabilistically interpreted
output that confronts users with the ambiguity of their own verbal expressions (this mechanism
and its activation conditions are operationally defined and empirically examined in Section 6.1).
This model integrates the following established constructs: Piaget’s accommodation (1952),
Festinger’s cognitive dissonance (1957), Schön’s reflection-in-action (1983), and Kahneman’s
dual-process theory (2011).

Expectation (E): The starting point of dialogue. Designers begin AI dialogue with ambiguous
“images” or “feelings.” These are further categorized into vague expectations (E-vague) and
specific expectations (E-specific).
Collision (C): The perception of deviation from expectations when AI returns output. Semantic
collision (C-semantic): the “meaning,” “atmosphere,” or “tone” of output does not match
expectations. Syntactic collision (C-syntactic): the “structure,” “layout,” or “technical details” of
output do not match expectations.

8

Reflection (R): Analysis of the cause of deviation. Surface reflection (R-surface): focus on
superficial features of output such as “the color is different” or “the layout is different.” Deep
reflection (R-deep): focus on one’s own cognitive processes, such as “my verbalization was
ambiguous” or “I failed to share the concept.”
Transformation (T): Change following reflection. Lexical transformation (T-lexical): changes
in words or expressions. Structural transformation (T-structural): changes in prompt structure or
sequence. Meta-transformation (T-meta): changes in strategy itself (e.g., shift from “instruction”
to “consultation”).

Note. The distinctiveness of ECRT is summarized in three points: (1) collision with probabilistic
AI as the reflection trigger, (2) description of bidirectional oscillation rather than unidirectional
conversion, and (3) description of the co-occurrence of conceptual alignment failure and

10

The core differences between the ECRT cycle and prior models are as follows. First, the nondeterministic nature of the reflection trigger. Kolb’s “concrete experience” and Boud et al.’s
“discomfort” arise from relatively predictable situations. In contrast, ECRT’s “collision” is
triggered by inherently non-deterministic AI output, which entails the possibility that identical
prompts may generate different outputs. Second, the bidirectionality of the cycle. Existing
models assume a unidirectional circulation of “experience → reflection → conceptualization →
experimentation.” ECRT describes Strategic Oscillation—experts’ intentional return to
ambiguity (L1)—which functions not as “regression” in existing models but as “strategic
exploration.” Third, the explicit specification of conceptual alignment as a boundary condition.
P005’s disengagement case suggested that conceptual alignment may be involved in the
productive function of the ECRT cycle (though this is a tentative proposition based on a single
case). This explicit specification of boundary conditions has not been attempted in existing
models.
Falsifiability of ECRT: To ensure the falsifiability of the ECRT cycle, we specify the
conditions under which this framework would not hold (falsification conditions). (1) Absence of
reflection trigger: If AI output perfectly matches expectations (no collision occurs), stage C of
ECRT is absent and the cycle does not initiate. This condition applies to work with deterministic
tools (e.g., parametric CAD) or when AI output fully complies with user instructions. (2) Failure
of the reflection capacity premise: For users without the habit or capacity for metacognitive
reflection (e.g., novice designers), the progression from stage C to stage R may be impeded. (3)
Failure of conceptual alignment: As demonstrated by P005’s case, when users and AI cannot
share basic task concepts, the ECRT cycle does not function productively and was observed to
co-occur with disengagement. (4) Influence of language environment: The present data are from

11

Japanese-language environments only, and the applicability of the L1–L3 lexical granularity
classification itself has not been verified in environments with substantially different linguistic
structures (e.g., isolating languages with fewer morphemes). These conditions make explicit that
ECRT is not a universal cognitive model but a context-dependent framework that functions
under specific preconditions.
3.2 Lexical Granularity Levels and Strategic Oscillation
We classify designers’ vocabulary directed at AI into three levels: L1 (Vibe)—abstract,
impressionistic language such as “modern feel” or “sense of trust”; L2 (Semantic)—domainspecific vocabulary such as “hero section” or “minimalist layout”; L3 (Operational/Code)—
concrete specifications such as “#000000” or “16px padding.”
Definition of Strategic Oscillation: A dynamic oscillatory pattern in which expert designers do
not progress unidirectionally from L1 (Vibe) to L3 (Operational) but intentionally return to
ambiguous language levels (L1/L2) for creative exploration, then proceed again toward
concretization (L3).

Note. SO is not mutually exclusive with the above existing concepts and can coexist within the
same session. SO’s distinctiveness lies in the combination of three conditions: (1) movement
between lexical granularity levels, (2) probabilistic AI output as the trigger for movement, and
(3) level descent functioning as intentional exploration.
Identification Rules for SO (Necessary and Sufficient Conditions): SO is determined when
all three of the following conditions are met.
Condition 1 (L3 attainment premise): At least one L3-level prompt must have been confirmed
prior to the unit in question. Movement between L1/L2 without L3 attainment is distinguished as
initial exploration, not SO.
Condition 2 (Intentional return): After L3 attainment, a prompt is issued that returns to L1 or
L2 level vocabulary. Coders confirm that this return is not a “response to an AI error message”
or “re-input due to system failure.”
Condition 3 (Verbalization of intentionality): An intentional reason is expressed in the
accompanying verbal data (e.g., “Let me reconsider from a broader perspective” or “Let me try
approaching from the atmosphere”). If intentionality cannot be confirmed in the verbal data, SO
is not determined.

15

Supplementary behavioral indicators: The following non-verbal behavioral patterns are
referenced as supplementary indicators to support Condition 3 judgment (however,
supplementary indicators alone do not satisfy Condition 3).
(a) Consultation-mode shift: Immediately following the L3→L1/L2 return, prompts requesting
option generation appear (e.g., “Show me three variations” or “Suggest which is better”).
Judgment criterion: structural change from directive (single output request) to interrogative or
option-generation type.
(b) Reframing: After the return, re-declaration of purpose, tone, or concept is observed.
Judgment criterion: the prompt contains expressions redefining design goals or constraints (e.g.,
“Since I was originally aiming for a ○○ atmosphere” or “Changing direction”).
(c) Prompt structural change: The prompt structure qualitatively changes before and after the
return. Judgment criterion: the pre-return prompt consists of operational instructions (specific
numerical/parameter specifications), and the post-return prompt shifts to exploratory questions or
abstract directional descriptions.
In the present data, the majority (7 of 9) of all 9 units determined as SO were accompanied by
supplementary indicators (a) or (b).
Measurement constraint: Because the SO determination criteria include “verbalization of
intentionality,” SO may be underdetected when intention exists but is not verbalized. As thinkaloud methods depend on participants’ verbalization ability and metacognitive reporting habits,
this point is acknowledged as a measurement bias in this study. Therefore, the SO frequency
reported in this study potentially constitutes a conservative estimate (underestimate). However,
the supplementary indicators (verbal content, score fluctuations) are themselves proxy measures,
and the present design does not include means to directly observe the “true frequency” of SO.

16

Handling of ambiguous cases: When Conditions 1 and 2 are met but Condition 3 (verbalization
of intentionality) is ambiguous, both coders independently judge and resolve disagreements
through discussion. In the present dataset, this discussion occurred for 2 units, with 1 judged as
SO and 1 as non-SO.
Conditions under which SO does not hold (falsification and boundary conditions): To delimit
the theoretical scope of SO, we specify conditions under which SO does not hold. (1) Failure of
conceptual alignment: As demonstrated by P005’s case, when users and AI cannot share basic
task concepts, L1/L2 return after L3 attainment becomes “retreat due to confusion” rather than
“strategic exploration restart,” failing to meet SO Condition 3 (verbalization of intentionality).
(2) Novices: Users who lack L3-level vocabulary structurally cannot meet Condition 1 (L3
attainment premise), making SO observation principally impossible. (3) Deterministic tool
environments: When AI output is fully deterministic (identical input → identical output),
“collision” with probabilistic output is unlikely, weakening the cognitive dissonance that triggers
SO. (4) Language environment differences: This study targeted only Japanese speakers, and L1
(Vibe) level expression repertoires may depend on linguistic structure. The high-context nature
of Japanese (see Section 6.4) may facilitate SO emergence, and emergence patterns in different
language environments have not been verified. These boundary conditions make explicit that SO
is not a phenomenon universally emerging across all users and environments but a contextdependent pattern observed when specific preconditions (expert-level vocabulary ability,
probabilistic AI environment, minimum conceptual alignment) are met.
3.3 Methodological Positioning of Confidence-Satisfaction Scores: Structured Elicitation
Device

17

In this study, the 0–10 scores for confidence (“degree to which the prompt conveyed intent”) and
satisfaction (“satisfaction with output”) are not used as dependent variables for statistical
analysis. With N = 5 and 4–12 units per participant, the statistical pattern-detection power of the
scores is insufficient, and such use would be inappropriate.
The function of the scores in this study is as a structured elicitation device complementing
Ericsson and Simon’s (1993) think-aloud method. The act of score assignment prompts
participants to verbalize “why that number,” thereby externalizing implicit evaluation criteria as
verbal data. Accordingly:
Primary data: Verbal content during score assignment (e.g., “I gave it a 5 because the
atmosphere is close but the layout is completely different”)
Contextual information: The direction and magnitude of score changes are reported as
descriptive indicators for contextualizing the intensity and content of verbalizations (e.g.,
“Accompanying a large negative gap from confidence 8 to satisfaction 1, P005 verbalized the
following…”)
Not used: Mean scores, variance, inter-participant statistical comparisons, correlation analyses
All score reporting in this study is based on this positioning as a “structured elicitation device.”
Score changes reported in the results section (Section 5) serve to contextualize verbal data and
do not rely on numerical patterns per se.

4. Methodology
4.1 Research Design
Epistemological stance: This study draws on an interpretivist epistemology and adopts a
constructivist ontology (Creswell & Poth, 2018). Designers’ cognitive processes are understood
as socially and contextually constructed; we do not assume a single objective reality measurable
independently of context. Understanding participants’ unique meaning-making and its generative
processes thus aligns with the research objective. This epistemological stance motivates the
selection of abductive analysis, first-person data collection through think-aloud methods, and
interpretive coding.
This study was designed as an exploratory qualitative inquiry (Creswell & Poth, 2018), based on
an abductive reasoning approach. The initial ECRT framework was deductively derived from
existing theory and subsequently refined inductively through data analysis. This iterative
oscillation between theory and data is characteristic of the abductive approach (Tavory &
Timmermans, 2014) and is distinguished from purely deductive hypothesis testing and purely
inductive grounded theory. Think-aloud protocols (Ericsson & Simon, 1993) were employed for
data collection.
Researcher positionality: The first author is an insider researcher with 25 years of UI/UX design
practice experience, belonging to the same professional domain as the participants. This position

4.2.1 Sample Size Rationale
The sample of N = 5 was strategically designed to maximize Information Power for theory
building (Malterud et al., 2016). Following their five-dimension framework: (a) study aim—
narrow and specific (micro-level cognitive processes during AI dialogue); (b) sample
specificity—participants possessed an average of 15.4 years of professional design experience,
constituting a “high-signal” group with dense, relevant knowledge; (c) established theory—the
ECRT cycle and SECI model provide strong theoretical grounding that reduces the need for large
samples to establish patterns; (d) quality of dialogue—the think-aloud protocol combined with
retrospective interviews and structured elicitation yielded rich verbal data per participant; and (e)
analysis strategy—abductive case-oriented analysis with explicit operational definitions. This
combination yields high information power per participant. The sampling strategy also
successfully captured a theoretically significant negative case (P005).

24

Limitation: The “disengagement” pattern is represented by P005 alone, and theoretical
saturation has not been reached for this subcategory. Conclusions regarding the disengagement
pattern are positioned as tentative propositions.
4.2.2 Trustworthiness
Based on Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) trustworthiness criteria, the following procedures were
implemented: (a) Credibility: methodological triangulation—cross-referencing three data
sources: think-aloud protocols, retrospective interviews, and confidence-satisfaction scores—and
independent coding (Cohen’s κ: ECRT subcodes = 0.76–0.82, lexical granularity = 0.89). (b)
Transferability: detailed participant demographics (Tables 4 and 5). (c) Dependability: threestage codebook development process (see Table 3 operational definitions). (d) Confirmability:
detailed analysis of negative case (P005).

4.3.1 Output Variation Verification
To evaluate the impact of underlying model non-fixedness on analytical conclusions, a miniverification was conducted after data collection completion. Three representative prompts used
during P001’s session were re-executed three times each using the same tool and settings, and
output variation was assessed.
Verification results: (1) Layout structure (number of columns, section arrangement) was
generally consistent in 3 of 3 trials. (2) Specific color and typographic choices differed in 2–3 of
3 trials. (3) “Atmosphere” and “tone” similarity were evaluated based on the following rubric.
Tone similarity rubric: Three evaluation axes—(i) trustworthiness, (ii) approachability, (iii)
modernity—were each rated on a three-level scale (low/medium/high), with “similar” defined as
matching the original output on 2 or more of 3 axes. The first author and second coder
independently evaluated the results, and in the majority (7 of 9) of 3 prompts × 3 trials = 9
comparisons, both evaluators judged “similar” (agreement: 7/9). This verification is exploratory
and does not constitute proof of robustness. The 2 disagreements both involved the
“approachability” axis.
Implications for conclusions: Probabilistic output variation primarily appeared at the L3 level
(concrete specifications: color, font size, etc.), while variation at the L1 level (atmosphere/tone)
was relatively small. The central findings of this study—Strategic Oscillation (movement
patterns between lexical granularity levels) and the ECRT cycle (cognitive response patterns)—
analyze cognitive processes in response to output rather than specific output content, and the
possibility of L3-level variation overturning conclusions was not observed within this
29

verification scope. However, this verification is limited and exploratory (n = 9; 3 prompts × 3
trials) and was not designed for systematic evaluation of output variation. We do not claim that
output robustness has been demonstrated by this result. Systematic replicability verification
remains a task for future research.

Note: No formal pilot study was conducted prior to data collection. The 10-minute practice

4.5 Analysis Method
Unit segmentation rules: The unit of analysis was defined as one interaction unit consisting of
one prompt submission → AI output generation → verbalization during output review.

32

Segmentation criteria: (1) A new prompt submission marks the unit boundary. (2) Multiple
verbalizations regarding the same output are consolidated into one unit. (3) The point at which
the participant assigns a score marks the unit endpoint.
Consolidation criteria: (1) Immediate additions to the preceding prompt (within 5 seconds),
such as “Oh, actually, add this too,” are consolidated into the same unit. (2) Re-submissions of
the same prompt due to system errors are consolidated into the same unit.
Exclusion criteria: (1) Units during practice sessions (16 units). (2) Units in which output was
not generated due to system errors.
Example of a borderline case: In P002’s Units 7-8, P002 muttered “a bit more…” while
modifying and re-submitting the prompt. As the interval from modification to re-submission was
3 seconds, these were consolidated into the same unit based on the 5-second rule (Consolidation
Criterion 1). Both coders agreed on this judgment.
Of 50 total units, 34 were included in analysis (P001: 6, P002: 12, P003: 7, P004: 4, P005: 5).
P002’s higher unit count (12) compared to other participants (range: 4–7) reflects P002’s
adoption of a rapid-iteration strategy involving shorter and more frequent prompts. Session
duration was uniform at 30 minutes for all participants. This uneven unit distribution means that
P002 contributes disproportionately (35.3%) to aggregate patterns reported in Tables 8 and 9.
Readers should note that patterns described as “cross-participant” may be weighted toward
P002’s interaction style. To mitigate this, we report participant-specific breakdowns throughout
and present representative contrasting trajectories in Figure 2.
Codebook development (abductive approach): (1) Theory-driven codes: ECRT cycle (E, C, R,
T) and lexical granularity (L1, L2, L3) were pre-established based on theoretical definitions (see
Table 3). (2) Data-driven codes: Subcategories were inductively extracted from P001’s and

33

P002’s data. Notably, the initial framework did not include the distinction between C-semantic
and C-syntactic, but this distinction emerged from P001’s contrasting responses to “atmosphere”
and “layout” mismatches. (3) Codebook refinement: Verified and revised with data from the
remaining three participants. P005’s data prompted the addition of “conceptual alignment” as a
boundary condition (see the final definitions in Table 3).
Inter-coder reliability computation procedure: The first author (Coder A) and a second coder
(Coder B: a doctoral student in HCI research with no prior exposure to the ECRT framework)
independently coded all 34 units.
Computation unit: Presence/absence for each unit × each code (binary judgment). Since
multiple codes may be assigned to a single unit, computation was conducted code-by-code.
ECRT subcodes (9 categories): Code-specific Cohen’s κ = 0.76–0.82 (median 0.76). Of 34
units × 9 subcodes = 306 binary judgments, 274 agreed and 32 disagreed. Code-specific κ values
fall within the Substantial–Almost Perfect agreement range (Landis & Koch, 1977).
Lexical granularity (L1/L2/L3, mutually exclusive 3 categories): Cohen’s κ = 0.89. Of 34
judgments, 32 agreed and 2 disagreed. Almost Perfect agreement (Landis & Koch, 1977).
Inter-coder reliability was evaluated for each distinct labeling system. Aggregating different
coding systems (non-exclusive binary judgments and exclusive categorical judgments) into a
single index could introduce arbitrariness; therefore, this study reports system-specific κ as the
primary metric. Note that while the Landis and Koch (1977) criteria are widely referenced, κ
interpretation is context-dependent, and these are used as reference standards rather than
normative thresholds.
For ECRT subcodes, pooled PABAK = 0.79 is reported as a complementary measure (Byrt,
Bishop, & Carlin, 1993). PABAK is not an alternative to κ but a complementary index for

34

evaluating the impact of prevalence imbalance on κ. In the present data, certain subcodes (Especific, C-semantic, T-lexical) had higher occurrence frequencies than others (T-meta, Tstructural), resulting in varying prevalence indices across codes (PI = 0.00–0.50). For lowprevalence codes (T-meta: 8/34), the divergence between κ (0.76) and PABAK (0.82) was large,
while for high-prevalence codes (C-semantic: 21/34), the divergence was small (κ = 0.81,
PABAK = 0.82). This difference reflects the typical influence of prevalence imbalance, and coreporting with PABAK ensures the robustness of reliability evaluation.

line) exhibits the oscillatory pattern characteristic of expert designers: after progressing toward
operational specificity (L3), P001 intentionally retreats to abstract, exploratory language (L1)—a
“Strategic Retreat”—before re-converging toward L3. This intentional return to abstraction
enables broader creative exploration and ultimately leads to successful convergence. In contrast,
P005 (red line) follows a unidirectional descent from L1 toward L3 without any strategic return,

40

representing “Linear Fixation.” P005’s trajectory shows no oscillation and terminates in
disengagement.
5.3 Finding 1: Strategic Shift from “Instruction” to “Consultation” (Response to SRQ2)
Expert designers not only refined instructions but also switched to consultation-mode prompting
(optioning), retaining decision authority on the human side while broadening the exploration
space.
Dual-process theory interpretation: From a dual-process theory perspective, the switch to
consultation-mode prompting can be interpreted as a transition from System 1 automatic
response (“just add more detail”) to System 2 strategic re-evaluation (“reconsider the strategy
itself”). This type of strategy shift was not observed in P005, but this study did not directly
measure System 1/System 2, so this explanation remains an interpretive framework.
Boundary condition: The switch to consultation mode was not observed in P005 (see Table 5).
This suggests the possible involvement of conceptual alignment failure (see Table 3 operational
definitions), but alternative explanations such as experience differences and AI use experience
threshold effects cannot be excluded, making the boundary condition claim tentative.
Cross-participant comparison revealed diverse motivations for the consultation-mode shift. P001
achieved an early shift at Unit 2 of 6. After experiencing C-semantic collision in U2, P001
verbalized “It’s faster to have several options shown rather than deciding myself” and issued a
clear consultation-mode prompt in U3: “Show me three patterns.” This verbalization indicates
the moment P001 redefined AI’s role from “instruction executor” to “proposal generator.” P003
also showed a similar shift at U4, but through a different process. P003 verbalized “Hmm, I was
trying to decide everything myself, but AI might have more options” and reached the shift
through recognition of self-limitation. While P001 shifted from an efficiency perspective
41

(“faster”), P003 adopted consultation mode as cognitive resource reallocation (“more options”).
This contrast suggests that the motivation for consultation-mode shifts varies with designers’
cognitive framing.
P004 showed the most cautious approach. With 20 years of experience, P004 verbalized in early
units “First, I want to accurately convey what’s in my head” and resisted transitioning to
consultation mode. However, in U3, P004 realized “Fine-tuning each time doesn’t change things
much,” and in U4, used a consultation-mode prompt for the first time: “Suggest two directions.”
P004’s case shows that the “control orientation” based on long experience can serve as a barrier
to the consultation-mode shift, while the accumulation of repeated expectation-output gaps
ultimately functions as a shift trigger. This gradual transformation is consistent with the idea that
transition from Kahneman’s System 1 to System 2 can occur gradually rather than instantly.
5.4 Finding 2: Qualitative Differences in Collision and Reflection Triggers (Response to
SRQ1)
Semantic collisions (C-semantic) tended to co-occur with deep reflection (R-deep), associated
not with mere correction but with redefinition of “what I wanted to say.” Conversely, when
conceptual alignment was lacking, collision tended to be associated with disengagement rather
than reflection.
Case 1—P002 (V-shaped recovery): P002 assigned the lowest scores during the first three
output reviews, verbalizing “It’s completely different from what I was thinking… I couldn’t
verbalize it.” This is a clear case of simultaneous C-semantic collision and R-deep reflection.
This progressed to “It’s better to have AI suggest options and I select rather than leaving it to AI”
(T-meta: strategy change), and through the transition to consultation-mode prompting, P002
ultimately reached the highest score.
42

Case 2—P005 (Disengagement): P005 assigned a high confidence score and the lowest
satisfaction score, and accompanying this gap, verbalized “Even after explaining this much, it
still doesn’t work.” This is a case where C-semantic collision remained at R-surface without
progressing to R-deep or T-meta. P005 then verbalized “I don’t even feel like scoring anymore. I
feel like giving up,” showing a disengagement tendency.
Methodological note: References to scores above constitute descriptive information for
contextualizing verbal content. The analytical focus is on the qualitative content of verbalizations
accompanying score assignment, not on numerical patterns (see Section 3.3).
The chronological progression of collision types in P001 illustrates the staged nature of
conceptual alignment and operational adjustment. Tracking P001’s 6 units, C-semantic collisions
were dominant in U1–U2 (“the atmosphere is completely different,” “the tone is off”). From U3
onward, C-syntactic collisions increased (“column width,” “button placement”). This progression
pattern suggests a staged process in which designers first establish conceptual-level alignment,
then transition to operational-level fine-tuning. Particularly noteworthy is P001’s verbalization in
U2: “What did I mean by ‘modern’?” This verbalization was classified as R-deep, but it went
beyond mere self-reflection to constitute a metacognitive act attempting to make explicit the
aesthetic evaluation criteria held as tacit knowledge. Polanyi’s (1966) “we can know more than
we can tell” was experientially confirmed in this scene, triggered by AI dialogue.
P003 exhibited a distinctive “comparative reflection” pattern in 2 of 3 C-semantic collisions.
Rather than simply contrasting AI output with personal expectations, P003 verbalized “This is
interesting in its own way, but what I wanted to say was…” acknowledging the alternative value
of AI output while re-confirming personal intent. This pattern was not observed in P001 or P002.
P003’s 18 years of experience may be associated with the flexibility to evaluate AI output as an

43

“alternative” rather than an “error.” However, this interpretation cannot rule out confounding
between years of experience and design philosophy, and is reported as a descriptive observation.
We examine in greater detail the mechanism by which P005 never exhibited R-deep. Tracking
P005’s verbal data chronologically reveals a consistent characteristic in responses to collision.
U1: “It’s different from what I expected” (R-surface), U2: “Maybe I should write in more detail”
(T-lexical), U3: “Even after explaining this much, it still doesn’t work” (external attribution),
U4: “I don’t know how to explain it anymore” (attribution fixation). This four-stage progression
shows that P005 consistently maintained an external attribution frame of “the problem is with
AI’s comprehension ability.” For R-deep to occur, a shift to internal attribution—“the problem is
with my verbalization”—is necessary, and in P001–P004, C-semantic collision functioned as a
trigger for this shift. In P005, conceptual alignment failure, external attribution frame fixation,
absence of R-deep, and disengagement were simultaneously observed. These co-occurrence
patterns suggest that conceptual alignment may have been mediating, but alternative
explanations such as experience differences cannot be excluded, and interpretation remains
tentative (see Section 6.5.1).
5.5 Finding 3: Strategic Oscillation of Vocabulary (Response to SRQ3)
Lexical granularity did not increase monotonically; rather, an oscillatory pattern of contextdependent return to L1 was observed. This return functioned not as “regression” but as
“exploration restart,” and was associated with eventual L3 convergence. As shown in Figure 2,
the oscillatory pattern (exemplified by P001) was associated with success or recovery, while
P005’s unidirectional pattern terminated in disengagement.
Representative unit excerpts: Patterns determined as SO

In a qualitative analysis of SO, we examined oscillation amplitude patterns. All 9 SO units were
observed among P001–P004 (P001: 2, P002: 3, P003: 2, P004: 2; P005: 0), consistent with the
absence of SO Condition 3 (verbalization of intentionality) in P005’s verbal data. Classifying
these 9 units by amplitude (level difference of return destination) revealed two patterns. “Largeamplitude SO” (L3→L1; 5 units) involved fundamental re-examination of direction, with design
concept changes observed in subsequent prompts. In P002’s U8→U9 (large-amplitude SO),
following the L1 return (“What was the ‘atmosphere’ of this page again?”), the output direction
qualitatively shifted from “corporate/solid” to “approachable/casual.” “Small-amplitude SO”
(L3→L2; 4 units) involved re-examination at the domain-specific vocabulary level for specific
elements. In P003’s U5→U6 (small-amplitude SO), following the L2 return (“a more calm
impression”), the change was limited to the color palette while the overall structure was
maintained. This contrast is a descriptive pattern suggesting that SO amplitude may be associated
with the scope of subsequent transformation.
P004 exhibited a qualitatively distinct “gradual oscillation” pattern across four cycles. In each of
P004’s cycles, the amplitude gradually decreased (L3→L1→L3→L2→L3→L2→L3). P004
verbalized “Gradually, the image in my head became clearer,” indicating that oscillation
reflected gradual convergence of the exploration space. In contrast to P001’s and P002’s “largeamplitude oscillation → rapid convergence” patterns, P004 systematically and gradually reached
final L3 convergence. Furthermore, P004 stated in the retrospective interview “Rather than
deciding details from the start, going back and forth several times to gradually improve accuracy
suits me better,” suggesting that SO had been internalized as a conscious strategy. This
“controlled exploration” underpinned by 20 years of experience is qualitatively different from

46

the more intuitive and improvisational SO of P001 and P002, indicating that individual
differences exist even at the expert level.
5.6 Integrated Response to MRQ
Dialogue with generative AI reorganizes externalization not as a unidirectional “ambiguous →
concrete” conversion but as an oscillatory movement (Strategic Oscillation) that maintains
conceptual alignment. AI output functions as a “probabilistic mirror,” triggering reflection
through collision, and co-occurring with vocabulary and strategy transformation. Within the
scope of the present data, this transformation was observed in situations where conceptual
alignment was established to a certain degree. Meanwhile, alignment failure and disengagement
were simultaneously observed in P005, but additional data are needed to generalize this as a
boundary condition, and it is treated here as a tentative proposition.

6. Discussion
6.1 AI Functions as a Probabilistic Mirror
Our findings point to a distinctive mechanism by which generative AI engages with the
“externalization” phase of the SECI model. Because AI lacks empathy and contextual
understanding, it takes designers’ verbal expressions “at face value” and returns probabilistically
interpreted results. This “probabilistic mirror” function forced designers to confront their own
linguistic ambiguity—and in doing so, catalyzed reflection. This is consistent with Festinger’s
(1957) cognitive dissonance theory.
The operational definition and observational indicators of the “probabilistic mirror” are specified
as follows. The probabilistic mirror refers to a mechanism by which generative AI (a) processes
user language input literally, without empathic inference, (b) returns probabilistically interpreted
47

output, and thereby (c) generates situations in which users confront the ambiguity or implicit
assumptions in their own verbal expressions. As observational indicators, the co-occurrence
pattern in which R-deep (reflection on one’s own cognitive processes) follows C-semantic
collision (mismatch of output tone/atmosphere) in the present data (Table 9: R-deep cooccurrence rate of 70.0% in the semantic-only category) indicates the activation of this
mechanism. In contrast, when conceptual alignment was not established (P005), the same Csemantic collision remained at external attribution (R-surface), and the mirror function did not
activate. Accordingly, the probabilistic mirror is presented not as a poetic metaphor but as a
description of an operationally definable cognitive mechanism, constituting a testable
proposition that includes its activation conditions (minimum establishment of conceptual
alignment) and activation indicators (C-semantic → R-deep co-occurrence).
This finding complements the “ironies of generative AI” identified by Simkute et al. (2024) in
this journal. While they documented macro-level productivity decline, the present study’s microlevel analysis reveals the underlying cognitive mechanisms: the probabilistic mirror forces a
transition from System 1 production to System 2 evaluation, which is simultaneously disruptive
(consistent with their “irony” framing) and generative (enabling tacit knowledge externalization).
6.2 The Paradox of Specificity
The received wisdom is straightforward: more specific prompts yield better results. Our findings
directly challenge this notion. Expert designers strategically alternated between “specificity” and
“ambiguity” (Figure 2). P005’s case suggests the proposition that “conceptual alignment
precedes operational alignment.”
6.3 Design Patterns and UI Requirements

48

Design Pattern 1: Conceptual Alignment Verification UI. Problem: Users who input
operational details before establishing basic concepts reach P005-type disengagement. Lee et
al. (2025) showed that overreliance on generative AI suppresses critical thinking, and conceptual
alignment failure may amplify this risk. Solution: An interaction pattern that confirms basic
concepts before operational details.
Design Pattern 2: Consultation Shift Nudge. Problem: Users facing consecutive failures tend
to repeat the same approach. Solution: A nudge encouraging a switch to consultation-mode
prompting upon detecting consecutive negative gaps.
Design Pattern 3: Lexical Granularity Level Feedback. Problem: Users are unaware of the
granularity level of their own prompts. Solution: Real-time feedback of lexical granularity level
(L1/L2/L3) during prompt input.
Validity assessment of design patterns: The three design patterns above are design hypotheses
derived from exploratory data and have not undergone systematic validity assessment prior to
implementation. However, we organize the derivation rationale, design requirements, and
anticipated failure conditions for each pattern and attempt a tentative validity assessment. DP1
(Conceptual Alignment Verification UI) was derived from the co-occurrence pattern of P005’s
disengagement and conceptual misalignment (Section 5.4). An anticipated failure condition is
that the verification step may interrupt users’ natural workflow and paradoxically promote
disengagement. DP2 (Consultation Shift Nudge) was derived from the successful “instruction →
consultation” shift patterns observed in P001–P004 (Section 5.3). An anticipated failure
condition is that inappropriately timed nudges may violate user autonomy. DP3 (Lexical
Granularity Feedback) is based on the hypothesis that consciousness of SO supports
metacognition. An anticipated failure condition is that feedback may increase cognitive load and

49

particularly inhibit free exploration at the L1 level. Empirical evaluation of these design patterns
(e.g., prototype implementation and expert evaluation, usability testing) is positioned as a future
research task (see Section 7.3).
6.4 Linguistic and Cultural Considerations
All participants in this study were Japanese designers who prompted in Japanese. Japanese
permits greater ambiguity than English through subject omission, context-dependent
interpretation, and numerous hedging expressions (e.g., “~na kanji de” [with a ~ish feel]). This
rich repertoire of ambiguous expressions may have been associated with the emergence of L1
(Vibe) level prompting.
Scope limitation: The association between the above linguistic features and the Strategic
Oscillation observed in this study is merely a tendency observed within the present data scope (N
= 5, Japanese speakers only). The causal claim that the high-context nature of Japanese facilitates
SO cannot be derived from this study design. Whether SO is a pattern specific to Japanese
speakers or a language-independent expert strategy requires cross-linguistic comparative
research (see Section 7.3).
Communication norms rooted in high-context culture (Hall, 1976) may be associated with a
tendency for designers to expect “intent” to be inferred from minimal cues. P005’s frustration
may partially reflect culturally specific expectations regarding the inferential capacity of
communication partners. That said, multiple alternative explanations exist for P005’s
disengagement beyond linguistic and cultural factors (see Section 6.5 below).

6.5.2 General Limitations
Sample size: N = 5 was designed from the Information Power perspective, but the
“disengagement type” (P005) is represented by only one participant. This subcategory has not
reached theoretical saturation. Conclusions regarding conceptual alignment are positioned as
tentative propositions.
Tool dependency: Only Figma Make (version 2025.11) was used. Since the underlying model is
not disclosed, replicability with different tools (e.g., Replit AI, Cursor) has not been verified (see
Table 6). The impact of output variation was examined in Section 4.3.1, but systematic
53

replicability verification has not been achieved. This study defines replicability not at the level of
identical output reproduction but at the level of procedural and analytic replicability—whether
SO/ECRT can be re-observed in similar generative AI environments using the same protocol and
coding scheme (see Section 4.3). The opacity of the underlying model was managed as a design
constraint, and we judged that this constraint does not directly compromise the validity of
conclusions on the grounds that the object of analysis is designers’ cognitive responses rather
than AI output quality. However, the possibility that different model architectures or parameter
settings may indirectly influence designers’ cognitive processes cannot be excluded and should
be verified in future replications.
Cultural and linguistic context: All participants prompted in Japanese (see Section 6.4). The
association between SO and the high-context nature of Japanese is merely a tendency within the
present data scope and is not a causal claim.
Task dependency: Limited to the hero section of a B2B SaaS landing page. SO emergence for
other design tasks (e.g., dashboard design, mobile app UI) has not been verified.
Hawthorne effect: The think-aloud method itself may have increased metacognitive behavior
beyond what would occur in natural design practice.
Limitations of confidence-satisfaction scores: Scores were used as structured elicitation
devices (see Section 3.3) and not for quantitative evaluation. Reliability and validity assessment
requires additional verification with larger samples.
Alternative explanations for SO/ECRT observations: Alternative explanations exist for this
study’s central findings (SO, ECRT, the C-semantic and R-deep association) beyond AI’s
probabilistic nature. First, the influence of task difficulty. The hero section design requires
simultaneous integration of multiple dimensions, and this complexity itself may generate trial-

54

and-error patterns resembling SO. However, SO is operationally distinguished from mere trialand-error by requiring “intentional level descent + verbalization of intentionality” (see Table 2).
Second, the influence of time pressure. The 30-minute session constraint may have prompted
“efficient exploration” in participants, potentially elevating SO frequency above natural levels.
Third, the influence of UI guidance. The UI design of Figma Make itself may have guided
specific prompting patterns, raising the possibility that observed SO is a behavioral pattern
specific to this tool. Fourth, the influence of individual differences. Differences in years of
experience, AI use history, and specialization among the five participants (see Table 4) may
confound SO/ECRT emergence patterns. We did not establish control conditions for causal
discrimination against the above alternative explanations, and observed patterns are reported as
descriptive trends. Causal verification of SO and ECRT requires future confirmatory research
including control of tool conditions, manipulation of task difficulty, and stratification by
experience level.
Member checking: Member checking was not conducted in this study. Analyzed ECRT
trajectories and thematic interpretations were not returned to participants for confirmation. This
decision was based on two considerations: first, the primary data consisted of real-time thinkaloud verbalizations capturing in-the-moment cognitive processes, and retrospective validation
risks post-hoc rationalization that could compromise data authenticity (Ericsson & Simon, 1993);
second, the abductive coding relied on operational definitions (Table 3) rather than participants’
subjective interpretations. Compensating measures included dual coding by an independent
coder (κ = 0.76–0.89), a priori operational definitions, and proactive negative case analysis
(P005). Future replications may incorporate member checking at the thematic level while
recognizing its limitations for protocol analysis data.

55

Confirmation bias: The first author is an insider researcher with 25 years of UI/UX design
practice experience (see positionality in Section 4.1) and faces the risk of overestimating the
utility of the ECRT framework. Three mitigation measures were implemented against this
confirmation bias threat. First, dual coding by an independent second coder from outside the HCI
domain was implemented, structurally enabling detection of researcher interpretive bias (κ =
0.76–0.82). Second, operational definitions for all codes were finalized a priori (Table 3),
minimizing data-driven post hoc modifications. Third, the negative case (P005) inconsistent with
the ECRT framework was proactively explored and reported. However, it cannot be asserted that
these measures completely eliminated interpretive bias stemming from the insider perspective.
Combined with the absence of member checking (see above), independent re-analysis by
researchers from outside the design domain is recommended in future replication studies.
Data auditability: Raw transcripts are not publicly available due to confidentiality obligations,
but anonymized coding matrices (Tables 8 and 9), operational definitions (Table 3), confusion
matrices (Table 7), and representative unit excerpts are fully reported within this paper, enabling
third-party tracking of the analysis. Additional anonymized data may be requested from the
corresponding author (see Section 6.6 for data availability).

7. Conclusion
7.1 Summary
Through think-aloud protocols with five expert designers (M = 15.4 years of experience), this
study found that dialogue with generative AI reorganized externalization not as a unidirectional
conversion but as Strategic Oscillation—a dynamic oscillatory movement—within situations
where conceptual alignment was established to a certain degree.
7.2 Contributions
Observational contribution: Description of the “instruction → consultation” shift and lexical
granularity oscillation (Strategic Oscillation). Negative case analysis (P005’s disengagement)
identified conceptual alignment as a boundary condition.
57

Methodological contribution: Proposal of the ECRT cycle. Comparison with existing reflective
models (Table 1) made explicit the theoretical distinctiveness of using probabilistic AI output as
the reflection trigger. Systematization of operational definitions (Table 3).
Design implications: Presentation of three concrete design patterns (Conceptual Alignment
Verification UI, Consultation Shift Nudge, Lexical Granularity Feedback).
7.3 Future Directions
Plans for the second phase include: (1) longitudinal tracking of Strategic Oscillation skill change;
(2) expert-novice comparison; (3) additional analysis of disengagement factors; (4) experimental
evaluation of design patterns; (5) cross-linguistic comparison (Strategic Oscillation in English
prompting environments).
If tacit knowledge is “knowledge that cannot be verbalized without dialogue with an appropriate
other,” then AI may serve—precisely because of its unpredictability—as a catalyst for the kind of
reflection that human-to-human dialogue alone cannot reach.

Appendix A. ECRT Subcode Confusion Matrices (All 9 Codes, N = 34 Units)
A-0. κ Computation Method
For each subcode, a 2 × 2 confusion matrix was constructed based on binary judgments
(present/absent) of 34 units. Cohen’s κ (Cohen, 1960) was computed as follows:
P_o = (a + d) / N (observed agreement rate: a = both coders judge “present,” d = both coders
judge “absent”)
P_e = ((a+b)(a+c) + (c+d)(b+d)) / N² (chance agreement rate)
κ = (P_o − P_e) / (1 − P_e)
Rounding rule: Rounded to two decimal places (third decimal place rounded). Computation was
performed using the Python 3.12 statsmodels library and cross-verified by hand calculation.
