Introduction

Digital learning tools such as interactive simulations, languagelearning apps, math learning games, etc. translate instructional
goals into interfaces through which learners encounter content
knowledge, take actions, and receive feedback. Designing these
learner-facing interfaces requires bringing together knowledge
from the learning sciences, interaction design, and the subject domain of instruction. Consider a designer who creates an interface
to help a novice learn fractions. Learning sciences may suggest that
learners benefit from connecting symbolic expressions to visual
representations and maintaining desirable difficulty when receiving
support. Domain expertise is needed to ensure that those representations preserve the mathematical relationships the learner is meant
to understand. The interaction design then determines how these
ideas become concrete, i.e., whether learners manipulate pieces
of a fraction model, enter a numerical answer, receive a hint after
an error, or move between visual and symbolic representations.
Each of these (seemingly small) interface decisions requires reasoning in learning science, domain, and design. More importantly,
these design decisions are consequential and can shape learners’
access, effort, persistence, and engagement. For example, in a largescale study of an online mathematics and science platform, simply
making the option to retry a failed problem more visually significant increased the students’ likelihood of attempting the problem
again [5].
For designers, translating learning goals into interface decisions
can be challenging because there is often a many-to-many mapping
between theories about how people learn and interface designs.
These decisions often involve competing objectives. For example,
providing guidance can help a novice begin a task while minimizing
the opportunity for productive struggle. Simplifying an interface
can reduce extraneous cognitive demands while also removing
work central to the learning objective. On the other hand, increasing
the choice of the learner can support agency while overwhelming
learners who lack sufficient prior knowledge or self-regulation [10,
100]. Learning-interface design is therefore an ill-structured design
problem, with many of the characteristics associated with wicked
design problems [18], i.e., there is rarely a single correct solution,
design goals can conflict, and the appropriateness of a solution
depends on the learner, learning objective, domain, and surrounding
context.
For such design problems, abstract principles and prescriptive
guidelines are not enough. Designers face a multitude of plausible
instructional and interaction choices [63], while learning theories
typically operate at a level of abstraction that leaves open how
they should be implemented in concrete interfaces. Previous work

Lyon, et al.

has sought to make learning theory more actionable through mappings to learning activities and technological affordances, learning
experience design, pedagogical pattern languages, and technologyspecific design guidance [25, 30, 47, 56, 77, 78, 93, 108]. However,
these efforts operate largely above or within particular classes of
technology, minimizing the focus on the recurring mechanisms at
the interface level through which learning principles become concrete in what learners see, do and receive in response. In practice,
designers bridge this gap through combinations of intuition, precedent, prior experience, and subject-matter expertise [21, 118]. The
resulting knowledge of how learning principles become interface
decisions is often embedded in design practice and existing
products rather than explicitly articulated.
Existing learning applications offer a rich source of such knowledge. Across products and domains, designers have developed concrete ways to handle key design issues for learning environments
[28] to structure problems, guide attention, coordinate representations, provide feedback, regulate learner agency, and support
self-monitoring. In this work, we take a bottom-up approach to
examine existing interfaces to surface the design knowledge embedded in their recurring interface mechanisms. We curated 873
screenshots from 65 publicly available learner-facing applications
spanning various subject domains and interaction formats. Following corpus-based approaches to design space analysis and pattern
mining [48, 49, 92], we iteratively analyzed interface artifacts and
identified 27 recurring interface patterns organized under nine
higher-level design commitments. We call this design knowledge
LINK (Linking INstructional Knowledge to interfaces). We use design commitments to describe broader learning-oriented design
goals that shape interface decisions and design patterns to describe
recurring interface mechanisms through which those commitments
are realized. Importantly, we do not treat these patterns as universally effective or “best” designs. Instead, the library makes existing
design choices legible by connecting interface mechanisms with
the learning problems they address, their theoretical rationale, and
the contexts and tensions surrounding their use.
Our study is guided by the following research questions:
RQ1: What recurring interface patterns and higher-level design
commitments characterize learner-facing digital learning
applications?
RQ2: How do designers use and interpret a library of these patterns
when reasoning about learning interface design?
Based on the identified design commitments and patterns, we
conducted a user study with 14 designers to examine how this
intermediate design knowledge enters design practice. Designers
used the pattern library to generate and extend interface ideas,
reconsider emerging designs, and articulate rationales for their decisions. Their use of the library also surfaced challenges in making
design knowledge actionable, including the importance of concrete examples, interpretable terminology, and ways to navigate a
large collection of patterns. Our main contributions are: (1) LINK: a
corpus-grounded synthesis of 27 learning-interface design patterns
organized under nine design commitments, and (2) an evaluation
showing how designers use the pattern library to generate ideas,
reason about decisions, and articulate design rationale.

2 Related Work
2.1 Operationalizing Learning Theories
The learning sciences offer a rich body of theory about how people
learn. Even within computer science education, Szabo et al. identified 75 learning theories that span experiential, cognitive, and
social perspectives [102]. Learning theories in education have been
said to be like toothbrushes: everyone needs one, but no one wants
to use anyone else’s. Previous work has translated broad theoretical traditions into instructional strategies [62] and linked types
of knowledge to appropriate learning processes and instructional
choices [64]. However, we find that educational technologies are
often weakly grounded in explicit theory, including virtual reality
environments [69] and educational games, whose pedagogical assumptions remain implicit in their mechanics [58]. Previous work
has sought to bridge theory and design. For example, Conole et
al. map theoretical positions to e-learning activities [30], while
Jeong and Hmelo-Silver connect collaborative-learning needs to
technological advantages and design strategies [56]. Other work
provides theoretically-informed guidance for particular populations
or technologies [111, 112]. Learning experience design (LXD) integrates human-centered design with theoretically grounded learning goals [53, 93]. Central to LXD is the distinction between a
learner and a generic user [99]: optimizing only for ease of use
can remove challenge, structure, or cognitive activity important for
learning [86, 100].
The remaining challenge for edtech UX designers is translating these principles into concrete interface decisions. Learning
and instructional designers often rely on subject-matter experts,
precedent, and professional judgment more than on codified theory
or data [21, 118], while novices can struggle to expand learning
goals into design moves [23]. HCI has developed forms of intermediate design knowledge, including design principles [13], design
patterns [14], and generative and analytical frameworks [39]. In
educational design, Sandoval’s design conjectures similarly connect
the features designed with the hypothesized learning processes and
outcomes [90, 91]. We build on this tradition by using design commitments to capture higher-level learning rationales that connect
learning concerns to recurring interface mechanisms.

2.2

Interface Design Knowledge for Learning
Technologies

HCI has long treated learners as a distinct user population [99].
Early work translated learning concerns into interface guidance,
including metacognition-based guidelines [57], usability criteria for
e-learning systems [3], and a scaffolding framework for inquiry software [85]. Such work demonstrates the value of connecting learning
activities and interface design, but typically remains anchored in a
particular theory or class of system. More recent work shows that
even small interface choices can be pedagogically consequential. A
large-scale experiment found that a visual retry cue and persuasive
trigger increased persistence after failure [6]; authoring systems
encode pedagogical decisions into interfaces for educational simulations and games [61, 117]; and interface presentation can shape
learners’ perceptions of the learning activity itself [106]. Design

knowledge also emerges through situated practice, including multistakeholder co-design [70] and negotiations between learning and
UX designers [86]. These approaches generate rich knowledge, but
it often remains tied to the systems and settings in which it was
produced.
A smaller body of research aggregates knowledge across systems. Studies have synthesized interaction designs in 62 mobile
augmented-reality learning applications [108], 95 studies of gamebased robotics education [78], and 47 disengagement-support applications [25], while other work has modeled Computer-Supported
Collaborative Learning (CSCL) scripts [35]. These analyses demonstrate the value of recovering recurring design knowledge, but
remain scoped to particular technologies or problem domains. We
extend this approach across a wider range of learner-facing interfaces and examine how recurring mechanisms can be represented
as reusable, learning-oriented design knowledge.

2.3

Design Patterns as a Knowledge
Representation

Design patterns provide an established form for representing reusable
knowledge in context-dependent design spaces. Originating in architecture [2], patterns later moved through software engineering [45] into HCI [15]. Unlike simple guidelines, patterns preserve
a recurring problem, a possible solution, and the context, forces,
and rationale surrounding its use [110]; pattern catalogs have consequently become practical design resources [105]. Patterns therefore
narrow a design space without implying that one solution is universally correct. Education research has developed a parallel pattern
tradition. The Pedagogical Patterns Project codified experienced
teachers’ practices [97]; Goodyear positioned patterns between
abstract theory and overly prescriptive guidance [47]; and Mor
and Winters proposed them as a means of accumulating design
knowledge in technology-enhanced learning [77]. These patterns
primarily describe pedagogical activities, social arrangements, and
learning structures, whereas conventional HCI patterns describe
interface solutions without their learning rationale or associated social context, such as the specification of participant roles. Interfacelevel patterns that preserve both the mechanism and its learning
rationale remain comparatively underdeveloped as contributions
to learning environment and UX design.
Corpus-based design analyses offer a way to recover such intermediate knowledge from existing artifacts. In data visualization, Grammel et al. synthesized approaches to visualization construction and their tradeoffs [48], while Satyanarayan et al. compared visualization-authoring systems to expose recurring design
choices [92]. However, observing a recurring pattern does not establish its effectiveness; instead, patterns make existing mechanisms,
rationales, and tradeoffs available for dialogue and critical examination. Dearden and Finlay found limited evidence that patterns
improve design outcomes and cautioned against adopting their form
without their underlying values [32]; Fang et al. similarly note that
HCI often produces frameworks faster than it evaluates them [39].
We respond by grounding patterns in recurring instances across
independent systems and by examining how designers interpret
and use the resulting library in an evaluation study.

3

Method

We used a corpus-based, bottom-up approach to surface recurring
design knowledge embedded in existing learning interfaces. Our
method combined qualitative design pattern mining with iterative,
abductive analysis of interface artifacts. We began with observable interface mechanisms and learner interactions while drawing
on learning-science concepts to interpret the learning problems
and rationales which those mechanisms appeared to address. This
approach is based on prior design-space analyses of existing systems [48, 66, 92], pattern taxonomies derived from the corpus of
deployed interfaces [49], and content analyses of educational applications [20]. The analysis produced an initial set of patterns that
we subsequently refined through independent reorganizing and an
evaluation study with designers (Section 5).

3.1

Researcher Positionality

Our analysis is shaped by the combined expertise of the research
team in learning sciences, human-computer interaction, and learning technology design. Team members have worked in academic,
nonprofit, and commercial educational-technology settings, and
three have taught graduate-level courses in learning technology design that explicitly train students to translate learning theories into
design artifacts. This combination of learning-science, HCI, and design expertise helped us interpret how interface mechanisms relate
to learning goals and theoretical principles. At the same time, it
may have made patterns aligned with theories and design practices
familiar to us more readily visible. We treat the resulting patterns
as interpretive constructions rather than objective features of the
corpus, and we used independent regrouping and team discussion
to surface and challenge these interpretations.

3.2

Corpus Collection

Our goal is to capture a sufficiently broad range of learner-facing interfaces from which recurring design mechanisms can be identified.
We use purposive sampling [81, 82], beginning with widely-used
and publicly-available learning applications across desktop and
mobile, including applications familiar to the authors and highly
ranked applications in the Apple and Google app stores, an approach used in previous educational-application analyses [20]. We
subsequently broadened the corpus using Y Combinator’s educationstartup directory1 , the Accessible Digital Learning Portal2 , and
other collections of learning technologies. As the analysis progressed, we intentionally added applications representing interface
types and learning domains that were underrepresented in the
existing corpus.
We included applications that (1) were primarily learner-facing
and organized around identifiable learning objectives, (2) were publicly accessible through a mobile application or desktop browser,
and (3) provided an English-language interface. We excluded systems primarily intended for teachers or administrators, such as
classroom-management and teacher-authoring tools. We also excluded augmented-reality applications because this design space
has recently been systematically characterized by Tzortzoglou et
al. [108].

Our primary analytic artifacts were interface screenshots [33, 37].
Most screenshots were collected by researchers while walking
through applications and their interactions, following the principles of the walkthrough method [67]. When interfaces were inaccessible due to subscriptions or other access restrictions, we
supplemented these materials with publicly available product previews, promotional screenshots, and examples from the Mobbin
interface-reference site [76]. We distinguish these sources because
promotional and reference materials provide evidence of intended
interface design rather than direct evidence of an application’s
complete interaction flow.
After an initial review and subsequent additions, the resulting
corpus contained 873 screenshots from 65 platforms. We define
a platform as a cohesive digital learning experience organized
around a distinct subject domain, learning objectives, content, tasks,
and interaction patterns. For example, we treated Duolingo Math
and Duolingo Music as separate platforms because they support
different learning objectives through substantially different interfaces. The corpus spans conventional instructional platforms, learning games and simulations, programming environments, and AIsupported learning tools. It was designed for the breadth of interface
mechanisms rather than statistical representativeness.

3.3

Pattern Mining and Analysis

3.3.1 Initial Pattern Identification. We imported the corpus into
a shared Miro workspace that offers a flexible canvas to spatially
organize content and add notes and annotations on one integrated
surface [75]. We organized screenshots into broad interface formats, including conversational tutors, programming environments,
structured-response activities, video-based lessons, simulations,
and games, to assess the breadth of the corpus. Note that these
categories were used only to identify coverage gaps and were not
retained as analytic categories. When a category or interaction
form appeared to be underrepresented, we collected additional
applications and screenshots before continuing the analysis.
We then annotated screenshots for learner actions, interface
mechanisms, the learning problem the mechanism appeared to
address, and possible connections to learning-science concepts.
Our unit of interpretation was not simply a visual component,
but a relationship between a learner problem, an interface mechanism, and its plausible learning rationale. For temporal mechanisms
such as progressively revealing actions or changing representations
across a learning sequence, we examined screenshots in the context of the walkthrough sequence rather than treating an isolated
screen as sufficient evidence. Using affinity diagramming [68, 96],
we grouped annotations that reflected similar interface mechanisms
and learning problems. We iteratively compared candidate groups
between applications, split groups that captured meaningfully different mechanisms, and merged groups that represented variants
of the same underlying design response. This process produced an
initial set of 41 candidate patterns organized into 13 provisional
groups.

grouped patterns under broad categories such as guidance and
agency and, in some cases, arranged them along dimensions of
implementation. Through continued analysis, we found that these

summarize its constituent patterns in terms of three elements: the
interface mechanism, the conditions under which that mechanism
may be useful, and the tension or tradeoff introduced by its use. In
each table, the mechanism is expressed as an interface-level design
move followed by the change it produces in the learner’s activity.

Structure Problem Solving

DESIGN COMMITMENT

DC1: Expose enough of a task’s structure to help learners make progress while preserving the problem-solving
decisions they are meant to practice.
Problem solving requires learners to represent a goal, identify
pertinent information, select actions, and monitor their progress
toward a solution [34, 74, 84, 98]. However, novices may have difficulty identifying productive approaches or structuring unfamiliar
problems [12]. Step decomposition and worked examples can help
students identify goals and strategies for problem solving [7, 22].
Cognitive apprenticeship similarly makes otherwise hidden expert
processes visible by modeling and scaffolding learners through a
process [29].
Across our corpus, interfaces translated these ideas by selectively exposing different parts of a problem’s underlying structure.
The three patterns differ in where that structure is externalized. Decomposition of Steps exposes the intermediate process; Ideal Output
Modeling exposes the desired endpoint while leaving the process
open; and Structured Representational Space embeds domain relationships into the actions that the interface permits. Thus, the
commitment is to decide which aspects of the problem should be
made explicit and which should remain for learners to infer, select,
or construct themselves. Each choice leaves different reasoning
for students to perform themselves. The design tension is between

Support and Expand Learner Actions

DESIGN COMMITMENT

DC2: Calibrate the learner’s action space to their current competence by initially guiding or constraining
action and progressively increasing autonomy as support becomes less necessary.
Learning interfaces often present learners with an action space:
the set of inputs through which they can act on content. Open
or complex action spaces may require subject-matter knowledge,
interface familiarity, and self-regulation that learners have not yet
developed. A learning scaffold is a temporary support that can be removed once the learner has gained sufficient mastery [83, 115]. Scaffolding can enable learners to perform beyond their unaided competence by allowing the system to perform a part of the task [31],
but this support should ultimately contribute to independent performance rather than becoming a permanent crutch [83].

preserve the broader action space, but make a productive subset of
possible actions more salient. Unlocking Actions, in contrast, change
the boundaries of the action space itself by initially withholding

4.3

Align Cognitive Work to Learning

DESIGN COMMITMENT

DC3: Reduce cognitive work that is incidental to the
learning objective while directing attention and effort
toward the cognitive work learners are meant to perform.
During learning, learners engage in cognitive work such as processing information, directing attention, searching, integrating, and
reasoning. Because working memory and attention are limited resources, demands introduced by an interface can compete with
those required by the learning task. Cognitive load theory distinguishes the demands of processing learning materials from the
extraneous load created by their presentation [94, 101]. Mayer and
Moreno’s Spatial Contiguity Principle similarly shows that keeping
related instructional elements physically close reduces unnecessary visual search and integration [72]. Sustained tasks can also
benefit from interventions that redirect attention when it starts to
drift [104].
The current literature framing also connects this commitment
to adjunct questions and interspersed testing. Rothkopf (1970) described the “Mathemagenic Effect of Selective Attention,” in which
questions embedded within a reading passage function as attention guides and shift learners from passive exposure toward active
search [88]. Likewise, this work draws a connection to “progressive
disclosure,” attributed to Van Dam (1988), in which interface capabilities are gradually revealed so that users do not need to process
the full complexity of a system at once [109].

Since learning itself requires cognitive work, these patterns distinguish work that advances the learning objective from work introduced incidentally by the interface or by prerequisite information.
Interfaces can offload some of these processing demands into external representations [26, 27, 94], but cognitive load should not
be minimized indiscriminately. Guiding Visual Attention reduces
incidental search; In-context Informational Support externalizes secondary knowledge at the point of need; and Embedded Questions
and Solutions place prompts or support within the content context
where learners can use them without reconstructing what they
were previously attending to.
Across the three patterns, interfaces take responsibility for different forms of incidental cognitive work: finding relevant information,
retrieving secondary information, or maintaining attention through
an extended activity. However, the same interface move can be productive or counterproductive depending on the learning objective.
An aid is useful when it removes work peripheral to that objective;
it can undermine learning when performing that work is itself what
the learner is intended to practice.

4.4

Connect Multiple Representations

DESIGN COMMITMENT

DC4 Make relationships among representations perceivable and actionable so learners can coordinate, translate,
and transfer knowledge across them.
Multiple representations, such as text, imagery, symbolic notation, and interactive visualizations, can provide learners with different ways of accessing and storing knowledge. Dual Coding Theory
and Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning motivate combining
verbal and visual representations [71, 80]. Multiple representations
can also provide complementary information or support different
forms of problem solving [1]. However, learners do not necessarily
integrate knowledge across representations merely because they
are presented together [1].

Findings

For this analysis, we will focus on the second activity, as the redesign activity mainly familiarized designers with the patterns and
their application. 13 of the 14 designers selected and implemented
design patterns during their design process. Of these, generative use
ranged from two to ten instances, with a mean of 3.5 and a median
of two. We define generative LINK use as participants explicitly
using LINK to guide design choices. Four participants applied design patterns without explicitly mentioning them. We interpret the
use of unmentioned patterns as evidence that the participants had
already internalized these patterns and that the patterns captured
existing knowledge of learning-design. In particular, the four designers who used patterns implicitly had prior educational design
experience. Lastly, six participants initially misinterpreted or misattributed patterns. We attribute these errors to how the patterns
were presented, as mentioned below.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that the contribution of a pattern library for
learning interfaces is to make recurring forms of design reasoning visible, not to prescribe which interface elements designers
should use. The library is located at what Höök and Löwgren call
the intermediate level of design knowledge, between the theory of
learning on one side and individual systems on the other [39, 51].
The intermediate level connects the interface mechanisms observed
across existing learning technologies with broader commitments
to learning-related design, preserving the problem, context, and
rationale that the form of the pattern is meant to carry [47, 110].
Our designer study shows how such a representation is taken up
in practice: designers used it to generate and extend interface ideas
and, more consistently, to explain why a decision served a learning goal. Their use of the library also confirms that patterns are
resources for reasoning as a learning designer rather than recipes
for producing effective learning experiences.

Although we present patterns individually, real learning interfaces often instantiate several at once, and these combinations can
create competing commitments. The tensions our participants experienced are familiar from the learning sciences: in-context support
can reduce incidental cognitive work while removing the effortful
processing that produces durable learning [10, 43]; explanatory
feedback can clarify an error while removing the value of another
problem-solving attempt [60]; and customizable learning paths can
increase learner agency while removing the structure upon which
learners with limited prior knowledge rely [59, 100]. Whether a
mechanism helps, therefore, depends on the kind of knowledge
being learned and the learner’s expertise level [64]. In terms of
patterns, these are the forces surrounding a solution [110], and they
are the reason why the library should not be read as a checklist
in which more patterns produce a better design. Its value lies instead in making those forces visible when a designer is choosing a
mechanism.

theories, while interaction design and computer science courses focus on interfaces, interaction techniques, and implementation. However, the translation between them is often overlooked. Students
may understand a principle such as scaffolding or metacognitive
support while having few examples of the interface mechanisms
through which it might be instantiated, or of the tradeoffs those
mechanisms introduce, a difficulty consistent with evidence that
novices struggle to expand learning goals into concrete design
moves [23]. By connecting higher-level design commitments to
recurring interface mechanisms, LINK makes several such translations visible and comparable. It gives students a repertoire of
precedents, which is what experienced practitioners already reason from [21, 118], together with a vocabulary to justify design
decisions in terms of both the learner and the user [93, 99].

6.2

Representing Design Knowledge for Use

Designers’ difficulties with terminology, volume, and examples
point to a design problem in the representation itself. Pattern names
are meant to become a shared vocabulary [2, 15], yet several of
ours had to be decoded before they could be used. Participants
also worked primarily on the specifics of the annotated examples,
sometimes reproducing their surface form while missing the mechanism they illustrated, a fixation effect well documented in design
ideation [54]. Examples are what make a pattern usable, but they

also make its rationale easy to bypass, which is the failure mode
that Dearden and Finlay describe when the pattern form is adopted
without taking on its underlying values [32]. Developing a pattern library is therefore both an interaction design problem and an
authoring one.

6.3

Patterns for Generative and Bespoke
Learning Software

The need for this kind of intermediate design knowledge is likely
to become more vital, as generative AI lowers the cost of producing
interactive software. Learners, teachers, designers, and developers
can increasingly generate small applications, simulations, tutors,
and interfaces for highly specific learning goals rather than relying
only on professionally designed, general-purpose educational platforms. In such settings, a pattern library can provide conceptual
scaffolding for this emerging form of software creation. A person
prompting a generative interface system could, for example, reason
explicitly about whether a task should expose intermediate problem
structure, what information should remain for the learner to retrieve, how feedback should preserve opportunities to retry, or who
should determine the progression of content. Patterns could therefore inform not only manual design practice but also the prompts,
specifications, critiques, or iterative conversations through which
bespoke learning applications are generated.

Design Patterns for Educational Technologies

This possibility also creates a risk. Generative systems make it
easy to reproduce recognizable interface forms without preserving
the learning rationale that made them appropriate in their particular context. A generated “leaderboard,” “hint,” or “worked example”
may look plausible while being poorly aligned with the learner, domain, or intended learning activity. The usefulness of the patterns
in generative workflows therefore lies less in providing a menu of
features to request, and more in making the conditions and tensions
surrounding an interface mechanism explicit for consideration and
warranted disputation. Applicable to our context, Argyris (1996)
[4] distinguished Single-Loop vs. Double-Loop Learning: When
educators (learning designers in our case) merely perform the surface features of an innovation without shifting their mental models,
they engage in single-loop learning (changing tactics to meet an
outward metric). True innovation requires double-loop learning,
where practitioners fundamentally question and understand the
underlying rationale and values of the new model.

6.4

Limitations and Future Work

Our patterns describe recurring mechanisms in existing learning
interfaces; they do not establish that these mechanisms improve
learning. Recurrence is evidence that a design problem and a response appear across systems, not that the response is optimal for
a particular learner, domain, or context. The learning rationales
we associate with the patterns are analytic interpretations rather
than evidence of the intentions of the original designers, closer
to design conjectures that remain to be tested than to established
mappings [91].
The corpus also reflects the applications that we were able to
identify and access. Although we intentionally sampled across domains and interface forms, the corpus is neither exhaustive nor representative of all learning technologies: paid, inaccessible, emerging,
culturally specific, or less widely-distributed systems may contain
mechanisms not captured here. Furthermore, the preponderance of
the learning applications we identified focused on the individual
learner, so the vibrant research field of CSCL (Computer-Supported
Collaborative Learning), with its extensive literature on ’collaborative learning scripts’ [36, 65, 113] is not well-represented in the
screenshots nor, accordingly, the Design Commitments and Design
Patterns. Remedying this CSCL omission deserves its own research
project. Our designer study also examines bounded design activities; it does not demonstrate that the use of patterns leads to better
interfaces or improved learning outcomes.
The library also does not include accessibility as a design commitment. Across the corpus, the interfaces addressed differences
in visual ability, reading ability, proficiency in the interface language, and sensitivity to visual clutter through mechanisms such
as audio descriptions, alternative text for images, language and
reading support, and controls that allow learners to show, hide, or
modify visual information. These mechanisms cross commitments
rather than serve a particular form of learning activity: toggling
graphics alters visual attention and cognitive load, alternative representations change how information is accessed, and language or
reading support can determine whether a learner can participate
in the focal task at all. We chose not to reduce them to a single
pattern or commitment because accessibility is not an optional

feature that can be added to an otherwise complete design. Presentation controls expand access and let learners adapt aspects of an
experience to their needs, but they do not substitute for making the
underlying content, representations, and interactions accessible by
design. Whether accessibility warrants design commitments of its
own, and how each of the present commitments should be realized
for learners with different competencies are questions the library
leaves open.
These limitations motivate several directions for future work:
individual patterns and their combinations can be evaluated in
relation to learner behavior and learning outcomes, CSCL design
commitments and design patterns should be investigated, and future studies can examine how the library is used in longer design
processes and by multidisciplinary teams. Generative and adaptive
interfaces, in particular, may assemble combinations of mechanisms
dynamically rather than committing to a single fixed interface, so
that decisions about when to introduce and fade support [85] are
made by the system during use rather than by a designer in advance.
How design commitments should operate in such systems is an
open question for learning-interface research.

7

Conclusion

Learning interfaces embody designers’ decisions about how learning theory becomes a force shaping what a learner sees and does
in their learning environment, but the reasoning behind those decisions is seldom documented or explicitly taught. Designers make
such decisions by drawing on precedent and experience, and the
knowledge stays within the products. In this paper, we analyzed 873
screenshots from 65 learning applications and identified 27 recurring interface patterns, organized under nine design commitments
that connect each pattern to the learning concern it addresses. We
then observed 14 designers working with the library on two design
tasks. They used the patterns to come up with interface ideas and,
more often, to explain why a given design choice served a specific
learning goal. Their feedback also demonstrated what makes such
a library usable in practice: plain names, a manageable number of
patterns, and examples that carry their rationale with them rather
than standing in for it. Our patterns document what designers currently do; whether these mechanisms actually improve learning,
alone or in combination, remains to be tested. We also see LINK
as a first step toward a pattern language for learning interfaces,
one that would describe not only individual mechanisms but also
how they combine, so that learning theory and design practice can
inform each other more directly, in principled ways.
