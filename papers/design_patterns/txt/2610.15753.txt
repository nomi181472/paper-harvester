The integer and fractional quantum Hall effects reveal that quantized physical responses can take
integer and fractional values, respectively. This naturally raises the question of whether
quantization can extend beyond rational numbers to an irrational value. Here we show that such a
quantized response can emerge from the limiting behavior of a finite, experimentally realizable
geometric network. We develop a method based on infinite geometric structures, in which a sequence
of finite networks rapidly converges toward an infinite limit. Following this approach, we identify a
planar network whose normalized resistance converges to the irrational value √3, and realize a
finite approximation with high-precision geometric control. The measured normalized resistance is

1.735 ± 0.001 ℎ/𝑒𝑒 2 for a fully occupied Landau level in the designed pattern. Our experimental
results extend the concept of quantization beyond rational values and demonstrate that an irrational
quantized response can be approached through a finite physical system.

Quantization of physical observables is a hallmark of quantum phenomena, with particularly
striking manifestations in quantum transport. The integer quantum Hall (IQH) effect in high-quality
two-dimensional electron systems provides a robust quantized Hall conductance, 𝐺𝐺𝑥𝑥𝑥𝑥 = 𝑁𝑁𝑒𝑒2 /ℎ,
where 𝑁𝑁 is an integer1-3. Its robustness against disorder and sample geometry, together with the
underlying chiral edge states and topology, has established quantum Hall systems as a paradigmatic

platform for quantized transport and enabled precision resistance metrology through the von
Klitzing constant 𝑅𝑅K (ℎ/𝑒𝑒2 ). The fractional quantum Hall (FQH) effect further extends quantized
Hall conductance to fractional filling factors4, revealing fractionalized quasiparticles and new forms
of strongly correlated many-body physics, including the possibility of non-Abelian statistics in
certain states5-8. Quantum Hall physics has thus established quantized resistance at integer and,
through many-body effects, at fractional and more generally, rational values. Yet rational and
irrational numbers together exhaust the real numbers, leaving open whether quantization can extend
into the irrational sector. This progression naturally raises a fundamental question: can a quantum
resistance plateau be characterized by an irrational number?
The integer quantization of the IQH effect can be understood from both transport and
topological perspectives2-3. In the Landauer-Büttiker formalism9-10, each perfectly transmitting
chiral edge channel contributes 𝑒𝑒2 /ℎ to the conductance, leading naturally to an integer number

of channels. From the bulk perspective, the Hall conductance is related to the Chern number, an
1

integer-valued topological invariant. The FQH effect extends quantum Hall quantization beyond
this single-particle integer picture through many-body correlations11-14, with the composite-fermion
picture providing an intuitive understanding of a broad class of fractional states and hence rational
quantized values15-17. Thus, the established mechanisms of quantum Hall quantization are
intrinsically connected to integer-valued topology or many-body mechanisms leading to rational
values, but provide no obvious route toward a quantized response characterized by an irrational
value. A fundamentally different mechanism is therefore required to extend quantization beyond the
rational numbers.
Several quantum systems have demonstrated how non-periodic and complex geometric
structures can generate unconventional physical properties. Quasiperiodic and quasicrystalline
systems naturally involve irrational ratios and aperiodic long-range order18, while Hofstadter-type
systems exhibit rich spectral structures associated with irrational flux values19. However, the
presence of irrational structural parameters does not by itself imply a quantized response with an
irrational value. In physical systems, observable properties are often determined not only by
individual elements but also by their collective organization and geometric arrangement. Whether
the geometric organization of quantized elements can produce a quantized response with an
irrational value remains an open question.
In this letter, we develop a method based on infinite geometric structures, in which finite
realizations converge toward an irrational limiting value of the quantized response. Using this
approach, we identify a finite planar structure that is experimentally accessible and realize it with
quantum Hall resistance elements. The measured quantized resistance is consistent with the
predicted limiting value with experimental accuracy.
We consider an infinite two-dimensional network with a periodic structure, as schematically
illustrated in Fig. 1. A crystal lattice provides a natural example of such a structure, in which nodes
are arranged periodically in space and interact with other nodes within a finite range. More generally,
the nodes may represent physical degrees of freedom located at different positions in a twodimensional periodic structure, with local couplings connecting neighboring or nearby nodes. When
these couplings are linear, the response at each node can be expressed as a linear combination of the
inputs at other nodes. The complete set of coupling relations can therefore be represented by
matrices.

We now turn to a specific physical realization of this general framework. We consider the
purely resistive limit, for which 𝜔𝜔 = 0 and 𝐹𝐹𝑝𝑝 reduces to an effective resistance. In this limit, the

response is determined solely by the resistive elements and their network connectivity, providing a
direct physical realization of the mathematical structure described above. For a finite realization of
the network, however, the deviation from the infinite-size limit becomes a crucial issue. We
therefore approximate the infinite periodic structure by a finite network containing a large but finite
number of primitive cells. Let 𝑅𝑅𝐿𝐿 denote the effective resistance of a finite-size network with
characteristic linear size 𝐿𝐿, for example, the side length of a hypercube, and let 𝑅𝑅∞ denote the
corresponding infinite-system value under the limiting procedure. The finite-size error is given by
𝐸𝐸𝐿𝐿 = 𝑅𝑅𝐿𝐿 − 𝑅𝑅∞ (see Supplementary Material, Section B). For a sequence of finite networks

approaching the infinite system with 𝐿𝐿 increases, 𝑅𝑅𝐿𝐿 approaches 𝑅𝑅∞ , and the rate of this
convergence determines how efficiently a finite network can reproduce the response of the infinite
system. The convergence of a finite resistor network toward its infinite-size limit depends on how
the network geometry distributes the current throughout the structure. In a finite network, the
boundaries inevitably modify the current distribution, and their influence decreases as the network

size increases. The rate at which this boundary effect diminishes is therefore determined by the
connectivity and spatial structure of the network. Different network geometries can consequently
exhibit markedly different finite-size convergence, even when they are constructed from the same
resistive elements.

To experimentally realize the irrationally quantized network, we use the quantized resistance
plateaus of the IQH effect and FQH effect in a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) as the basic
building blocks. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, we implement a finite network with 𝐿𝐿 = 4, constrained
by the sample size and the practical difficulty of fabricating a large number of ohmic contacts. The
6

calculated resistance of this finite network differs from the infinite-network limit by only
approximately 4 × 10−6 in relative terms (see Supplementary Material, Table S1), reflecting the
rapid finite-size convergence of the designed structure. This deviation is well below the
experimental uncertainty in this work, indicating that the 4 × 10−6 network provides a satisfactory
realization of the theoretically defined infinite structure. Moreover, the structure of Fig. 3b is
noticeably more difficult to realize.

We characterize a conventional six-terminal Hall bar fabricated on the same sample as a control.
At 300 mK, several well-developed IQH plateaus are observed, as shown in Fig. 4b. This establishes
the accuracy of the measurement system and provides a reference for the network measurements
under the same conditions. We then measure the quantum network. For a fully occupied Landau
level with filing factor one (𝜐𝜐 = 1), the network resistance is 1.735 ± 0.001 ℎ/𝑒𝑒 2 , compared with

the predicted infinite-network value √3 ℎ/𝑒𝑒2 . The measured value is slightly higher than the
theoretical value, as expected because the finite ohmic contact resistance contributes positively to
the measured value. Since the finite-size correction of the 𝐿𝐿 = 4 network is only approximately
4 × 10−6, much smaller than the experimental uncertainty, the observed deviation from the ideal

value is dominated by experimental factors rather than by the finite size of the network.

We further examine the stability of the network quantization by varying the temperature. As
shown in Fig. 4a, well-developed quantized resistance plateaus are observed as a function of
magnetic field at 𝜐𝜐 = 2 . Measurements performed at 300, 600, and 1200 mK show that the
quantized network resistance remains essentially unchanged over this temperature range. This
temperature stability indicates that the observed quantization is robust against thermal variation
within the investigated range.
A more direct and systematic test of the predicted irrational quantization is obtained by
examining the scaling of the resistance with the inverse filling factor. As shown in Fig. 4b, the
conventional integer Hall resistance follows the expected linear dependence on 1/𝜐𝜐 , while the
network resistance for the IQH states follows the same dependence with a slope smaller by a factor

of √3. The network data at 𝜐𝜐 = 1, 2, 3, 4 consistently follow this scaling relation, demonstrating
that the irrational coefficient is a systematic consequence of the network structure rather than an
isolated feature of a particular quantum Hall plateau. The agreement across multiple integer filling
factors provides a quantitative verification of the predicted irrational scaling.
We also investigate the network using the FQH states at 𝜐𝜐 = 4/3, 5/3. The corresponding Hall

resistances remain consistent with the same 1/𝜐𝜐 scaling established by the IQH states, indicating
that the fractional states themselves are quantized with acceptable experimental uncertainty. In
contrast, the network resistances at these fractional filling factors deviate from the √3 scaling
established by the integer states beyond the experimental uncertainty (see Supplementary Material,
Section C and Fig. S1). At temperatures around 60 mK, the finite contact resistance and reduced
contact quality may have a stronger influence on FQH transport and edge-state equilibration.
Alternatively, the deviation may reflect additional transport mechanisms associated with FQH states
that are not captured by treating them as ideal circuit elements.

In this work, we have established a route toward irrational quantization based on the limiting
behavior of geometric networks. The central idea is to encode the target value in an infinite
geometric structure and to approximate this limit with a finite network whose response converges
rapidly as the system size increases. This connects an infinite mathematical construction to an
8

experimentally realizable finite system.
Although different structures and configurations can approach the same limit, their finite-size
errors can exhibit distinct behaviors. The asymptotic behavior of the network response near 𝒌𝒌 = 0
effectively governs the rate of error decay. Thus, the rate of convergence can be optimized through
appropriate structural design. For instance, when the limiting value is rational, a well-designed
network can converge to the exact value at a finite size. Our results suggest that increasing the
dimensionality of the structure may expand the range of irrational number limits accessible to the
network.
For the structure studied here, the normalized resistance approaches √3, and the experimental
result agrees with the predicted behavior. The small positive deviation is consistent with the
contribution of contact resistance. If contact resistance could be eliminated by replacing
conventional ohmic contacts with artificial contacts based on density-mismatch junctions21, more
complex networks of edge currents might provide a more direct realization of the theoretical result.
More broadly, our approach shows how the limiting properties of geometric networks can provide
well-defined quantized responses beyond conventional rational values, offering a framework for
exploring quantization associated with non-periodic or infinite structures using finite physical
systems. Beyond this specific realization, the framework raises the possibility that quantization can
emerge as a well-defined asymptotic limit. A sequence of finite systems may possess rational or
otherwise conventional responses at every finite size while converging to an irrational value in the
infinite-size limit. As shown in Supplementary Material, Section B and Eq. S15, different irrational
quantization values are possible. Whether such asymptotic responses constitute a distinct form of
quantization remains an open question. The convergence law may also provide an additional means
of characterizing different geometric realizations.

become negligible in the infinite-size limit, so we adopt periodic boundary conditions for
