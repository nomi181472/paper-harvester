Abstract—We present a novel analytical framework for evaluating the age of information (AoI) in multi-path queueing
topologies where output streams from multiple servers converge
into a single pipeline. This theoretical scenario finds immediate application in ultra-reliable low-latency networks and edge
computing architectures, where multi-path routing and stream
merging can be vital strategies for maintaining fresh status
updates. Specifically, we investigate two setups: a split-merge
network and a duplicate-merge network. The split-merge case
has been addressed in prior work, but we show that existing
studies are incorrect, and our approach provides instead an exact
analytical formulation. Moreover, building on similar reasoning,
we provide the first formal analysis of the previously unexplored
duplicate-merge scenario. Finally, we show how to leverage
our theoretical results to solve key system-level optimization
problems, deriving the optimal randomized routing probabilities
and traffic injection rates that minimize average AoI.
Index Terms—Age of information, Statistics, Fork-join queues,
Multipath communication.

I. I NTRODUCTION
The advent of ultra-reliable low-latency communications in
next-generation networks is driving the deployment of applications that rely on real-time information about the surrounding
environment [1]. In such systems, the freshness of information
is a key performance indicator and is commonly quantified through metrics such as age of information (AoI) [2].
The precise mathematical formulation of AoI has revitalized
fundamental research at the intersection of queueing theory,
optimization, and stochastic analysis [3], [4].
At the same time, multiaccess edge computing (MEC) and
other distributed processing architectures require computational tasks to be distributed across multiple heterogeneous
servers [5]. The resulting parallel processing hierarchy often
requires data to be split or duplicated to avoid congestion
bottlenecks, with the outputs subsequently merged to produce
the final result. While this approach improves scalability
and resource utilization, it also substantially increases the
analytical complexity of the system, making the evaluation
of AoI-like metrics in converging architectures difficult [6].
Consequently, quantifying the freshness of information delivery in merging systems is not only a non-trivial mathematical problem, but also a fundamental design issue for
future communication networks. To address this challenge,
we present a novel analytical framework to evaluate the

Specifically, we focus on two different fork-split paradigms:
a split-merge architecture and a duplicate-merge architecture

The case of split-merge implies that two M/M/1 queues
are employed to serve status updates coming from a single
source. The traffic is randomly divided, each packet being
routed to server 1 with probability α or server 2 with probability 1−α. This directly relates to the stateless random-alpha
routing adopted in MEC [7], [8]. Although the AoI for this
topology has been studied in the prior literature [9], existing
analytical techniques suffer from high structural complexity,
which forced the introduction of some hidden assumptions.
We will show that this causes the existing models to become
incorrect at high loads. In contrast, our methodology yields an
exact closed-form analytical formulation.
Building upon this framework, we also provide the analysis
of the duplicate-merge scenario, where traffic is copied on
a secondary path with probability α, rather than split in an
alternative fashion. As a result, the first queue receives 100%
of the traffic, while the second queue only a fraction α of it,
and its packets are always present in the other queue. To our
knowledge, this case has never been attempted before, and we
provide its first exact formal AoI analysis.
Beyond performance evaluation, we demonstrate the practical utility of our theoretical results by showing how they
can be used for system-level optimization problems, such
as deriving the optimal randomized routing probabilities and
traffic injection rates required to minimize the average AoI.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
II discusses the related literature. Section III analyzes the
split-merge network. Section IV presents the analysis of the
duplicate-merge topology. We present numerical results in

Section V and conclude in Section VI.
II. R ELATED W ORK
AoI, introduced in [10], has emerged as a fundamental
metric for quantifying information freshness in real-time monitoring and status update systems. Initial investigations of multipath networks demonstrated that transmission path diversity
can substantially mitigate status staleness by providing alternative parallel routes [11]. To analyze complex queueing
dynamics in such multi-source scenarios, Yates and Kaul
developed a general analytical framework based on stochastic
hybrid systems (SHS) [12], which became the cornerstone for
computing AoI in networks with multiple updating streams.
These investigations about the interaction between packet
generation policies and service capacities in multi-source
environments were also revisited by the same authors for
multi-source M/M/1 queues to characterize optimal update
rates under preemptive and non-preemptive disciplines in [13],
while Moltafet et al. provided a more precise AoI evaluation
across various multi-source queueing models in [14] and also
extended the analysis to a wide variety of queueing systems
in [15], but always considering multiple independent sources,
each generating different traffic. The influential summary on
the main findings for AoI-related metrics reported in [2]
discusses many results, but most of them relate to the case of
independent sources and multiple servers in the same facility.
Subsequent efforts expanded these frameworks to structured queueing topologies where multiple sources are not
independent but rather pertain to similar observations. A first
line of research involves the study of correlated information,
often motivated by the observation that multiple monitoring
sources often measure overlapping portions of the surrounding
environment and/or similar metrics. In this spirit, Tripathi and
Modiano [16] as well as Tong et al. [17] proposed optimization
frameworks for source scheduling, while Zancanaro et al. [18]
studied a stochastic model similar to an M/M/1 queue. A
more recent extension by Liang and Zhou [19] included a
random access in the analysis, and Fidler et al. connected the
correlation to geometry aspects in [20], still making explicit
reference to classic queueing theory. All of these contributions,
even when directly addressing queues, present, however, fundamentally different models in that the system always consists
of multiple independent sources. The correlation of content
just considers some packets as informative for other sources
as well. However, preemption issues are not considered and
the presence of additional flows is always advantageous.
For the specific case where the interdependency of content stems instead from an initial separation among multiple
paths and a subsequent merge along the way, Chiariotti et
al. analyzed the impact of non-preemptive tributary traffic
converging onto a main transmission line, highlighting the
queueing delays induced by merging streams [21]. In [6],
Tang et al. studied a similar splitting policy for MEC, but
where the probabilistic split (the third policy examined in the
paper) revolves around a queue tandem representing different
stages of the processing of subtasks, instead of a split-merge

lane. For stream convergence, the performance of parallel
transmission channels was examined by Fidler et al., who
established statistical AoI limits to quantify when independent
channel diversity yields tangible freshness gains [3].
In a similar vein, Bhati et al. derived AoI expressions for
queueing systems with parallel heterogeneous servers [9]. This
last paper presents an analysis similar to ours; however, it
subtly introduces approximations that render it inexact, leading
to discrepancies in the AoI values. In contrast, our analysis is
exact and, as we will show, resolves these issues.
More recently, Chen et al. analyzed status update timeliness
when parallel updates are sent across heterogeneous update
devices [22], and Reddy and Venkatesh explored proactive
management strategies to discard obsolete status packets in
multi-hop multi-source queues [4]. The findings above have
had repercussions on cross-disciplinary issues such as stochastic control methods for M/M/k multi-server queues. In this
case, the optimization framework proposed by Lefebvre and
Yaghoubi [23], provides queue-management techniques for
dynamic server activation tuning the value of k.
Despite these extensive contributions, the existing literature
does not have an exact analysis of AoI in either of these
topologies. Thus, our work provides a tractable, exact analytical framework of both split-merge and duplicate-merge,
correcting the existing models of the literature for the former,
and solving the latter, which is an entirely open problem.
III. S PLIT-M ERGE : A NALYTICAL D ERIVATION
We consider a split-merge (also sometimes called fork-split)
status update system [9] in which a single source generates
status-update packets according to a Poisson process of rate
λ. Upon generation, each packet is independently routed to one
of two parallel first-come-first-served (FCFS), non-preemptive
M/M/1 queues: queue 1, with service rate µ1 , is selected with
probability α, while queue 2, with service rate µ2 , is selected
with the complementary probability 1 − α. Both sub-streams
are therefore Poisson, with rates λ1 = αλ and λ2 = (1 − α)λ
respectively, and each queue is stable if λi < µi , i = 1, 2.
Since both queues operate under the FCFS discipline, a
packet can never be overtaken by another packet queued
behind it in that same queue. However, because the two
queues are physically separate and are read independently
by the monitor, a queue i packet can be overtaken, in terms
of delivery order, by a packet that was generated later but
was routed to the competing queue j ̸= i and happens to
complete service first. When this occurs, the overtaken packet
no longer carries the most recent information available to the
monitor and, upon its (out-of-order) delivery, does not reduce
AoI. Only packets that are not overtaken, which we term
informative, actually reset AoI at the monitor.
The goal of this work is to characterize the long-term
average AoI of this split-merge system. Following the standard
graphical (area-based) decomposition of the age process [2],
the average AoI can be written in terms of two quantities
associated with the sequence of informative departures: the
system time S of an informative packet (i.e., the total time

it spends in the system, from generation to delivery) and the
inter-departure time T between two consecutive informative
departures, which is precisely the interval over which the AoI
grows linearly before being reset. The general expression for
the time-average AoI is

IV. D UPLICATE -M ERGE : A NALYTICAL D ERIVATION
We now turn to the case of duplicate-merge. Rather than
being routed to either of the two queues, every packet is now
guaranteed to be delivered through queue 1 and additionally
duplicated to queue 2 with probability α. As in the splitting
model, packet is informative only if it is not overtaken by a
fresher packet from the competing queue. The average AoI
is still given by (1). The two policies differ, however, in a
way that is consequential for the analysis: in the splitting
model the two queues are fed by independent Poisson substreams, whereas here queue 2’s arrivals are a sub-sample
of queue 1’s. This coupling leaves the marginal behavior of
each queue unchanged but makes the queues’ occupancies
correlated, which in turn requires two structurally different
derivations: one for the overtaking probability and one for the
joint law governing the case decomposition introduced below.
Since packets arrive according to a Poisson process of rate
λ, and they are all routed to queue 1 but independently, each
packet is also duplicated to queue 2 with probability α, by
Poisson thinning, the duplicated sub-stream is itself Poisson
of rate λ′ := αλ, and we write ν1 := µ1 − λ, ν2 := µ2 − λ′ ,
ρ2 := λ′ /µ2 for the stability-guaranteed rates and traffic
intensity of the two queues. The departure process decomposes
into three mutually exclusive cases according to which copy
of the record-setting packet determines the next informative
departure: Case 1, in which the record-setting packet was
not duplicated; and Cases 2 and 3, in which the packet was
duplicated and the first to depart is the queue-1 copy or the
queue-2 copy, respectively.

As both queues are FCFS, no packet generated after a
tagged duplicate can depart from either queue before the
tagged duplicate’s earliest copy does: any later-generated
packet routed through queue 1 necessarily departs after the
tagged copy already present in queue 1, and, if it is itself
duplicated, its queue-2 copy likewise departs after the tagged

one will be informative and the other will not, so no equivalent
of Pinf,1 is required for Cases 2 and 3; the relevant quantity

VI. C ONCLUSION
We introduced an analytical framework to characterize AoI
in multi-path queueing topologies with converging server

streams. By investigating split-merge and duplicate-merge
M/M/1 network architectures, we resolved critical analytical
limitations in prior studies. Specifically, our exact closed-form
solutions corrected inaccuracies found in existing split-merge
models, while our novel derivation for the duplicate-merge
paradigm established the first formal analysis of this scenario.
Our findings provide practical guidelines for tuning randomized routing probabilities and traffic injection rates to minimize
average AoI. These insights offer robust architectural foundations for ultra-reliable low-latency communications, edge
computing, and real-time cyber-physical systems.
