An Availability Management Framework for
Microservice-Based Safety-Critical CIoT Systems –
Architecture and End-to-End Evaluation
Hassaan Siddiqui, and Ferhat Khendek, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Safety-critical Cellular Internet of Things (CIoT)
systems increasingly rely on microservice architectures
orchestrated across heterogeneous edge and cloud platforms.
Although this paradigm improves modularity and scalability, the
fault-tolerance mechanisms of contemporary orchestration
platforms remain predominantly reactive, i.e., failed components
are restarted or rescheduled only after an outage has already
occurred. Such recovery can produce multi-second End to End
Service Outage Time (E2E SOT), which can grow further under
container restart backoff. This paper proposes an Availability
Management Framework (AMF), a generic monitor-predictrecover loop that complements reactive recovery with machine
learning-based anomaly detection and make-before-break
switchover to a standby replica. Candidate ML models are
evaluated offline under three protocols, including a change of
operating regime, in order to select a model for live deployment.
AMF is instantiated and evaluated on a three-tier tele-operated
driving (ToD) testbed under a staged resource-exhaustion
scenario. Relative to Kubernetes-only recovery, AMF completes
switchover during the anomaly window, reduces E2E SOT, and
avoids the tens-of-seconds outages associated with repeated inplace restarts under backoff. On this non-real-time testbed,
residual E2E SOT lies inside healthy-path jitter and is therefore
limited by path timing rather than by lateness of the recovery
decision. The paper whows that coupling a validated ML based
anomaly detector with proactive switchover can remove restartand backoff-dominated outage from a critical path.

Microservices Architecture (MA) has become a widely

denser failure surface. Services interact across nodes and
networks; dependency failures, resource contention, and
orchestration delays can cascade into observable service
outages even when individual components appear healthy.
Maintaining availability under these conditions requires not
only platform-level orchestration, but also availability
management mechanisms that can act before a fault becomes
an E2E disruption.
Container orchestrators such as Kubernetes (K8s) provide
automated placement, scaling, and recovery for microservice
workloads, and have therefore been a natural substrate for
CIoT deployments. In prior work we examined microservicebased Tele-operated Driving (ToD) services on K8s and

its recovery remains predominantly reactive and does not, by
itself, meet the availability expectations of safety-critical CIoT
applications [1], [9]. In particular, recovery begins after the
failure has already interrupted the service path, so the duration
of the outage is governed by detection latency, container
restart, and related control-plane delays. For applications that
must sustain a continuous command or control stream, such
interruptions cannot be tolerated.

mechanisms that complement reactive recovery with proactive

A. Problem Statement
Safety-critical CIoT systems require continuous E2E
service availability. Microservices coupled with container
orchestration platforms provide automated recovery, yet that
recovery is predominantly reactive, i.e., corrective action
begins only after a component has already failed, so the
service path may already be disrupted. Proactive FT based on
ML-based anomaly detection is a promising complement, but
two difficulties remain. First, an ML model must be selected
and validated so that it is suitable for live use, i.e., validation
under a single operating regime may overstate field
performance and lead to an inappropriate deployment choice.
Second, anomaly detection alone does not restore service,
unless anomaly scores are consumed by a recovery action,
such as switchover to a standby replica, before failure
materializes, E2E availability may be no better than under
reactive recovery. The overarching problem is therefore to
design an availability management framework that integrates
monitoring, ML-based anomaly detection, and proactive
recovery in addition to the reactive one, and to determine
empirically whether that approach reduces E2E service
disruption relative to platform recovery alone under a
representative failure scenario.

MA views applications as small, independently deployable
services that encapsulate distinct functions. Together with
containerization, this model improves scalability, modularity,
and evolution of individual components without redeploying
the entire application [12]. Container orchestrators are
commonly used to operate such systems at scale. K8s, in
particular, automates placement, scaling, load balancing, and
basic fault recovery by monitoring container health and
replacing failed units. The combination of MA and K8s
therefore provides a flexible substrate for distributed, safetycritical CIoT deployments [13]. In our earlier survey of
microservices for IoT [6], we concluded that MA is well
suited to heterogeneous and dynamic edge-to-cloud
environments [4], [5], [7], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18].
Nevertheless, high availability remains difficult to assure, each
service runs in its own failure domain, dependencies can
propagate faults across the chain, and orchestrator recovery is
typically triggered only after a disruption has already
occurred. Failures may arise from container crashes, resource
exhaustion, network impairment, or memory-related faults,
among other causes. In prior studies of a safety-critical CIoT
testbed built on MA and K8s, we showed that reactive
recovery alone does not meet the availability requirements of
such applications when evaluated on E2E service behavior [1],
[9].
FT mechanisms are often classified as reactive or
proactive. Reactive approaches detect failures after occurence
and then restore the affected component, for example, through
restart or rescheduling. Proactive approaches aim to anticipate
impending faults and initiate protective action earlier, thereby
limiting or avoiding service interruption [19]. For
microservice-based systems, several research directions
pursue ML-assisted proactive FT. Tran et al. [19] combine
recurrent failure prediction with proactive service migration
for containerized workloads. Almaraz-Rivera [20] presents a
cloud-native MLOps pipeline for anomaly-based monitoring
of on-premise Kubernetes clusters, combining metric
collection with Chaos Engineering to validate outlier alerts.
Their focus is detection and alerting for ephemeral container
workloads, rather than binding anomaly scores to proactive
recovery or measuring E2E service outage time.
Anemogiannis et al. [21] propose a K8s monitoring
framework that builds a graph representation of cluster
resources and uses graph features, together with supervised
models, to highlight abnormal components in a dynamic
environment. Like other detector-centric approaches, their

Despite this progress, much of the literature still evaluates
success primarily through detector-centric metrics,
classification accuracy, precision, recall, or F1-score, or
through recovery of an individual microservice, rather than
through sustained availability of an E2E service path. In
safety-critical CIoT, however, the user-visible outcome is
continuity of the complete service chain across devices, edge
nodes, and backend platforms. A timely anomaly score on one
pod does not, by itself, establish improved availability if
recovery begins only after crash, if actuation is absent, or if
switchover cannot complete within the precursor window.
Conversely, measuring outage duration alone does not justify
which detector or training protocol should be deployed in a
live management loop. The gap that motivates the present
study is therefore twofold; availability management must
integrate monitoring, ML-based prediction, and recovery into
a closed loop, and that loop must be evaluated with systemlevel measures of E2E service outage, not with offline
classifier scores alone.
The framework proposed and evaluated in this article
follows the closed control-loop tradition of autonomic
computing [28], in which monitored system state drives
analysis and corrective actions without operator intervention.
Relative to prior detector-focused and service-level recovery
studies, we emphasize offline validation of the deployed
anomaly detector together with online measurement of E2E
service disruption under reactive platform recovery and under
proactive switchover on a three-tier testbed.
III. THE AVAILABILITY MANAGEMENT FRAMEWORK
The proposed AMF is designed to strengthen the resilience
of MA-based safety-critical CIoT systems, taking into account
their distributed and heterogeneous character and the
limitations of MA when coupled with a container orchestrator

such as K8s. The framework addresses both reactive and
proactive fault management. Reactively, it supports recovery
from unforeseen failures by leveraging orchestration
mechanisms to restore failed microservices. Proactively, it
employs anomaly detection to anticipate impending faults
from observed behavioral patterns and to trigger switchover
before a disruption materializes on the E2E service path.
Together, these capabilities aim to sustain continuous service
availability in line with the stringent availability requirements
of safety-critical environments. As illustrated in Fig 1, the
AMF comprises three modules: the monitoring module, the
prediction module, and the recovery module.
A. Monitoring Module
The monitoring module comprises two components: a
healthcheck and a resource monitor. The healthcheck
periodically verifies that each microservice is operating as
intended. It differs from K8s default check, which primarily
indicates whether a pod is running or not. The AMF
healthcheck additionally assesses whether the application
logic and services executing inside the pod are up and
responding. This distinction matters because a pod may
remain Running while internal code or an in-pod service
misbehaves in a way that degrades the microservice’s
delivered function. The corresponding notification flow is;
when an unexpected behavior is detected and the healthcheck
fails, the recovery module is notified and initiates an
appropriate failover to restore the affected microservice.

B. Prediction Module
The prediction module comprises two components: a data
parser and an anomaly detector. The data parser retrieves
records from the resource-monitor database and prepares them
for consumption by the ML model. The anomaly detector
follows the conventional offline-training and online-inference
paradigm, as shown in Fig 2. The model is trained offline on
data collected from the CIoT system and is then used to detect
anomalies online. During offline training, historical telemetry
is collected, parsed, and appropriately labeled. A suitable
learning algorithm is trained on the labeled data, validated on
held-out samples, and packaged for deployment. Because of
the associated computational cost, this phase is performed
offline in a controlled environment. Once deployed, the online
phase operates in real time: live system data are ingested for
inference without further training, and the model continuously
analyses incoming streams to flag potential faults.

Selecting and training an ML model that detects anomalies
accurately and precisely remains non-trivial. It requires an
understanding of the data characteristics, the anomaly types of
interest, and the performance requirements of the detector. In
this work, the focus is on anomalies associated with elevated
memory and CPU usage, which may indicate issues such as
memory leaks or inefficient execution. For these anomaly
types, both supervised and unsupervised learning are
applicable. When the task is formulated as binary
classification of normal versus anomalous instances,
supervised methods such as Decision Tree or Random Forest
classifiers may be used. These approaches rely on a labeled
offline dataset in which normal and anomalous states are
explicitly annotated. A Decision Tree partitions the feature
space into subsets according to feature values, yielding a treestructured decision model, whereas a Random Forest
aggregates multiple trees to improve predictive performance.
Unsupervised clustering methods are also applicable and do
not require labels. Instead, they identify structure in the data
by grouping similar points, and treat poorly fitting points as
anomalies. Examples include K-Means, which partitions data
into K clusters by feature similarity, and Fuzzy C-Means. Both
families of methods have merits for detecting high memory
and high CPU anomalies, the choice depends on the

availability of labeled data and on the specific requirements of
the detection task.
Beyond the choice of learning paradigm, the prediction
module must also be validated under conditions representative
of the deployment. In CIoT environments, resource-usage
telemetry may change with workload and operating regime, so
a detector that performs well on an offline training corpus may
exhibit reduced recall or an altered precision–recall trade-off
once deployed. Because AMF couples anomaly scores to
recovery actions that affect E2E service availability, such
degradation is not merely a classification concern, i.e., missed
precursors can delay or prevent proactive switchover.
Accordingly, candidate ML models should be assessed under
complementary validation protocols prior to deployment, the
procedure used in this study is discussed in Section V and
based on our earlier offline evaluation [10].
C. Recovery Module
The third module of AMF is the recovery module, which
maintains service continuity by managing microservice
recovery in response to detected anomalies or actual failures.
It comprises four components: failover mechanisms,
switchover mechanisms, replica creation, and a state
controller. When a microservice fails, the system can trigger
failover by redirecting its workload to a pre-existing redundant
replica or by restarting the microservice, thereby limiting
downtime. Alternatively, when the anomaly detector reports
abnormal behavior indicative of an impending failure, the
recovery module can proactively create a redundant replica of
the affected microservice and switchover operation to that
healthy replica before failure materializes, thereby avoiding
interruption on the service path, as shown in Fig 3. However, if
the abnormal behavior is not detected, failure happens and the
recovery follows failure path. The choice of failover or
switchover strategy, including cold standby, hot standby, or
dynamic replica creation, can be configured according to the
requirements of the safety-critical CIoT system, available
resources, and whether the microservice is stateful or stateless.
This modular and adaptive design aims to provide robust FT
while remaining flexible with respect to deployment
constraints and operational priorities.

In line with classical FT design, AMF distinguishes at least
two principal models. Under hot standby, a replica runs
alongside the active instance and is kept ready to take over
with minimal interruption, which favors stringent availability
at the cost of continuous resource consumption. Under cold
standby, a replica remains inactive or unscheduled until
recovery is required, which reduces operational cost but
lengthens the time to restore service, including startup and any
necessary state preparation. Where no standby is configured,
recovery is a restart of the failed instance. Table 1 summarizes
the main differences between these models. For stateful
services, continuous or on-demand state replication, for
example via a state controller, may be required so that
switchover or failover does not discard application state,
whereas, for stateless services, lighter synchronization may
suffice. AMF does not prescribe one model for all CIoT
workloads. Instead, it provides the recovery actions, failover
after failure and proactive creation or activation of a standby
followed by switchover when an anomaly is predicted, so that
a software architect can map each service to a redundancy
model that matches its allowed downtime, resource budget,
and safety role.

In practice, that mapping is a design decision driven by
service criticality, i.e., mission-critical control paths typically
warrant hot standby, or an equivalent always-ready replica,
whereas less critical analytics or auxiliary services may use
cold standby. For example, a vehicle-control service
responsible for steering, braking, and acceleration may be
configured with hot-standby replicas and proactive state
synchronization, while an entertainment service with lower
safety impact may use cold-standby and fewer replicas. AMF
further allows standby activation to be triggered by anomaly
detection rather than only by hard failure, so a cold or dormant
replica can be brought online during a precursor window when
the chosen model and the prediction lead time permit. The
experiments conducted in this studyillustrate one such choice,
an on-demand standby that is scaled up and switched over
under ML-driven recovery for a critical path service, without
exhausting the configuration space of the framework. Other
deployments may keep an always-on hot pair, place replicas

on another node under node-level risk, or combine models
across services in the same system.
D. State Management
In AMF, both stateful and stateless microservices are
managed through the integration of K8s native controllers
with the recovery module’s state controller, with the aim of
supporting resilient and consistent service availability. For
stateless microservices, AMF uses the K8s Deployment
controller, which simplifies scaling and recovery by allowing
multiple interchangeable replicas to share access to a common
Persistent Volume (PV). This arrangement is suitable for
services that do not retain internal state across sessions.
Stateful microservices, which maintain service-specific data or
context over time, are managed using the K8s StatefulSet
controller. Each StatefulSet replica is associated with a
dedicated PV, so that state is preserved if the pod is restarted
or relocated. This association is important for consistency and
data integrity in stateful workloads. However, because the
original pod and a replica do not share the same PV by default,
state stored on the original PV is not automatically replicated
to the replica’s PV. As a result, failure of the original pod, or
switchover to a replica, may lead to loss of state. To address
this issue, AMF adopts the State Controller (SC) proposed by
Vayghan et al. [13], which periodically replicates the state of
the active pod into the PV of the standby replica so that the
two remain aligned. The SC interacts with the K8s API server,
monitors cluster events, and responds accordingly. It assigns
high-availability roles to active pods and enables them to
replicate state data to designated standbys, creating statereplication services as needed. Pods are organized into active–
standby pairs, with the standby labeled as a peer and linked to
the corresponding active pod. State replication supports both
sudden failure and predicted anomaly. Upon sudden failure,
the recovery module initiates failover. When the prediction
module detects an anomaly in a stateful microservice, the
recovery module instead initiates proactive switchover by
redirecting traffic to a healthy replica. In either case, the goal
is to limit service disruption while allowing the new instance
to resume with minimal loss of context. By combining K8s
controllers with AMF recovery logic, the framework provides
a foundation for availability management across both stateless
and stateful components in safety-critical CIoT systems. For
application, CPU- or memory-related anomalies, a replica may
be placed on the same node. For node-related anomalies, a
replica may be placed on another node.

A. The ToD Testbed
The testbed follows our earlier ToD case study [1], a
safety-critical CIoT application with an availability
requirement of five nines (99.999%) [29]. The system spans
three nodes, i.e., an On-Board Unit (OBU), an edge node, and
a Remote Driving Station (RDS). Each node runs its own
single-node K8s cluster where ToD microservices are
deployed on, as shown in Fig 4. For communication between
microservices and between nodes, MQTT brokers are
deployed on each node, and the brokers form a star topology
with the edge broker as the central hub.
The E2E service evaluated in this study is remote speed
control. A driver at the RDS sends speed commands that must
reach the vehicle’s OBU without interruption. RDS speed
microservice publishes on MQTT topic set/speed (100 ms

through the mesh to the edge broker, then to the OBU broker,
and finally to OBU speed microservice. Any unexpected
outage in that E2E service path directly affects remote control
of vehicle speed, so service continuity on this E2E path is the
availability metric that matters for the experiment. This E2E
service path also has several potential failure points, including
the MQTT brokers on each node. In this study, memoryrelated faults are injected at the edge broker, because it carries

B. AMF Deployment on the ToD Testbed
Beyond the functional design of its modules, AMF must
also be mapped onto the distributed topology of a CIoT
deployment. This subsection discusses recommended
placement of the monitoring, prediction, and recovery
modules.
As CIoT systems are typically distributed, the monitoring
module is preferably deployed on each node so that
microservice health and resource utilization can be observed
in real time. Placement of the prediction module is
challenging, as this module hosts a trained ML model, there
are pros and cons of either having a centralized ML model or
training multiple ML models on each node. Therefore, a
careful trade-off must be considered. Hosting the model in the
cloud may increase the latency of anomaly notifications,
which is undesirable in safety-critical settings. In 5G-based
CIoT deployments, Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) can
reduce that latency relative to a remote cloud, yet MEC nodes
generally offer fewer resources than cloud platforms, while
ML inference and training remain comparatively resource
intensive. For our AMF, we recommend deploying the
prediction module on the edge node so that predictions can be
provided to the other nodes with low latency.
Deployment of the recovery module should follow the
application and Cloud-native Network Function (CNF)
placement strategy. When microservices run on a container
orchestrator such as K8s, the recovery module should match
the cluster topology. If K8s is configured as a single multinode cluster hosting the full CIoT E2E system, the recovery
module may be placed centrally on the control plane. If,
instead, each site runs as a single-node K8s cluster, the
recovery module should be deployed on each node and
integrated with the local K8s instance.
For our testbed, as shown in Fig 5, the monitoring module is
deployed on all three nodes, prediction module is centralized
on the edge node, and recovery module is deployed on all
three nodes, consistent with the single-node K8s configuration
at each node.
This AMF deployment on the ToD testbed realizes the
three AMF modules as a closed data path from telemetry
collection through inference to recovery actuation.
Monitoring, prediction, and recovery remain functionally
separated, i.e., monitoring produces features, prediction
produces anomaly scores, and recovery alone mutates cluster
state.
For monitoring, resource telemetry is collected with
Prometheus [30], exposed on each node via NodePort 30000.
A Python client on the corresponding node issues the same six
pod-level PromQL queries used to construct the offline
training data, that includes six features, i.e., CPU and memory

Recovery module implementation details are provided in
Table 3. For failover, K8s deployment controller is used which

can restart a pod or container after crash has occurred. For
proactive switchover, recovery module uses an on-demand
standby broker replica, i.e., the replica creation agent scales up
the standby broker deployment, holds overlap, then scales
down the primary broker deployment when the prediction
module reports an anomaly. Before issuing scale actions, the
recovery module applies three actuation gates. The decision
threshold is 0.5, i.e, a poll is treated as anomalous only if
proba_anomaly ≥ 0.5. Debounce requires three consecutive
anomalous polls for the same pod at the 5s interval (about 10s
of sustained evidence); any below-threshold sample resets the
counter, so an isolated spike cannot trigger recovery. The
same three-sample rule is applied again after the standby is
Ready, before the primary is retired. The switch hold is a
minimum of 15s between the scale-up decision and primary
scale-down, so both replicas remain available for a non-zero
overlap. The state for the standby bridge configuration is
supplied through K8s ConfigMaps with distinct client IDs so
the primary and standby can run without clashing.
It is important to note that the prediction module never
modifies cluster state, and the recovery module never
computes anomaly scores. Consequently, correct detection
without this ML pipeline does not shorten the outage. Also,
AMF modules do not exchange control messages over the
ToD MQTT mesh. In this deployment, MQTT carries the
data-plane, i.e., application traffic, whereas AMF needs a
control-plane path for anomaly scores and recovery actuation.
The evaluated fault targets the edge broker, which is the hub
of that mesh, using the same brokers for detector-to-recovery
signaling would couple the control-plane to the component
under stress and could block actuation precisely when
recovery is required. Instead, inference output is passed
locally on the edge node, and recovery acts through the local
K8s API. Application traffic remains on MQTT but control
traffic does not.

V. ML MODEL SELECTION
The selection of an appropriate ML model is a critical
design decision, specifically when balancing detection
accuracy against stringent availability requirements. For AMF
to close the loop online, the prediction module needs an ML
model that is suitable for live deployment. This section
summarizes how that ML model was selected. We describe
the telemetry dataset, the validation protocols used to compare
candidate models, the models under consideration, and a brief
robustness analysis that guided the choice of the model used in

the experiments of Section VI. A more detailed study of these
protocols is reported in [10].
A. The Dataset
The dataset used to train ML model was generated from
the ToD testbed through Prometheus metrics collection. It has
been made publicly available on GitHub [31] to support future
research in safety-critical CIoT. The transition from raw
Prometheus telemetry to an analytical dataset requires a
structured feature engineering pipeline. We monitor six
features, as shown in Table 4, which are the primary metrics at

These metrics are extracted using the Prometheus query_range
API, which ensures that all features are aligned to a
normalized time grid. This timestamp synchronization is
critical for maintaining consistency across heterogeneous
sampling intervals and enabling accurate cross-metric
correlation within the ML model.

For ML model selection, we considered both supervised
and unsupervised learning approaches. While unsupervised
methods such as K-Means or Isolation Forest offer the
advantage of operating without labeled data, they present
significant challenges in automated recovery loops. In such
models, the resulting clusters often require manual post-facto
interpretation to distinguish between specific fault types,
hindering real-time response. Furthermore, in safety-critical
domains, explainability is paramount. Therefore, we selected
three supervised ML models, Random Forest (RF) [33],
Logistic Regression (LR) [34], and Hist-Gradient Boosting
Classifier (HGBC) [35]. These models not only facilitate
feature-level validation to identify the specific drivers of an
anomaly score but are also naturally suited to capture the cliffedge effects (e.g., sudden CPU thrashing or memory
exhaustion) common in microservice resource contention.

B. Validation Protocols
Candidate detectors are compared under three
complementary validation protocols, inspired by [36], that
reflect different relationships between the data used for
training and the data used for evaluation. The protocols are
used here to select a model suitable for deployment, not as a
standalone study of open-world Out-Of-Distribution (OOD)
learning. Taken together, they contrast an optimistic indistribution baseline with more realistic operating conditions
for safety-critical CIoT systems. In this article, these protocols
guide ML model selection for the live AMF loop. The model
deployed in Section VI is chosen on the basis of P1, which
balances offline evidence of recovery under stressed
conditions with the availability of limited labeled support.
a) P0 – i.i.d. Baseline: Models are trained and tested on
a shuffled, stratified split of the consolidated dataset
(containing both Dnominal and Dstressed). Training and test
samples are drawn from the same distribution, approximating
an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) scenario. P0
therefore provides an optimistic upper bound on classifier
performance when the evaluation distribution matches the
training mixture. Telemetry is temporally autocorrelated, so
P0 should not be interpreted as a strict i.i.d. guarantee.
b) P1 – Few-Shot Domain Adaptation: P1 represents a
practical deployment setting in which some labeled stressedregime data are available for model training. Training uses
Dnominal together with a stratified 30% support subset of

Dstressed; evaluation uses the held-out 70% of Dstressed. This
protocol measures how well detection performance recovers
when the detector is retrained on the concatenated labeled
support, rather than classical few-shot adaptation in the
machine-learning sense.
c) P2 – OOD Generalization: P2 trains on the
full Dnominal data (both classes) and evaluates on the
full Dstressed data, with no labeled target-regime support at
training time. It therefore assesses transfer across operating
regimes on the same testbed. P2 is a zero-shot regime-transfer
setting, not an OOD benchmark across unrelated deployments.
C. ML Model Robustness Analysis
Offline robustness analysis is used here to choose an ML
model for the AMF recovery loop, not to claim a new learning
method. In AMF, an anomaly score is not an end in itself, in
fact, a positive decision can trigger standby creation and
switchover. The relevant trade-off is therefore between false
alarms and missed precursors. Precision measures how often a
raised anomaly corresponds to a true anomalous sample, i.e.,
low precision implies frequent false alarms and unnecessary
recovery actions. On the other hand, recall measures how
often true anomalies are detected, i.e., low recall implies
missed precursors and lost opportunity to act before failure.
For closed-loop availability management, both matter, but
they are not interchangeable, i.e., a high-recall, low-precision
detector is a poor driver for automatic switchover, whereas a

high-precision, low-recall detector may rarely act when action
would help. Other evaluation metrics, accuracy, F1-score and
AUC are reported in [10] and are omitted here, because they
do not directly affect the recovery action trade-off.

ML model selection for the AMF prioritizes (i) high
precision, so recovery is not dominated by false alarms, and
(ii) among high-precision models, higher recall under a change
of operating regime, so precursors are missed less often. LR is
discarded for live actuation because of its low P2 precision.
RF and HGBC both satisfy the precision requirement under P1
and P2. Between them, HGBC is selected because it retains
higher recall under P2 (0.465 vs 0.353) while preserving
precision of 1.000. Under P1, RF is marginally stronger on
precision and recall, we do not claim HGBC is uniquely
optimal on P1 alone. The live detector used in our deployed
AMF is therefore the P1-trained HGBC model with decision
threshold of 0.5. A more complete robustness study, including
accuracy, F1-score, and AUC, is reported in [10].

B. Faults & Anomalies Profile
In safety-critical systems, harmful conditions often develop
gradually, i.e., performance degrades, internal state becomes
unstable, and only later does a failure interrupt the service
offered to the user. Availability management is most useful in
that interval, when a proactive recovery action can still
prevent or shorten an outage. This subsection defines how
anomalies and faults are modeled on the testbed and the
anomaly/fault injection target.
An anomaly is an abnormal behavior that signals risk but
does not by itself stop the E2E service, e.g., sustained high
resource usage while the component remains operational.
Whereas a failure is an event that interrupts service until
recovery is completed. An anomaly may precede a failure,
detecting an anomaly does not mean a failure has already
occurred. E2E SOT is tied to the failure’s impact on the
consumer, not to the first anomalous event, as illustrated in Fig
7. An anomaly occurs at t0, and if it is detected and proactive
switchover (Rs) is applied, then a failure can be avoided. But if
either an anomaly is missed or Rs is not applied, then it can
become a fault and cause a failure (t1) which would need a
failover (Rf) to recover the service.
The anomaly and fault profile here follows memory-related
stress characterized in [9]. It uses a staged injector on the edge
broker pod, as described in Table 8. Phase 1 aligns with sublimit stress used in offline training data, as discussed in
Section V, and allows Prometheus rate features to update.
Phase 1 also allows to record gaps/jitter on the service path
during a short no-stress window, so that cut-over or failure
gaps can be compared to the immediately preceding healthy
baseline rather than to an abstract bound alone. Phase 2
matches broker OOM behavior in [9]. Phases run
consecutively with no idle gap between them. An event with
only sub-limit stress can be detectable yet may not produce a
clear outage, whereas an event with immediate OOM leaves
almost no precursor window. The staged profile supports both
precursor detection and, when recovery does not act in time,
an E2E service outage. The injector does not execute recovery
actions.

Stress was applied at one microservice, the edge MQTT
broker, see Fig 5, because it is the central hub of the broker
mesh for the evaluated service path, i.e., every RDS-to-OBU
message for remote speed control passes through it. Failure or
loss of that relay therefore affects the full E2E service, not a
single local consumer or publisher in isolation. Injecting the
same profile on OBU or RDS speed microservice would test
failure on one leg of the path, a different failure semantics
question. Injecting a non-hub broker would not reproduce hubwide loss of connectivity for this service. The campaign thus
exercises availability management on the highest-impact
single point along the defined E2E service path, which is
sufficient to validate whether precursor detection coupled to
recovery improves continuity on that path. AMF is intended to
be configured per microservice, therefore demonstrating the
closed loop on this central broker hub role establishes the
mechanism without repeating the same study on every other
pod, which would mainly vary component type rather than the
hub-outage hypothesis under test.

threshold. It is therefore not a model-compute delay of 14s,
but the reaction time of the monitoring and inference loop.
E2E SOT was still 4.41s, a multi-second, crash-aligned hole of
the same class as the 2.67s first-crash baseline in Fig 8. The
difference between these two first-crash runs is consistent with
ordinary variation in reactive restart and mesh reconnection,
i.e., the detector did not actuate recovery.
D. E2E Service Outage with AMF
We now enable the full AMF loop, i.e., the anomaly
detector output is consumed by the recovery module, which
performs on-demand standby scale-up followed by
switchover. Five injections were executed where injection
target, fault profile, and E2E SOT measurement are
unchanged from previous subsection VI-C. In all five runs the
replica creation agent acted while the primary broker pod was
still Running and serving. It confirmed the anomaly 18-27 s
after the onset of Phase 1, at proba_anomaly ≈ 0.999 in every
run, and issued the standby scale-up. This 18-27 s interval is
not model compute time, it is the observe-only detection
latency of Section VI-C (approximately 14s for the monitoring
and inference loop to cross threshold) plus the confirmation
debounce, which requires three consecutive above-threshold
samples at the 5s polling interval before any action is taken.
The agent then waited until the standby Deployment reported
Ready and only afterwards scaled the primary to zero, so both
replicas were Ready simultaneously for a non-zero interval
and there was no window in which neither broker was serving.
The interval from the scale-up decision to retirement of the
primary was 15-16 s across the five runs, set by the switchhold parameter of Section IV, this bounds the dual-Ready
overlap from above, because standby rollout time within that
interval was not separately instrumented. Switchover
completed 34-42 s after the onset of Phase 1, i.e., 78-86 s
before the scheduled escalation at the end of the 120s anomaly
phase.
Because the primary broker pod was retired mid-Phase 1,
the injected fault never materialized on that pod. The anomaly
load ended with the pod shortly after each switchover, and the
escalation command could not be delivered against a target
that no longer existed. No OOM occurred and no container
restart was recorded.
Table 9 reports, for each run, the largest outage, we call it
jitter, on the service path in the healthy (no-stress) window
immediately preceding injection, and the largest E2E SOT, in
the 30s after the first switchover. In runs 2 and 4, the OBU
client recorded no above-threshold inter-message gap near
switchover. Under the 250ms recording rule, those two runs
are upper bounds rather than measured zeros, and we report

them as < 0.25s. The probe was live across both windows,
samples tens of seconds before and after those switchovers.
The E2E SOT falls inside the jitter of healthy mesh. Across
no-stress windows in the campaign, jitter have a median of
0.77s and a maximum of 1.09s, whereas in the windows
following the five switchovers the median is 0.68s and the
maximum is 1.05s. The mean E2E SOT with AMF is 0.61s
which is smaller than the mean jitter i.e., 0.99s. We therefore
cannot claim zero downtime. We claim that AMF-driven
switchover produced no interruption larger than the jitter of
the healthy path on this testbed, in contrast to the 2.67s E2E
SOT without AMF in Fig 8.

VII. DISCUSSION AND THREATS TO VALIDITY
The proposed AMF is a generic availability management
loop, i.e., monitor, predict, recover, that can sit beside reactive
orchestration on microservice platforms. The AMF
deployment on the ToD testbed, as discussed in Sections IV–
VI, is one closed-loop instance and not the architecture’s
definition.
The architectural contrast with default K8s recovery is
therefore between when remediation is allowed to start.
Failover (Rf in Fig 7) begins after failure, whereas switchover
(Rs) begins during the anomaly window while the primary is
still serving. On the measured path, first-crash E2E SOT under
failover is 2.67 s (detect-only 4.41 s), whereas AMF
switchover completes in Phase 1 with a maximum recorded
payload gap of 1.05 s at cut-over and no OOM on the retired
primary. Treating the two recordings below the 250 ms
threshold as 0.25 s for summarization gives a mean maximum
gap of 0.61±0.39 s under AMF, inside the healthy-path jitter
of this mesh (mean 0.99±0.03 s). Those cut-over
measurements are therefore not classified as failover-class
E2E SOT. Break-before-make laboratory restorations on the
same probe produce E2E SOT of 2.36-3.93 s (mean 3.12 s)
and confirm that multi-second outages remain visible when
ordering is reversed. The exponential growth in Section VI-C,
from 2.67 s on the first crash to 83.31 s on the fifth, is repeated
in-place restart of one pod. Those crashes came too close
together for the restart wait to clear, so E2E SOT grew from
one crash to the next. The later crashes show that growth. The
proposed AMF is compared with the first crash, under the
same injection, fault profile, and E2E SOT measurement.
AMF also helps avoid the exponential growth on its own runs
because switchover removes the primary during the anomaly,
before the OOM, and each AMF run begins from a fresh
primary.
AMF can improve E2E availability only when the anomaly
lasts long enough for detection and gated recovery to finish
before the fault. That requirement follows from the monitorpredict-recover loop in Section III, not from this testbed alone.
Any instantiation of the AMF needs an anomaly window
longer than detection latency plus the recovery module’s gates
(threshold, debounce, switch hold). Abrupt faults with almost
no anomaly window fall back to reactive failover by
construction, so do missed detections if the live regime shifts
toward the low-recall P2 case of Section V. The staged profile
in Section VI exercises the case the architecture targets, i.e., a
usable anomaly window before fault. How long that window
is in a given deployment depends on the fault and the metrics
pipeline, it is not a promise that every memory related failure
warns for two minutes.
The five-nines (99.999%) availability requirement cited for
the ToD testbed [29] must be read against the same separation
of concerns. This campaign does not measure long-term
availability against that target, it rather measures second-scale
E2E SOT on one path under a controlled fault. In the present
instance, AMF finished switchover with tens of seconds of
Phase 1 remaining, so restart- and backoff-dominated outage
is removed from the critical path. Residual recorded gaps at
cut-over are then on the order of the healthy-path jitter of a
non-real-time, QoS 0 broker mesh on constrained nodes, not

the product of a late recovery decision. That jitter is a
limitation of the present testbed and service path, not of the
AMF loop. Whether a given deployment ultimately meets
five-nines depends on path timing, fault rate, and observation
horizon beyond a single staged injection, which these
experiments do not claim to establish.
Several instance limits follow without narrowing the
architecture itself. The evaluated recovery path is the edge-colocated subset of Section III, with a single switchover per
configuration and no role exchange afterward. No spurious
switchover appeared in the immediate pre-injection healthy
windows of the five AMF runs; quantifying false-alarm cost
under long healthy operation remains open. Extending the
loop across tiers, fault classes, and control-plane scales is
potential future work.
These limits set the scope of the claim, and the comparison
inside them is still worth reporting. The observations concern
the edge broker, through which every message on this service
path passes. The AMF and the default K8s recovery were
observed under the same injection, the same staged memory
and CPU stress, and the same live OBU measurement of E2E
SOT. Although five AMF runs do not estimate an E2E SOT
distribution for other CIoT systems, we believe they are
enough to support the conclusion for this testbed.
What another ToD service or another CIoT system may
take from this work is the condition, not the measured
seconds. Where a usable anomaly window exists and standby
switchover can finish before the fault, AMF has the potential
to keep that multi-second outage off the E2E service path. The
values reported here belong to this broker, this probe, and this
stress profile. A fault that reaches OOM before detection,
gating, and switchover completion follows default K8s
recovery, as the architecture already specifies.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This article proposed an AMF, a generic availability
management framework that couples resource monitoring and
ML-based anomaly detection with proactive recovery for
microservice-based safety-critical CIoT systems. Building on
a prior architectural proposal [11], we described the monitorpredict-recover loop, selected an anomaly detector using
offline protocols that include a change of operating regime
[10], and evaluated one closed-loop instance on a three-tier
tele-operated driving testbed under a staged memory and CPU
exhaustion scenario.
The experimental results clarify what the architecture
contributes. Default K8s recovery leaves a first-crash E2E
SOT of 2.67s and, under repeated in-place restarts, E2E SOT
of tens of seconds once restart backoff accumulates. With the
proposed AMF, switchover completes during the anomaly
window, i.e., across five runs, the maximum recorded E2E
SOT at switchover is at most 1.05s (within healthy-path jitter),
the injected fault never materializes as an OOM on the retired
primary pod, so the backoff-dominated regime is never
entered. Furthermore, the healthy-path jitter is a property of
the present testbed and service path, not evidence that the
AMF decision was late, i.e., switchover finished with tens of
seconds of the anomaly window still unused. Meeting the fivenines availability requirement remains a joint outcome of
