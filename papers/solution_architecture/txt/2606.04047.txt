Abstract. In recent years, the detection of anti-patterns in microservice architecture has gained traction, particularly to identify instances of
Microservice Architectural Degradation. In such tasks, the microservice
architecture is often modeled as a network of microservice dependencies.
Temporal community detection methods have been proposed to analyze
the community structure of networks that evolve in time. We performed
temporal community detection within the microservice architecture of
six releases of the train-ticket benchmark and analyzed the composition
of the discovered communities and their activities over time. We observed
a stable architecture with a clear separation of services into two communities, which we could identify with two business processes performed by
the system.
Keywords: Microservices · Community detection · Temporal networks
· Wrong cuts · Knot service

1

Introduction

Over the past decade, the adoption of Microservice Architecture (MSA) has led
to the identification of various architectural patterns and anti-patterns to prevent Microservice Architectural Degradation [2]. Specific anti-patterns include
Wrong cuts, when different responsibilities and business processes are incorrectly
divided among the services, and Knot services, when services are too coupled to
each other and communicate with too many other services [8]. Detecting such
anti-patterns often involves modeling the microservice system as a network of
connected services [4,3]. Moreover, in the context of architectural degradation,
it is the evolution of such networks that is of interest [5].
In the field of network science, the study of temporal networks (TNs) has
gained traction as a powerful way to model and analyze networks that evolve
[12,5]. Specifically in MSA, Bakhtin et al. have applied temporal network methods to networks of microservice architecture [5] and developer collaboration [6].

In particular, temporal community detection allows to identify communities that
persist or re-emerge through time [10]. In MSA networks, community detection
can help us identify groups of services that communicate a lot and assess whether
such groupings correspond to business processes [9], or if their dependencies are
the result of improper division of responsibilities or unoptimized behavior [11,13].
Therefore, leveraging the promising results obtained previously [5,6], in this
work, we analyze the temporal community structure [10] of several releases of
train-ticket OSS benchmark. Our contributions include the following: (i) we
performed temporal community detection in the reconstructed MSA; (ii) we
analyzed the temporal activity of the discovered communities and noted stable
community structure; (iii) we observed the composition of microservice communities and how they reflect the business processes of the system.
Due to space constraints, background is provided in the Online Appendix 3 .

2

Related Work

Community detection among microservices within MSA is an unexplored direction. The only works that we could identify are due to Khodabandeh et al.
[13], analyzing the community structure of microservice clusters in the Alibaba
production environment, and Gaidels and Kirikova [9], who analyzed an MSA
network with several community detection algorithms and identified the context
of each community. Crucially, temporal community detection has not been leveraged for the assessment of microservice architectural evolution, while Bakhtin et
al. [5] only applied temporal centrality for this purpose. Abufouda and Abukwaik
[1] argued for properly adopting TN methods in Empirical Software Engineering. However, their review focused only on applications of TNs for developer
collaboration networks. Bakhtin et al. [6] have leveraged temporal community
detection to analyze the structure and trends of TN of developer collaboration
on microservices in an OSS benchmark, determining the groups of collaborating developers at each release. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to
apply temporal community detection to reconstructed microservice architecture
networks in the context of Microservice Architectural Degradation.

Empirical Study Design

The goal of our study is to analyze how the temporal community structure
of microservices evolves over time and on what basis the microservices communities are formed. Therefore, we defined the following Research Questions (RQs):
RQ1 : Do the structure and activity of the microservice communities evolve?
Since microservice architecture strives for high cohesion, low coupling design of the system, the presence of strongly coupled groups of microservices in
the MSA network could indicate improper decomposition of the system into

microservices or the presence of certain anti-patterns. Moreover, temporal variations in the community structure indicate that microservices change their dependencies from one release to the next, suggesting the instability of the architecture. Observing the trends in community activity and structure over time
thus allows us to judge the stability of the architecture. Since the approach of
Gauvin et al. [10] provides us with the communities’ activity profiles over the
analyzed time-span, we can observe if communities have near constant activity
in the analyzed releases or if their activity varies a lot. We can leverage standard
deviation for this analysis, and thus formulate the following null and alternative
hypotheses: (H01 ) the activity values of each community stay within a 3-sigma
interval around the mean; (H11 ) at least one community activity exceeds a 3sigma interval around the mean.
While the activity patterns of the discovered communities show the stability
of the overall system, membership of particular services in different communities
could serve as an indicator of Wrong cuts or Knot-like behavior:
RQ2 : Do microservices form communities based on business processes?
Once we have determined the composition of each community, we can analyze whether these communities consist of microservices performing related
business processes. For example, in the case of the train-ticket benchmark [16],
we could assume the services for booking a ticket, reserving a seat, and checking the timetable would be in the same community, since they would all be
invoked when purchasing a ticket. Moreover, since the method by Gauvin et
al. [10] provides overlapping communities, i.e., the same service can belong to
several communities, we can check if there are microservices that simultaneously
belong to different communities focused on different business processes. If this
is the case, it could hint at an improper service decomposition and responsibility allocation within the microservice system. We thus formulate the following
hypotheses: (H02 ) all microservices belong to exactly one community; (H12 ) at
least one microservice belongs to several communities.
Data Collection. We have previously compiled a dataset of OSS MSS
projects suitable for network analysis by reconstructing their architectures the
Code2DFD tool [14,4]. In a follow-up study [5], we determined the OSS MSS
projects whose architecture evolved throughout development. We discovered that
the train-ticket 4 microservice benchmark project is the only available and reconstructed system that shows significant structural evolution. We thus leveraged
the networks of all the train-ticket releases reconstructed in our previous work
[4,5] as the TN for our analysis.
Data Analysis. We performed temporal community detection in the TN
of train-ticket, applying the method by Gauvin et al. [10] with our previously
developed implementation [6].
To determine the best number of communities to fit, we leveraged the core
consistency metric [7] similar to [6,10]. According to the original authors, the
core consistency values between the maximum of 100 and 90 are considered a
good fit [10,7]. For each choice of the number of communities from 2 to 6, we

4

Results

In this section, we report the results of temporal community detection in the TN
of train-ticket architecture and answer the RQs. The two detected communities
are denoted consistently as blue round, solid stems and orange crossed, dashed
stems in Figure 1.
Looking at the temporal activity of the communities (RQ1 , see Figure 1
in the Online Appendix), we observe that both blue and orange communities
have high activity, consistently above 0.8 in all the releases. The activity on
the interval v0.0.2 - v0.1.0 is constant, indicating identical, unchanging architecture, as noted by Bakhtin et al. [5]. On this interval, blue community
appears to be dominating the orange community, since the former increased
its activity while the latter decreased compared to release v0.0.1.
To answer RQ1 , we computed the mean and variance of the temporal activities of each community. The exact temporal activity values of the blue community
are 0.99, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 0.97, providing a mean of 0.993 and standard deviation
of 0.012. Thus, the 3-sigma interval is [0.956; 1.000]. For the orange community,
the values are 0.84, 0.82, 0.82, 0.82, 0.82, 0.81 with the mean 0.822, standard
deviation 0.0098, and the 3-sigma interval [0.793; 0.851]. Thus, for both communities, we fail to reject the null hypotheses H01 , since all temporal activity
values stay within the respective 3-sigma intervals.
RQ1 answer: We observe stable community structure across the releases
of train-ticket, with all activity values within 3-sigma interval around the mean.
Considering the composition of the temporal communities (RQ2 ), we
determined the membership of microservices in the communities by setting a
neutral threshold of 0.5 on the computed membership strength (Figure 1).
Observing the blue community, we notice that it corresponds to preserve,
preserve-other, and travel-plan services making calls to services like seat, station,
ticketinfo, and travel2 services. The aim of the preserve service is to preserve the
ordered ticket data in the database, thus this community seems to correspond to

Discussion

In this section, we discuss the results of our RQs, their implications for practitioners and researchers, as well as address the threats to validity.
Stability of the temporal community reconstruction. Overall, we have
observed a stable architecture of the train-ticket project, as indicated by the
stable, almost constant temporal activity of the communities (RQ1 ) and
clear separation of the microservices into communities (RQ2 ). However,
the fact that we could only fit a community structure to the TN of releases v0.0.1 v0.2.0, excluding the latest release v1.0.0, could itself indicate problems with the

architecture stability due to sudden changes in the MSA network. The change
between releases v0.2.0 and v1.0.0 includes the refactoring of food-related services, which could have resulted in noticeable changes in microservice dependencies in that region of the MSA, and since the whole MSA consists of
“only” 42 services, this could have had a significant effect on the performance of the community detection algorithm. We believe we would see more
robust results if we considered an industrial-scale system with a long history of
releases, since in an Agile environment, releases occur frequently and incrementally. On the other hand, temporal activity was constant between the releases
v0.0.2 and 0.1.0, which is the interval which we previously identified as having
no modifications in the architecture [5]. Thus, the leveraged method is robust to constant data and produces stable, non-fluctuating results when given
a temporal network with identical snapshots.
Membership strength paints a nuanced picture. While we resorted to
setting a threshold on the membership strength to interpret microservice communities and test our hypothesis, we also noticed that many services have non-zero
membership strength below the 0.5 threshold (Figure 1), and such observations
could still provide value to architects and developers. Thus, the raw membership
strength values can enable more fine-grained interpretation. For example,
rebook and route-plan services have membership strength in the blue community
close to 0.5. Moreover, services belong to several communities, suggesting that
they participate in several business processes. This could indicate an inappropriate division of responsibilities between the services. Furthermore, some
services have both the incoming and outgoing membership strength in the same
community , like food, travel, and travel2 services in the blue community. This
could indicate instances of Wrong cuts, Knot-like, or Hub-like behaviour
[3], since these services appear to be both called by and to call many other
services. We believe that performing this kind of analysis on a large industrial
system will enable more detailed observation of communities and their identification with the business process. Moreover, with more communities detected
overall, the membership of a microservice in several communities would be easier
to connect to bad practices or anti-patterns, with many overlapping communities
potentially indicating higher severity.
Implications for researchers and practitioners. The proposed temporal
community analysis can provide valuable insights to practitioners who wish
to analyze how their MSA is evolving. It could highlight positive trends, e.g.,
architecture remaining stable, or negative trends, e.g., services not related by
a business process or function forming a community or a community significantly
increasing its activity. For researchers, future directions could include considering more closely what kinds of insights could be extracted from such an
approach, in particular when analyzing a system that contains more communities that show sudden changes in activity, and how they relate to anti-patterns
such as Hub-like or Knot services or Wrong cuts [3,8]. Moreover, the applicability of other temporal community detection approaches with different
parameters, constraints, and outputs could be considered.

Threats to validity. We discuss the threats to the validity following the
guidelines by Wohlin et al. [15]. Construct validity: we leveraged the dataset
of several versions of the train-ticket benchmark as reconstructed in our previous work [4,5] with the Code2DFD tool [14] as well as our own implementation
of the community detection methods [6]. These tools have their limitations as
research prototypes. Internal validity: we had to rely on train-ticket as the
only MSA that is reconstructed for only seven releases with some architectural
variability. However, even this variability is limited, with several releases having
an identical architectural structure. Moreover, since the system contains only 42
microservices with many dependencies among them, we could only identify two
communities, where a lot of services belong with a small, but non-zero strength.
External validity: the train-ticket benchmark project is one of the biggest
OSS MSA projects, and the only one that we could analyze in this work. However, it still does not represent an industrial-scale, in-production microservice
system. We were thus careful not to overgeneralize our observations and conclusions , aiming instead to showcase the leveraged community detection algorithm.
Conclusion validity: we are not closely familiar with all the services, functionalities, and business processes of train-ticket, so the mapping between the
communities and business processes is subject to interpretation bias.

6

Conclusion

In this work, we performed temporal community detection within a temporal
MSA network of six releases of the train-ticket benchmark project and identified
two communities with near-constant temporal activity. Several services have incoming and outgoing memberships to several communities, which we identified
with distinct business processes. For practitioners, the proposed methodology
could highlight positive and negative trends in MSA evolution, such as architecture remaining stable or services not related by a business process forming a
community. For researchers, this work opens up future directions for studying
how MSA community structure and service membership interplay with bad practices and anti-patterns. Furthermore, different community detection algorithms
could be analyzed and compared.
