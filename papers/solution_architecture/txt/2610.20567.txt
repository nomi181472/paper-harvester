Abstract—Cloud services rapidly shift towards independentlymanaged microservices architectures. This independence may
lead to temporary microservices miscoordination, resulting in
overloads and rejections of valuable transactions. This weakness
may further be exploited by attackers to launch overload attacks
inflicting performance and cost damages.
Retry operations are a key machinery aiming at overcoming
seldom rejections. However, during overload periods they: a) Fail
protecting valuable transactions from rejections, and b) Trigger
large-scale performance-degrading retry storms.
We propose simple modifications in retry operations, overcoming these deficiencies by transforming retries into a rejection prioritization mechanism, DiffTry (Differential Retries). It is based
on a simple, lightweight, distributed and stateless design and
requires neither overload sensing nor microservices coordination.
We develop an analytical model capturing DiffTry’s effectiveness for two services in tandem, then generalize it to networks of
services. Our theoretical framework lets practitioners quantify,
for a given network, the resulting prioritization and rejection
rates, and yields formal guarantees that DiffTry substantially reduces the impact of both incidental and adversarial orchestrated
overloads. We validate these predictions in simulation and on a
live Kubernetes deployment, where DiffTry is a simple dropin service-mesh plugin requiring no application changes; the
measured rejection closely matches the analysis.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Cloud service providers have largely converged on microservice architectures, decomposing monolithic applications into
hundreds, often thousands, of loosely coupled services [1],
[2]. This paradigm allows each component (microservice) to
scale elastically in response to demand, implementing capacity
scaling, retries, timeouts, and circuit breakers autonomously.
While these mechanisms achieve high microservice independence and simplicity in design, their coordination is
challenging and their operations can get miscoordinated. During periods of miscoordination, in particular, miscoordination
of service capacity, some microservices may become overloaded due to their service capacity not up-scaled to the load
generated by upstream services. Such overload periods may
result in significant losses of transactions, due to their blocking/rejection by the downstream overloaded microservice.
The negative economic effects of such rejection periods
are further compounded when they affect high-value (highpriority) transactions. These may include premium transactions (such as a customer with a loaded cart, or a preferred
customer), mission critical transactions and others.
Two main overload-prevention methodologies are used in
the literature and in practice: (i) local auto-scaling approaches
(e.g., [3]), and (ii) network-wide centralized approaches (e.g.,
TopFull [4], which offers network-wide source throttling).

In this paper, we propose DiffTry as a mechanism for service
differentiation and load control using retries.
A. DiffTry: Differential Retries
DiffTry (Differential Retries) is a simple and stateless
mechanism for differential operation of retries across transaction classes, yielding differentiation (and prioritization) in
their rejection rates. In a nutshell, DiffTry intervenes right
at the point where rejections occur, protecting high-priority
transactions during overload by assigning class-specific retry
limits. The key idea is to assign a different number of retry
attempt limits to different classes or tiers of requests. For
example, a premium user may be allowed up to three attempts,
while a ”bypassing user” may be limited to one. These limits
can be configured per route, user tier or traffic class (e.g., with
priority carried in an HTTP header), and enforced locally at
the retry decision point (e.g., the sender API).
By embedding the prioritization logic directly into the retry
process, DiffTry eliminates the need for explicit coordination
or tight coupling between services. It effectively transforms retries into a distributed rejection-prioritization control, enabling
systems to both prioritize critical flows and suppress retry
amplification in a local, coordination-free, scalable manner.
During attacks, this may have a particularly critical effect,
as it protects highly valued transactions from large volumes of
attacking requests (naturally classified as low-priority tasks).
Furthermore, without requiring explicit overload detection or
sensor, the mechanism has an effect only under high-load or
overload conditions and remains effectively silent otherwise.
To illustrate DiffTry under overload, consider a front-end
Service A and a downstream Service B (Fig. 1). Requests
are labeled by priority, either High (e.g., premium or latencysensitive users) or Low (e.g., a bypassing user or batch job),
and Service A retries them after timeouts or errors.

B. Main Contributions: DiffTry Analysis and Results
Our paper focuses on establishing and analyzing the behavior of DiffTry. This should serve designers in (i) Gaining trust
in this new machinery, (ii) Using the analysis to optimize the
operations of DiffTry and plan the system performance.
We start by presenting the DiffTry mechanism, properties,
and model in Sec. III. Then, we analyze a sub-system in
which the upstream Service A sends traffic to the downstream
Service B (Sec. IV). We first focus on the overload region, and
using a fluid-model approximation we derive the per-stream
rejections and throughput. We then consider the no-overload
region, where rejection depends on the underlying queueing
model. We show how queueing-specific blocking expressions
can be plugged into our retry analysis, to derive the perstream rejections; we demonstrate this on the M/M/1/m model.
Simulation results demonstrate the model accuracy in Sec. V.
Having established the analysis for a single overloaded node
we turn to deal with networks (Sec. VI). This is motivated
by real microservice systems, which often contain tens or
hundreds of services, e.g., Uber [1] and Netflix [2]. There are
two key motivating questions behind this analysis: 1) Whether
the plausible behavior (low rejections for high-priority transactions) derived for a single node would hold under an arbitrary
network. In particular, could high-priority class enjoy low

rejections in one node, but affect the traffic in a way that
it will suffer high rejection rate on other (downstream) nodes.
2) Approximate the network rejections under DiffTry.
We adopt the well known open-network model [6], combined with flow classes/priorities, to model the traffic in the
network. We then extend it to model DiffTry. By incorporating
the rejection equations we generate a set of equations whose
solution (computed in a simple iterative approach) yields the
rejection rate of each priority class in each node. This allows
deriving the end-to-end rejection rates. We further prove that
the iterative approach is always guaranteed to converge to a
solution and derive network rejection probabilities. We also
investigate whether the solution is guaranteed to be unique
(Sec. VI-E). We derive a set of conditions under which
uniqueness is guaranteed. In particular it holds for a wide set
of applications where the transaction flow graph is represented
by a DAG (examples include Online Boutique [7] and WeChat
[8]). For other cases, we prove uniqueness for some cases, but
the general theoretical problem remains open in the context
of this work; for those cases, we conduct an extensive set of
experiments, all of which yield a unique solution.
We establish monotonicity results (Sec. VI-G) showing that
network-wide class-level success rates increase monotonically
with retry limits. This establishes the effectiveness of DiffTry
as well as allowing establishing performance lower bounds.
Lastly, in Sec. VII we study DiffTry’s network-level behavior numerically and validate it on a live Kubernetes deployment. There, DiffTry is a drop-in service-mesh plugin
that needs no application-code changes, and the measured
rejection closely matches our analysis, confirming that the
local mechanism composes into network-wide prioritization
on a real system. Concluding remarks are given in Sec. VIII.
II. R ELATED W ORK
Traffic and overload control are challenging in microservice
systems due to miscoordination among independently autoscaled services. Prior work shows that uncoordinated scaling
decisions can lead to instability and excessive cost under burst
workloads [9], [10]. Several recent studies address these challenges using centralized control. For example, [4] performs
top-down overload control at the application ingress, [11] uses
an application-wide controller to set global performance and
resource targets, and [12] relies on centralized request routing
and traffic engineering across services and replicas.
In cloud systems, retry mechanisms are tied to service
communication protocols, including HTTP [13] and RPC [14].
Major cloud providers integrate automated retry logic directly
into client and service libraries [15], [16], and service mesh
platforms provide built-in retry handling at the infrastructure
layer [17], [18], [19], [20]; all with a constant limit per route.
Traditional priority mechanisms (e.g., [6]) are offered by
cloud providers [21], [22], but their capabilities differ from
DiffTry’s. They attach the priority to the queue discipline,
whereas DiffTry attaches it to the retry budget. They were
designed to prioritize transaction processing and are applied
at the service point (of Service B, in Fig. 1), where transactions

exit the queue and enter service, as opposed to DiffTry, which
is applied before queue entrance, at the rejection point. As
such, unless coordinated with other mechanisms, they would
have difficulties handling rejections. Also, in no-overload situations, they may unnecessarily starve low-priority transactions
and inflict long delays. An alternative approach, consisting of
prioritization of rejections at the entrance to Service B, would
be challenging to implement as well, since it would require
coordination with the buffer management of Service B.
Recently, major cloud providers and service-mesh platforms
have warned that retries can trigger retry storms and lead to
performance problems and security threats [23], [24], [25].
However, systematic analysis of retries under load remains
limited, with only a small number of studies examining its impact on throughput and latency in microservice systems [26],
[19]. DAGOR [8] also protects high-priority requests, but
sheds at admission, requiring each server to sense overload and
propagate its admission level upstream; RetryGuard [27] prevents retry storms, but without differentiating classes. DiffTry
protects important jobs through the retry budget, needs neither
overload detection nor coordination, and so acts immediately.
III. DiffTry: M ECHANISM O PERATION AND M ODEL
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the key idea of DiffTry is to
assign different retry-attempt limits to requests from different
priority classes. By doing so, DiffTry ensures that high-priority
requests receive preferential treatment under overload, while,
at the same time, suppresses retry storms. DiffTry is especially
effective during miscoordination-induced overload periods and
helps reduce the economic costs associated with it. It addresses
three key problems in existing microservices applications:
1) Insensitivity to priority: High-priority requests are rejected as much as low-priority ones.
2) Retry amplification: Excessive retries inflate load and
waste capacity and CPU.
3) Over-scaling: Retried low-priority traffic triggers unnecessary scaling, increasing cost.
This simple differentiation allows DiffTry to operate effectively across a range of conditions and it possesses the
following properties: (1) During overload times, DiffTry shifts
the mass of rejections caused by overload periods toward lowpriority (non-latency-sensitive or non-revenue-critical) tasks,
thereby protecting critical traffic. (2) During attack times, it
significantly reduces the economic damages of an attack by
preferentially rejecting low-priority requests that would otherwise overwhelm the system. (3) During no-overload times,
the mechanism remains non-intrusive and does not interfere
with normal traffic, while automatically becoming effective
when overload conditions arise. (4) DiffTry is designed for
distributed operation, without centralized coordination or
explicit overload detection, and relying only on local retry
decisions. (5) Through retry suppression, DiffTry limits excessive retries from low-priority flows, thereby reducing the
risk of retry storms and stabilizing system behavior.
We formally model DiffTry as follows: Service A receives
requests from P distinct sources where the arrival rate of

class p is λp . It processes the incoming requests and forwards
them to Service B (as in Fig. 1), whose processing rate is µ
requests/sec. If Service B is unable to accept a request due to
overload, Service A retains the request and retries it later.
Each flow p is assigned a retry budget kp . This means that
for every p ∈ {1, . . . , P } flow p may retry a failed request up
to kp times. Let λip denote the rate of requests from stream
p currently in their i-th retry attempt, for i = 1, . . . , kp and
p = 1, . . . , P . A request of type p is dropped permanently
if it reaches its maximum retry budget (kp ) without being
successfully served. During the first kp attempts, it is eligible
for retries, governed by the retry logic of Service A.
Using this model, we next study DiffTry’s operation using
a two-tier microservice system consisting of two services. In
Sec. VI, we extend this model to account for an entire network.

DiffTry in two interacting services. A general system may
consist of a network of tens/hundreds of services (e.g., TopFull

an analysis of DiffTry over a general network is needed.

flow model (as used in Jackson networks [33], [6], and in

B. Experiment and numerical examination results
1) End-to-end rejection rate (Fig. 5(b)): Without prioritization, a high-value request is rejected almost as heavily as
the attacking flood (≈ 54% under overload). DiffTry reduces
the high-priority rejection rate by an order of magnitude under
moderate overload (∼ 10× at ρ ≈ 1.4), and still by ∼ 3.5× at
the heaviest flood (ρ ≈ 2.1, from ≈ 54% to ≈ 15%), while
the rejection of the low-priority flood rises only slightly (to
≈ 61%). DiffTry thus induces a clear and persistent end-toend differentiation between the classes.
Crucially, the measured rejection on the live experiment
tracks the numerical fixed-point prediction (≈ 0.03 difference), confirming that the local per-node mechanism manifests
as a network-wide effect on a real system that the analysis
predicts well. Below capacity (ρ < 1) both classes see
≈ 0 rejection, so DiffTry is silent when the system is not
overloaded, consistent with the single-service results of Sec. V.
2) Latency and resource consumption (Fig. 5(c,d)): Without prioritization, the uniform retries back the over-offered
flood up into a retry storm whose median latency reaches
≈ 270 ms. DiffTry caps the low-priority budget so the flood
fails fast, collapsing its latency to ≈ 20 ms, and the highpriority latency to ≈ 70 ms (∼ 3.6× lower than baseline) –
the intrinsic cost of its k=5 retries. Thus DiffTry drastically
decreases the latency of both classes far below the baseline.

DiffTry adds negligible overhead—a single, stateless retrybudget filter—and instead reduces the mesh’s internal work.
Each external request fans out to ≈ 3.8 internal gRPC calls;
under overload the uniform retries inflate this to ≈ 6.4 RPCs
per request, whereas DiffTry drives it below the fan-out floor,
to ≈ 3.3: the flood fails fast at the first congested service
and never issues the deeper downstream calls. Per successfully
served request the gap widens further (≈ 13.8 vs ≈ 7.2).
Because rejection is enforced by rate limiters, before a request
reaches the service logic, CPU and memory stay comparable.
3) Conclusions: These results reproduce, on a real deployment, the prioritization and protection predicted by the
analysis. Equally important, they show that DiffTry is trivial
to adopt using a simple low-overhead service-mesh plug-in.
VIII. C ONCLUSIONS
We introduced DiffTry, a distributed, stateless mechanism
that embeds priority into retry logic, turning retries into a tool
for differentiating request rejections under overload without
explicit overload signaling or inter-service coordination.
We developed an analytical framework that lets practitioners
quantify the prioritization behavior and rejection rates. We
proved convergence of its solution and established conditions
and evidence for uniqueness, including DAG service graphs.
We further proved monotonicity of class-level success rates
in the retry limits, establishing DiffTry’s effectiveness and
performance bounds even without guaranteed uniqueness.
We validated the analysis both numerically at the network
level and on a live Kubernetes/Istio deployment, where DiffTry
is a drop-in service-mesh plugin whose measured per-class rejection matches the numerical prediction, requires no changes
to application code, and incurs negligible overhead.
