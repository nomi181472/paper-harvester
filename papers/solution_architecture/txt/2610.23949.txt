DS-Net: A Dynamic Split Network for Edge-Cloud
Collaborative Inference

arXiv:2610.23949v1 [cs.LG] 9 Oct 2026

Xinyun Zhang, Li Wang*, Senior Member, IEEE, Lei Jin*, Lianming Xu, and Zhu Han, Fellow, IEEE,

Abstract—Collaborative inference serves as a critical methodology in edge intelligence. Existing collaborative inference methods
operate by splitting the deep neural network (DNN) model
between edge devices and cloud servers. However, these methods
need to load different models for multiple split points when
the communication or device changes, thus resulting in long
switching times and substantial storage overhead on the edge
side. To address this issue, we propose a Dynamic Split Network
(DS-Net), which not only enables efficient split point switching
but also enhances the collaborative speed and accuracy across
various scenarios. Specifically, we design a dynamic switching
framework that enables flexible selection among multiple split
points within a single model, thereby reducing storage, memory,
and switching overhead. To mitigate the feature distribution shifts
and task-relevant information loss caused by dynamic splitting
and high-ratio compression, we further propose a hierarchical
compensation mechanism. Local alignment calibrates the decoded feature distribution at each split point, while task-specific
global compensation restores task-relevant information at the
global level. In addition, we incorporate sample-adaptive global
compensation selection into split-point selection based on sample complexity and runtime resource conditions. Experimental
results demonstrate that DS-Net reduces memory overhead by
74.6% and achieves millisecond-level switching latency. Moreover,
under a 10 Mbps bandwidth constraint on the Nano platform,
DS-Net outperforms state-of-the-art collaborative methods by
achieving 1.2% higher accuracy on the COCO dataset, reducing
latency by 42.5%, and improving throughput by a factor of 1.8×.
Index Terms—Edge-cloud collaborative inference, model splitting, feature compensation.

In CI, the optimal split point is generally determined by
jointly considering the computational capacity of edge devices
and the communication bandwidth between the edge and the
cloud. The selection of split points generally follows layerwise [8], [9] or graph-based [10] strategies. Furthermore,

model splitting [11], [12]. In practical edge-cloud deployments, runtime conditions are often dynamic. Fluctuations in
bandwidth may arise from wireless channel variations, device
mobility, or network contention, while the available edge
computational capacity can vary due to workloads or energy.
Because the latency-optimal split point depends on edge computation, feature transmission, and cloud computation jointly, a
fixed split point chosen for a predefined scenario may become
suboptimal under changing conditions. Thus, dynamic splitpoint switching is essential to sustain an efficient latency-

accuracy trade-off during continuous inference. However, frequent split-point switching also introduces new challenges. As
illustrated in Fig. 1, current methods typically require training
and deploying independent models for each possible split
point. This approach incurs significant switching latency and
memory overhead on edge devices. Moreover, intermediate
features at different split points exhibit distinct distributions,
making the effects of compression highly split-dependent.
Therefore, reducing accuracy degradation across dynamically
selected split points while avoiding repeated model switching
remains a challenging problem.
To address these limitations, we propose DS-Net, a unified adaptive collaborative inference framework. First, DSNet integrates multiple candidate split points into a shared
backbone and inserts each point with switchable EncoderDecoder and Identity paths. At runtime, only the EncoderDecoder path of the selected split point is activated, avoiding
the deployment and loading of multiple models. Second, we
design a hierarchical compensation mechanism to mitigate
feature distortion caused by high-ratio compression. At each
split point, local alignment calibrates the decoded feature
distribution using the channel-wise mean and standard deviation of the original feature. At the global task level, taskspecific compensation extracts spatial information from earlylayer features to enhance discriminative and object-related
regions. Specifically, saliency and foreground-aware masks
are generated for classification and detection, respectively, to
recover task-relevant information not captured by local alignment alone. Finally, we incorporate sample-adaptive global
compensation selection into the split-point selection process.
The split point and compensation state are jointly determined
based on sample complexity, real-time bandwidth, and device
computing capacity, thereby improving the latency-accuracy
trade-off under dynamic runtime conditions.
We conduct extensive experiments on Jetson TX2 and Nano
edge devices with an RTX 4090 cloud server. DS-Net is evaluated using four backbone networks and three vision datasets
under both controlled and real-world bandwidth conditions.
We compare DS-Net with state-of-the-art collaborative inference and lightweight methods in terms of accuracy, latency,
throughput, and switching overhead. The results demonstrate
that DS-Net enables millisecond-level split-point switching,
improves inference efficiency, and effectively alleviates the
accuracy degradation caused by model splitting and feature
compression.

(DAGs) and apply graph optimization algorithms to find split
points. Yan et al. [22] formulated split point selection as
an optimal stopping problem and combined it with model
placement strategies. Mu et al. [23] achieved fast accuracy
estimation via sparsity control and regression-based prediction
models. Certain methods [24], [25] used the reinforcement

Feature Compression. Existing studies mainly reduced communication overhead in split inference through fixed partitioning and intermediate feature compression [27]–[30]. DeepJSCC [31] jointly optimized feature pruning and wireless
transmission, while Assine et al. [32] designed a configurable
encoder to trade off bandwidth, computation, and accuracy.
AECNN [33] and SpikeBottleNet [34] compressed intermediate features through channel attention and spiking representations, respectively. Nazir et al. [35] introduced joint sourcetask decoding for distributed semantic segmentation, directly
mapping compressed bottleneck features to segmentation outputs. RCIF [36] improved collaborative inference robustness
under lossy wireless links, while Azizian et al. [37] enhanced feature compressibility by suppressing task-irrelevant
information. Wang et al. [38] developed DeepAdaIn-Net,
which combined high-ratio feature compression, bandwidthaware configuration, and feature compensation for low-latency
AR inference. DVFO [39] split intermediate feature maps
by attention-estimated importance, preserving critical features
while quantizing less important ones to reduce transmission
overhead with limited accuracy loss.
Joint Splitting-Compression Designs In edge-cloud collaborative inference, model splitting and feature compression are
closely coupled. The selected split point determines the data
size of transmitted intermediate features, while compression
directly affects both communication latency and task accuracy.
Several studies have jointly considered these two aspects. BottleNet++ [40] introduced autoencoder-based bottleneck units
at different split points to reduce feature transmission. AutoSplit [41] combined graph partitioning with mixed-precision
quantization under device and accuracy constraints. Shi et
al. [42] generated multiple pruned sub-models offline and
dynamically selected the optimal configuration online. CNNPC [43] combined channel pruning with 8-bit quantization
to search for the optimal split point and compression ratio.
SplitNets [44] designed compression modules on the sensor
side and recovery modules on the aggregator side, but its complex optimization process potentially compromised training

In this section, we conduct preliminary experiments to
analyze these challenges and motivate the design of DS-Net.
We first evaluate the switching overhead of existing splitspecific deployment strategies. To evaluate this overhead, we
reimplement BottleNet++ [40] on different network architectures and deploy the obtained split-specific models on the
TX2 platform. As shown in Table I, independent split-point

model design that supports fast split-point adaptation without
repeated model loading.

by using a feature reconstruction network (FRN) to reconstruct

B. Dynamic Switching Framework

A. Overview of the proposed DS-Net
Fig. 3 illustrates the overall architecture of DS-Net. DSNet first introduces a dynamic switching framework, in which
each candidate split point is equipped with a switchable
encoder-decoder path and an identity path. At runtime, only
the encoder-decoder path corresponding to the selected split
point is activated to compress and transmit the intermediate
feature, while the remaining candidate points forward their features through the identity paths. For FPN-based multi-branch
detection networks, DS-Net retains only the main backbone
feature for transmission and deploys a feature reconstruction
network (FRN) on the cloud side to approximately reconstruct
the multi-scale branch features required by the neck. To
mitigate the distortion introduced by high-ratio compression,
1-bit quantization, and feature reconstruction, DS-Net further
incorporates local alignment and task-specific global compensation. Local feature alignment calibrates the decoded feature
distribution using the channel-wise mean and standard deviation of the original split feature, whereas task-specific global
compensation enhances task-relevant spatial responses using a
saliency mask for classification or a foreground-aware mask
for detection. Finally, a sample-adaptive selection mechanism
jointly determines the split point and the global compensation
state according to input complexity, transmission bandwidth,
and device computing capacity.

We propose a dynamic switching framework that seamlessly
supports both single-branch and multi-branch networks. In this
framework, each candidate split point is associated with a

dedicated inference path, ensuring that feature compression

the encoder–decoder modules to deliver targeted, distributionaware compression for intermediate features at each split point,
thereby alleviating feature mismatch and reducing accuracy

module across multiple split points. By integrating all candidate split point models into a unified architecture, the framework allows for fast, runtime selection of split points without
redundant parameter loading, significantly minimizing both

Switchable Encoder-Decoder Module. To enable dynamic
split selection, a switchable encoder–decoder module is integrated after each candidate split point. Each module consists
of two parallel pathways: an encoder–decoder path designed
for feature compression and recovery, and an identity path
that allows features to bypass compression entirely. During
runtime, the split point selection mechanism activates only
the encoder–decoder path associated with the selected split
point, thereby ensuring that compression is applied precisely
at that location. For all other (non-selected) split points, the
identity branch remains active, passing the original feature
maps unchanged. Although all inference paths share the same
backbone network, each split point is equipped with its own
encoder–decoder module, allowing compression strategies to
be tailored to the specific feature characteristics at that depth
while maintaining high efficiency and flexibility.

Feature Reconstruction on the Cloud. The Feature Reconstruction Network (FRN) draws inspiration from SPReconfig [33], utilizing an upsampling-based architecture to
reconstruct low-level features from a single high-level feature map. In DS-Net, the FRN progressively upsamples the
received feature representation to restore the spatial resolution
required by complex vision tasks. It should be noted that
the reconstruction modules in the FRN are not designed as
exact mathematical inverses of the corresponding backbone
operations. This is due to the inherent non-invertibility of common backbone components, including convolution, C2/C2f/C3
blocks, SPPF, and pooling. Therefore, the FRN is formulated
as an upsampling-based reconstruction module, aiming to
recover task-relevant feature spatial resolutions rather than
exactly reconstructing the original intermediate features.
Table III summarizes the reconstruction design of the FRN.
For convolutional operations, PixelShuffle-based upsampling
or transposed convolution is used for feature reconstruction. When PixelShuffle is adopted, channel reorganization is
used to achieve lightweight sub-pixel upsampling [46]. For
C2/C2f/C3 blocks, we construct structure-preserving reconstruction modules by replacing the convolutional layers with
transposed convolutions while retaining the residual or crossstage connection patterns. For SPPF and pooling, transposed
convolution is used to approximate the recovery of spatial resolution because these operations are inherently non-invertible.
BatchNorm, activation functions, and concatenation are retained as standard normalization, nonlinear transformation,
and feature-fusion components, respectively, rather than being
treated as inverse operators.

Since dynamic split-point switching involves feature compression and reconstruction at different network depths, different split paths may suffer from different degrees of feature
distortion, causing inconsistent accuracy across split points. To
alleviate this problem, we propose a hierarchical compensation module consisting of Task-specific Global Compensation
and Local Alignment. The task-specific global compensation
module enhances task-critical responses in the final shared features by introducing classification- or detection-related spatial
masks. In contrast, local alignment mitigates distribution shifts
caused by compression and quantization by adjusting the statistical characteristics of the decoded features. By combining
these two designs, DS-Net not only reduces compression errors
across different split branches, but also enhances task-aware
representations for final prediction.

A. Experimental Setup
Prototype. DS-Net employs NVIDIA Jetson TX2 and Jetson
Nano as edge devices, and an NVIDIA 4090 GPU as the
cloud server. System specifications are summarized in Table
V. These devices are interconnected via a wireless network.
To emulate varying bandwidth conditions, we utilize the traffic
control tool Wondershaper 1 .
Backbones, Tasks, and Datasets. To evaluate the generality
of our framework in vision tasks, we select two representative
intelligent applications.
• Image Classification. We select two widely adopted
backbone architectures, VGG-16 [49] and ResNet-50
[50], and evaluate them on the commonly used CIFAR100 classification dataset2 .
• Object Detection. We evaluate YOLOv5s [51] and
YOLOv8s [52] on two benchmark datasets: COCO3
and VisDrone4 , which are widely recognized for object
detection evaluation.
Comparisons. We compare DS-Net with state-of-the-art methods. (1) Comparison with Collaborative Inference Methods:
The Edge-only method is executed locally on the edge device
as the baseline. The Cloud-only method runs entirely in
the cloud, where input images of size 32 × 32 for classification and 640 × 640 for detection are transmitted from
the edge. SP-Reconfig [26] applies 1-bit quantization and
restores convolutional layers at the split points. DeepAdaInNet [38] performs adaptive feature compression at the layer
immediately preceding the branch structure, using a fixed split
point. DVFO [39] partitions feature maps according to their
importance via an attention mechanism and quantizes the less
important features. For each fixed-split baseline, we train or
fine-tune the model separately at the corresponding split point
with its compression and reconstruction modules enabled.
The resulting split-specific checkpoint is used for evaluation,
ensuring a fair comparison with DS-Net under optimized
configurations. (2) Comparison with Lightweight Models:
YOLO-nano is a lightweight and compact variant of the YOLO
model. Knowledge Distillation (KD) [53] is a widely used
technique that transfers knowledge from the baseline model
to a smaller one. DepGraph [54] is a dependency graphbased pruning method that removes redundant computations
while maintaining model accuracy. YOLO-StarNet [55] is

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate end-to-end latency, throughput, and accuracy. Latency includes edge computation, feature transmission, and cloud computation, while downlink
latency is ignored. For pipelined collaborative inference, edge
computation and transmission form the first stage, and cloud
computation forms the second. Throughput is determined by
the slower stage. We report Top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-100 for
classification and mAP@0.5 for object detection.
Split Point Selection Strategy To achieve flexible and efficient collaborative inference, we select candidate split points
at the structural boundaries of the network, specifically at
locations where significant changes occur in feature map
dimensions or channel numbers. These positions typically
correspond to the equilibrium between computational and
communication, thereby presenting a higher likelihood of
being optimal split points. For ResNet-50, we place four split
points at stage boundaries (Conv2 x–Conv5 x). In VGG16,
we identify the four pooling layers after Conv2–Conv5 as split
points. For YOLO-based detectors, we select key backbone
layers (3, 5, 7, 10), where significant changes in feature map
size and transmission volume occur.
Implementation and Training Details. For classification
experiments, all models are implemented in PyTorch and
trained in the cloud using the SGD optimizer. The initial
learning rate is set to 0.1, the batch size is 128, the momentum
is set to 0.9, and the weight decay is 5 × 10−4 . The models
are trained for 200 epochs using a multi-step learning rate
schedule, where the learning rate is multiplied by 0.2 at epochs
60, 120, and 160. The input images are resized to 32 × 32.
For object detection experiments, all models are trained using
SGD with an initial learning rate of 0.01, final learning rate
factor of 0.2, momentum of 0.937, weight decay of 5 × 10−4 ,
and batch size of 32. Training is conducted for 500 epochs
following the detector training schedule. The learning rate is
updated using a LambdaLR scheduler, with the final learning
rate set to 0.2 of the initial learning rate. The input image size
is set to 640 × 640. Other detector-specific settings, including
anchor configuration, NMS threshold, and data augmentation,
follow the original baseline implementation. All inference
experiments on Jetson Nano and Jetson TX2 are conducted
using FP32 precision.

Robustness under Non-stationary Bandwidth. To evaluate DS-Net under non-ideal network conditions, we con-

As shown in Fig. 9(a), the resulting bandwidth trace contains

Fig. 9(b) shows the split-point trajectory of DS-Net. Under low
bandwidth conditions (40–80 s), it selects a deeper split point
(split 10), to reduce transmission overhead. When sufficient
bandwidth is available (80–100 s), it switches to a shallower
split point (split 3 or 5) to lower the computational burden on
the edge device.
Fig. 9(c) compares the latency performance of different
methods under this non-stationary bandwidth environment.
During the period of 40-60s when the bandwidth is extremely
low, the latency peak of DS-Net is smaller than that of other
methods, because it can dynamically select a more appropriate
splitting point. In the bandwidth fluctuation state from 20s to
40s, DS-Net can quickly switch between split points 5 and
10 while maintaining a low latency state, which demonstrates
that it avoids the reloading overhead for specific split models.
Fig. 9(d) further shows the accuracy variation over time. DSNet maintains stable accuracy on the COCO dataset during
both steady-state and switching phases, indicating that dynamic split-point adjustment does not introduce significant
transient accuracy degradation. Overall, DS-Net achieves a
better trade-off between latency stability and accuracy retention under non-stationary bandwidth conditions.
Sample-adaptive Switching Analysis. In the utility function,
we set the trade-off coefficient to ω = 0.22, which assigns
a moderate penalty to latency and encourages the system to
switch to lower-latency configurations when network conditions degrade. A larger ω would make the policy more latencysensitive and select local compensation more aggressively,
while a smaller ω would favor accuracy and therefore select
global compensation more often, especially under acceptable
bandwidth.
Fig. 10 shows that the proposed sample-adaptive strategy
can adjust the compensation mode based on available uplink
bandwidth. When the bandwidth is extremely limited, DSNet avoids using the global path to reduce communication
overhead. At a bandwidth of 1 Mbps, the global ratio of
this sample-adaptive strategy is 0. As bandwidth increases,
DS-Net gradually enables global features for more samples.
Within the range of 2–4 Mbps, the global ratio is 0.162,
indicating that only a small number of hard samples are
assigned to global compensation. At 4 Mbps, DS-Net achieves
a latency of 99.85 ms, which is 9.15% lower than that of the
global+local baseline, while the accuracy only drops by 0.85%.
Compared with the local-only baseline, DS-Net improves
accuracy by 0.37% with an additional latency increase of
only 1.95 ms. In the medium bandwidth range of 5 to 8
Mbps, the global ratio rises to 0.509, which means that DSNet selects the global configuration for approximately half

14

of the samples while maintaining the local configuration for
the remaining samples, thereby achieving a better trade-off
between accuracy and latency. At 8 Mbps, compared with
the global+local baseline, DS-Net reduces latency from 88.90
ms to 85.95 ms, representing a 3.31% latency reduction, a
3.91% throughput improvement, and only a 0.29% accuracy
decrease. When the bandwidth is sufficiently high, reaching
9–10 Mbps, communication overhead is no longer a critical
constraint. Accordingly, DS-Net converges to the global+local
configuration, with the global ratio reaching 1.0. Within this
range, DS-Net achieves the same accuracy and latency as
the global+local baseline, and attains the highest accuracy.
Overall, these results demonstrate that instead of statically preferring local-only or global+local execution, DS-Net gradually
transitions from local execution to selective global compensation as bandwidth increases, and eventually achieves full
global compensation, thereby maintaining a sound balance
among latency, throughput and accuracy.
D. Ablation Studies and Visualization Results of DS-Net
Ablation Analysis. Table IX summarizes the ablation study
results across each candidate split point of the proposed DSNet, using the YOLOv5 detection model on the COCO and
VisDrone datasets. The evaluation includes the output feature
volume at each split point and the corresponding detection
accuracy. The baseline, which is the original YOLOv5s model
without modifications, achieves 56.7% mAP on COCO and
35.4% mAP on VisDrone. Notably, layers 5, 7, and 10 are
multi-branch feature fusion layers. By integrating the FRN
module, redundant inter-branch feature transmission is significantly reduced. For example, at the 10th layer, the transmitted
feature size is reduced to 400KB. To further minimize transmission cost, we apply 4× channel reduction, compressing
intermediate features from 400KB to 100KB. Subsequent 1bit quantization reduces data precision from 32 bits to 1
bit, decreasing the feature size to merely 3KB. However,
such aggressive compression incurs a noticeable accuracy
drop. On COCO, the accuracy degradation is 3.4%, while on
VisDrone, it reaches 5.7%. This degradation arises because
the FRN module has limited capacity to fully reconstruct
the original features, and subsequent feature compression
intensifies the distribution shift. At split layer 10, adding
the local compensation module improves accuracy by about
2%, while adding the global compensation yields 2.5% and
3.3% gains on COCO and VisDrone, respectively. The local
compensation focuses on the split layer to help recover the
original feature distribution after compression. In contrast,
the global compensation not only enhances object awareness
but also compensates for the semantic loss caused by feature
compression at the edge. As a result, the global compensation
provides more complete semantic restoration and achieves
greater accuracy improvement than the local one. Finally,
compensation modules demonstrate strong performance on the
VisDrone dataset, achieving 34.3% accuracy, with only a 1.1%
drop compared to the original model. On COCO, at the split
point 10, the accuracy is only reduced by 0.5% in the end,
and then the features were compressed by 433 times.

The ablation results in Table X also provide an empirical
analysis of error propagation across different split points.
Without compensation, aggressive 1-bit quantization leads
to clear accuracy degradation at all split layers, indicating
that compression errors are propagated through the remaining
cloud-side network and further affect FRN-based feature reconstruction. On COCO, the accuracy drops from the baseline
56.7% to 54.6%, 55.2%, 54.8%, and 53.3% at split layers 3,

accuracy decreases from 35.4% to 30.7%, 32.0%, 31.5%, and

becomes much more stable across split points. The compensated model achieves 55.8%, 56.0%, 56.0%, and 56.2% on
COCO, and 34.3%, 34.5%, 34.5%, and 34.2% on VisDrone.
These results show that although reconstruction error inevitably exists and may accumulate, the proposed hierarchical
compensation effectively bounds its impact. In particular, local
compensation mitigates the distribution shift immediately after
decoding, while global compensation restores target-aware
spatial structures for FRN reconstruction, leading to stable
downstream performance across different split configurations.
Analysis of Hyperparameters. To determine an appropriate
weight for the auxiliary mask supervision loss, we evaluate
different values of λ on COCO and VisDrone. As shown in
Table X, the gray-highlighted row denotes the best-performing
setting. When λ = 1.0, DS-Net achieves the highest mAP@0.5
on both COCO and VisDrone, reaching 56.2% and 34.2%,
respectively. A smaller λ may provide insufficient supervision
for task-aware mask generation, while an excessively large
value may overemphasize the auxiliary mask branch. Therefore, we set λ = 1.0 in the following experiments.
We further compare predefined and learnable fusion coefficients in the global compensation module. As reported in

the best performance on both datasets, with 56.2% mAP@0.5
on COCO and 34.2% mAP@0.5 on VisDrone. Compared
with fixed coefficient settings, the learnable formulation can
adaptively balance the original feature response and the taskaware compensation term for different datasets and feature
distributions. In contrast, manually predefined coefficients may
be suboptimal and can even cause unstable performance on
VisDrone. Therefore, we adopt learnable fusion coefficients
in DS-Net.

Table XIII summarizes the performance of edge-only,
edge–edge, and multi-edge inference. For ResNet, the multiedge split pair (2, 4) achieves a 2.4× throughput improvement

over edge-only inference while incurring only a 3.3% accuracy loss and maintaining comparable latency. In contrast to
edge-edge collaboration, multi-edge collaboration boosts the
throughput rate by an additional 1.6×, primarily attributed to
the third device offloading computational tasks, enabling the
front-end devices to expedite subsequent data processing and
thereby enhancing the overall data flow throughput. Benefiting
from the proposed global compensation module, the average
accuracy degradation across all multi-edge configurations is
reduced only 4.2%. For YOLOv5, the multi-edge split pair
(5, 7) achieves a 1.8× throughput improvement over edgeonly inference, with a 2.4% accuracy drop. Compared with
edge–edge inference at split point 3, the multi-edge split pair
(3, 5) further improves throughput by 1.2× with a 1.9%
accuracy decrease.
As shown in Fig. 12, the proposed graph-based joint optimization reduces end-to-end latency compared with greedy
per-device split selection. The greedy strategy only minimizes
the current device computation and current-link transmission
cost, which may leave a heavier workload to subsequent
devices. In contrast, our method treats the complete multidevice split sequence as a graph path and jointly optimizes
computation and transmission costs over the entire device
chain. Under 10 Mbps bandwidth, even in the homogeneous
TX2-TX2-TX2 setting, the proposed method reduces latency
by 6.8%, indicating that global balancing of layer-wise computation and transmission cost is still necessary even when
devices have identical capabilities. The advantage becomes
more evident in heterogeneous device chains, where greedy
local decisions are more likely to assign unsuitable workloads
to weaker devices. For example, the latency is reduced by
14.6% on TX2-Nano-TX2 and by 8.5% on TX2-Nano-Nano.
Meanwhile, the accuracy remains stable or slightly improved,
with gains of 0.3% on TX2-TX2-TX2 and 0.6% on NanoTX2-TX2. This is because the proposed method tends to select
globally better split configurations, such as (7, 10) or (5, 10),
rather than the shallow local split configurations selected by
the greedy strategy.
VI. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose DS-Net, a dynamic split network
designed for edge-cloud collaborative inference. On the edge
side, DS-Net adopts a dynamic switching framework that
enables fast and low-overhead switching of split points by
adapting to real-time bandwidth and computational capacity.
To mitigate the accuracy degradation caused by splitting and
compression, a hierarchical compensation module is implemented on the cloud side: local alignment adaptively aligns
feature distributions at split points, while global compensation
restores structural details to enhance feature reconstruction.
We also consider adding sample-adaptive global compensation
switch during the process of selecting split points. Experimental results show that DS-Net reduces memory overhead
by 74.6% and achieves millisecond-level switching latency.
Compared with state-of-the-art collaborative methods, DS-Net
improves accuracy by 1.2% on the COCO dataset, reduces
latency by 42.5%, and increases throughput by 1.8× under 10
Mbps on the Nano platform.
