still needs to know what to prioritize. Today, the mapping from

bandwidth, a maintenance window, or an emergency response

is largely manual, performed by operators, or hard-coded into

time.

Abstract—5G edge network slicing must reconcile two coupled
requirements: understanding high-level operator intent and reallocating scarce computing and bandwidth resources under dynamic
contention. Centralized optimization can face computational and
responsiveness challenges under rapidly changing edge contention,
and Multi-Agent System (MAS)-based auctions can support
resource arbitration but still depend on operator-defined economic
parameters and priorities. Embedding a Large Language Model
(LLM) directly in a recurring control loop, by contrast, may
introduce non-negligible inference latency and non-deterministic
behavior. This paper proposes a two-timescale architecture that
addresses this gap: an LLM-based Strategist translates naturallanguage network intents into economic policy parameters (budget,
income, and bidding-aggressiveness) at the macro scale, while a
deterministic, Vickrey-inspired second-price mechanism run by
SPADE-based slice agents resolves recurring resource contention
at the operational scale, with the resulting allocations enforced
by patching Kubernetes CPU limits and Calico CNI bandwidth
annotations directly on User Plane Function pods. We evaluate
the architecture end-to-end on a real Kubernetes/Open5GS edge
cluster across two complementary scenarios, each repeated 30
times: a Smart-City scenario, in which natural-language publicsafety intents propagate into measurable changes in slice bids
and bandwidth allocations within a few auction rounds, and a
Hierarchical-slicing scenario, in which the mechanism provides
priority-dependent resource and QoE differentiation, adapts to
abrupt demand changes, and preserves configured minimum
guarantees while maintaining near-complete allocation of the
shared bandwidth pool.

This paper addresses this gap through a two-timescale
architecture that separates semantic intent interpretation from
deterministic resource arbitration. At the macro scale, a Large
Language Model (LLM) acts as a Strategist, interpreting naturallanguage network intents and translating them into economic
policy parameters, including slice budget, income rate, and
bidding-aggressiveness weight. At the operational scale, these
policies parameterize a deterministic Vickrey-inspired secondprice mechanism executed by the MAS, in which slice agents
compete for available resources according to their economic
parameters and observed resource conditions. The resulting allocations are enforced directly on the infrastructure by updating
CPU limits and Calico CNI bandwidth annotations on the User
Plane Function (UPF) pods of a real Kubernetes/Open5GS
edge cluster, without requiring manual intervention in each
allocation round. The key design principle is that generative
reasoning determines how operator intent influences service
priorities, while recurring resource-allocation decisions remain
independent of LLM inference.

B. Scenario 2: Hierarchical Slices
This scenario isolates the operational resource-arbitration
mechanism from the Strategist layer: three slices are assigned
static contractual priorities – Gold (8.0), Silver (5.0), and
Bronze (3.0) – and traffic is paused and resumed on each slice
in turn to emulate subscriber disconnection and reconnection,
without invoking the Strategist.

Table II shows clear service differentiation across the three
priority classes. Gold’s average bitrate is 3.09× Bronze’s,
exceeding the nominal priority ratio of 8.0/3.0 ≈ 2.67×. This
amplification is consistent with the cubic priority-to-coefficient
mapping ϕ(p) in Eq. (1), which intentionally strengthens
differentiation toward the upper end of the priority range. QoE
indicators follow the same ordering: Gold records the lowest
stall rate (0.09) and fewest quality switches (10.6), compared
with 0.65 stalls and 19.7 switches for Bronze.
Fig. 3 shows the reallocation mechanism directly. Following
event 1, Gold’s bandwidth falls from about 21.0 to 7.6 Mbps
over 300 s (ten rounds) as the Resource agent redirects freed
capacity to Silver and Bronze; after event 3, it takes about
six rounds (∼180 s) to climb back above 20 Mbps, tracking
the ramp-up of its own measured congestion rather than an
instantaneous restoration. Bronze’s pause (event 4) drives its
allocation toward the configured 1.0 Mbps floor, and it remains
close to that floor even after resuming (event 6), as Gold
and Silver’s combined demand continues to dominate the
pool. Summed bandwidth remains close to the 35 Mbps pool

separation combines semantic flexibility at the policy layer with
deterministic execution at the operational resource-arbitration
layer, without requiring generative inference for each allocation
decision. Evaluated end-to-end on a real Kubernetes/Open5GS
