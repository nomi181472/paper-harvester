Abstract—As smartphone penetration saturates, we are
witnessing a new trend in personal mobile devices—wearable
mobile devices or simply wearables as it is often called. Wearables
come in many different forms and flavors targeting different
accessories and clothing that people wear. Although small in
size, they are often expected to continuously sense, collect, and
upload various physiological data to improve quality of life. These
requirements put significant demand on improving communication security and reducing power consumption of the system,
fueling new research in these areas. In this paper, we first provide a comprehensive survey and classification of commercially
available wearables and research prototypes. We then examine
the communication security issues facing the popular wearables
followed by a survey of solutions studied in the literature. We also
categorize and explain the techniques for improving the power
efficiency of wearables. Next, we survey the research literature
in wearable computing. We conclude with future directions in
wearable market and research.
Index Terms—Wearables, wearable sensors, wearable computers, smart watches.

I. I NTRODUCTION
HILE the penetration rate for smartphones saturates,
we are witnessing a fascinating new trend in the mobile
device market. Users are showing an increasing interest in
practically wearing a mobile device that promises to enhance
the quality of life in a way that smartphones alone cannot achieve. These devices, which include smart watches,
wrist bands, smart glasses, smart jewellery, electronic garments, skin patches, and so on, are often simply referred to as
wearables.
Wearables can sense, collect, and upload physiological data
in a 24x7 manner, providing opportunities to improve quality of life in a way not easily achievable with smartphones
alone. Wearables can also help users perform many other useful micro tasks, such as checking incoming text messages and
viewing urgent information, much more conveniently and naturally than possible with a smartphone, which is often carried

The value proposition of wearables, especially the health
monitoring aspects, has some overlap with another research
topic known as wireless body area networks (WBANs) [12].
However, the vision of WBAN is much broader as it seeks
to network together a large number1 of wearable sensors and
actuators, including medically implanted devices, to provide a
very comprehensive and accurate monitoring of human health.
Understandably, WBAN requires significant research efforts in
physical, medium access control, and network layers before it
can reach the commercial success. In contrast, wearables are
low-cost, independent devices targeting personal use without
any significant binding to medical professionals. This simplicity makes wearables more widely deployed, which in turn
has led research attention at a much faster pace compared to

Over the last few years, a wide variety of wearables have
appeared in the market offering different functionalities and
wearing options. At the same time, a significant volume of scientific articles have been published in the literature highlighting various research challenges related to wearables. Although
a few articles surveying the broader research topic of WBAN
have been published in the past [12], they do not capture
the recent developments related to wearables. In this article,
we survey the current trends, technologies, research challenges and solutions for wearables. We also discuss the future
market trends and research directions related to the design,
development, and improvements of next generation wearables.
To the best of our knowledge this is the first survey about
available wearable devices, prototypes, and overall research
challenges. Several works looked into the very specific aspects
of some technologies associated with wearables. For example, Hartman [13] provides an overview of available electronic
components and toolkits to design and build smart garments.
Similarly, Sazonov and Neuman [14] present the operating
mechanisms of various sensors available for wearable devices
and different fundamental algorithms associated with them
such as step counting and activity recognition. Some work
look into the use of wearable sensors in specific applications in
the likes of health monitoring and prognosis [15]–[17], activity recognition [18], and assisted living [19]. While surveying
mobile crowd sensing techniques, Zhang et al. [20] briefly
surveyed the use of wearables as sensing units and similarly
Harle [21] surveyed the use of wearables in indoor positioning
applications. In contrast, in this survey we focus on consumer
wearables, associated research challenges, available solutions,
and potential future research directions.
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In
Section II, we survey and classify the state-of-the-art wearables by examining the features and specifications of over
100 different products and research prototypes. Section III
surveys the security vulnerabilities of the communication protocols used by commercial wearables and examines various
approaches for addressing them. Section IV surveys research
on improving the power consumption and battery life of wearables. Section V discusses research in wearable computing.
Future directions and open research challenges are discussed
in Section VI. Section VII concludes the paper.

A. Existing Products
1) Accessories: We define Accessories as the wearable
devices worn externally by users and do not fit into the
category of main clothing items. Accessories are by far the
most popular category of devices in the current wearable market. Such devices include smart watches, wrist bands, smart
glasses, chest straps, smart belts, and various clothing clip-ons.
We next describe the existing products in Accessories under
three sub categories along with their use cases.
a) Wrist-worns: Wrist-worns, as the name implies are
the wearable devices that are worn on the wrist. The existing
commercial wrist-worn products are either smart watches or
wrist bands and we delve into details of these two types of
products below.
Smart Watches: Smart watches are one of the most popular wearable device types. According to recent Gartner
reports [10], in the wearables market smart watch sales
are only second to Bluetooth headsets with approximately
50 million units predicted to be sold in 2016.
Usually the functionality of current smart watches are
twofold. First, they act as communication and notification
tools by complementing smartphones with features such as
receiving notifications (e.g., phone calls, SMS, email, voice
control, and weather updates) and perform micro interactions
(e.g., launch an app in the phone, limited Web browsing, add
reminders, and taking voice commands). Second, most of the
smartwatches also can monitor some human physiological
signals and biomechanics and thus act as fitness tracking
devices that help users to log their daily activities such as
automatically recording work out times, tracking heart rate,
step counts, and calories burnt. The collected data is transferred to the smartphone or a cloud server for analytics and
better presentation for users such as through dashboards.

While most of the smart watches have software and hardware features comparable with previously explained Moto
360, there are some unique features that stand out among the
devices we surveyed. Majority of the smart watches do not
support in-built GPS modules, rather rely on the GPS capability of accompanying smartphones. This can be inconvenient
for the users, who want to track their fitness activities such
as running as they need to carry the smartphone with them
to track the path they ran and the distance. For example,
smart watches: Apple Watch, Samsung Gear S2, and Fitbit
Surge have in-built GPS modules, whereas other watches like
Huawei Watch, Motorola Moto 360, and Pebble Time rely on
the GPS sensor of paired smartphones.
Another distinguishing feature is the availability of a heart
rate monitoring sensor. Again this is a feature useful for users
who want to use the smart watch as a fitness tracker. Seven out
of ten smartwatches we surveyed have heart rate sensors and
there are two types of heart rate sensors. Smart watches such as
Fitbit Surge and Apple Watch monitor heart rate continuously
by default, whereas the continuous heart rate tracking function
needs to be triggered using apps on watches like Moto 360
and LG Urbane 2 during a workout.
We also noticed some differences in communication capabilities of the smart watches. For all the smartwatches we
studied, the normal mode of communication is to pair it
with a smartphone over a short range communication technology (predominantly this is Bluetooth/Bluetooth LE). However,
some smart watches required smart phones of specific types
for the full operation. For example, Apple watch can be only
paired with an iPhone. Also, as mentioned previously full features of Moto360 is only available when it is paired with an
Android phone. While the exact reason for this is unclear,
we believe this limitation occurs due to both commercial
(maintaining the eco system) and technical reasons (OS compatibility). Some smart watches also had the ability to connect
to Internet via 3G/4G (e.g., LG Urbane 2 [89] and Samsung
Gear S2 [31]) and 7 out of 10 of the smart watches listed
in Table XV support WiFi connectivity without the need of a
smartphone.
Finally, beside the two large areas of smart watch applications; communication and notification and monitoring
human physiological signals and biomechanics, some smart
watches in the likes of Samsung Gear S2 and Moto360
are also equipped with ambient sensing capabilities such
as light sensors which enables them to change the screen
brightness accordingly. Such features have been used for
novel applications that are not currently available in consumer
wearables [90] as we discuss in Section II-B.

To summarise, smart watches can be considered as a market success. As mentioned earlier over 50 million device sales
were predicted for 2016, more than 50 smartwatch models are
estimated to be in the market [91]. Also, more recently in
addition to the manufactures coming from smartphone industry such as Motorola, Apple, and Samsung, and manufactures
started directly with smart watches such as Pebble, number of regular wrist watch manufactures such as Casio, Tag
Heuer, Fossil, and Rolex, are also coming up with smart
watches which also indicates the popularity of smart watches.
Nonetheless, the product analysis showed that there are still
some usability issues mainly related to the operating system
readiness such as limited support in different types of smart
phones and support for different types of watch faces which
more resemble conventional wrist watches. Finally, number
of smart watches offer distinctive features such as GPS sensor, heart rate sensor, and 3G/4G or WiFi connectivity to the
Internet.
Wrist Bands: Wrist bands are the other popular category of
wrist-worn wearable devices. While there are similarities with
smart watches, wrist bands are designed specifically to track
a focused set of health and fitness activities. As a result, most
wrist bands do not have a display screen for notifications or
have a limited form factor compared to smart watches that aim
at replacing the conventional watches.
UP4 by Jawbone [36] is an example wrist band that
utilises bio-impedance sensors to capture signals such as
heart rate, movements, body temperature, and galvanic skin
response (GSR). It can passively track and record user activities such as walking and running and also has the ability of
sleep monitoring. There are five bio-impedance sensors located
on the inner side of the band that measure the resistance of
the skin tissues to calculate heart rate. Other sensors in UP4
include a tri-axis accelerometer and temperature sensors (separate sensors for skin temperature and ambient temperature).
Being a wrist band UP4 does not have a screen. The data is
visible via the smartphone app that is available for both iOS
and Android. Through NFC, UP4 allows to make payments
like a credit card.
Similar to most smart watches, UP4 wrist band uses a
battery of type Li-Poly with a capacity of 38 mAh that is
claimed to sustain for 7-8 days without charging. However, we
highlight that compared to smart watches, wrist bands have
batteries with less capacity as they don’t have large display
screens. Being a proprietary platform that does not allow third
parties to develop applications, details are less known about
UP4’s operating system and the CPUs used.
Major limitation of UP4 involves its inability to measure
continuous heart rate as there is no optical heart rate sensor.
Instead, it measures two types of heart rates; resting heart rate
and passive heart rate. Resting heart rate is the measurement
taken just before the wearer gets out of bed in the morning
and passive heart rate is measured at random times during the
day when the wearer is sitting still. While these two measurements give a good indication of one’s general health condition,
sometimes it is important to take heart rate measurements during workout sessions which is not supported by UP4 and is a
limitation of bio-impedance sensors.

replacing the traditional wrist-watch and acting as an extension device for the smartphone vs. accurate and specialised

Nonetheless, it is likely that more sophisticated fitness tracking
wrist bands will continue to exist for users who need advanced
analytics.

Smart Eyewear: Smart eyewear includes spectacles or contact lenses with sensing, wireless communication, or other
capabilities such as virtual or augmented reality.

Although not expected to be worn for day-to-day use,
Microsoft Hololens [43] is a pair of mixed-reality glasses that
blends the physical world with the 3D holographic content
(augmented reality). It runs the Windows Holographic platform and runs 32-bit Windows 10 operating system. Hololens
has an inertial measurement unit (IMU), four “environment
understanding” cameras, two ordinary cameras, one microphone array and one ambient light sensor. Microsoft Hololens
enables gaze control of the cursor, gesture control to open
apps, and drag and drop holograms, and voice commands
for navigation. The example applications of Hololens includes
gaming, 3D-design (e.g., architecture and home design), and
remote assistance.
HoloLens is powered by a 1 GHz, CPU, and a custom GPU
for holographic processing. It has 2 GB of RAM and 64 GB
of flash memory. In terms of communication, it supports WiFi
802.11ac and Bluetooth LE and can communicate with any
device that support them. However, the development of the
HoloLens is limited via a Windows 10 machine. HoloLens is
expected to have 2-5 hours lifetime of active use depending
on the load and 2 weeks of standby time [95]. The capacity
of the battery bank is 16.5 Wh.
One unique feature of the Hololens is its ability to operate
standalone without any companion computer or a smartphone. Major negative review of the HoloLens is, despite the
adjustable headband, the device is reported to be hard to wear
in a comfortable way as it is not meant to sit on the nose
like traditional eyewear and most of the weight is carried by
its front part. The total weight of the device 579 g which is
slightly to the higher side for a wearable. Another limitation
that has been highlighted is the limited field-of-view where
the user’s view is clipped at level that disturbs the experience.
Also the active battery life is considered to be insufficient for
some application scenarios.

eyewear with a limited set of functionalities we could not find

so that the map and navigation path is visible to the athlete.

Headsets and Ear-buds: Bluetooth headsets are the first consumer wearable devices and still by far the most sold wearable
type in the market. Traditional Bluetooth headsets are designed
to make hands-free calls while more recent products are able to
perform more advanced tasks (these products are also known
as hearables) and we below discuss some of the commercial
products that provide additional functionalities.
The Sony Xperia Ear [52] is a more intelligent version of
a Bluetooth enabled headset. Apart from the typical functions
such as taking calls and playing music, the ear bud is capable
of taking voice commands such as navigation inquires, dictating messages, and searching the Internet. With the built-in
proximity sensor, accelerometer and gyroscope, Sony Xperia
Ear is able to tell if the device is worn by a user, not being
used, or being charged. It can also detect simple head motions
like a nod of the head. The ear bud works by communicating with a paired handheld device through NFC or Bluetooth
and the host mobile phone app (supports only Android) allows
users to make customised settings.
The device weighs only 6.6 g and can be charged using
a portable charging pod which is embedded in the carrying
case. The battery that powers Sony Xperia Ear has a capacity
of 65 mAh and the charging pod has a capacity of 300 mAh.
The ear bud is expected to provide 4 hours of talk time when
fully charged. With the support from the charging pod, the
device is expected to provide 16 hours of active time and
the standby operation time is claimed to be 80 hours. Further
details on the embedded hardware is not released by the
manufacturer.

Main drawback of Sony Xperia Ear is its short battery life
during active use as users claim that 3-4 hours of operation
per charge is not enough especially when they do activities
such as listening to music. Another limitation highlighted is
the limited capability of the artificial intelligent assistant. For
example, to trigger voice commands the user first need to tap
on the device button which is inconvenient. Also, as Sony
Xperia Ear is a one-sided earbud, it is not a good option for
listening to music and the optimisations done to preserve the
voice quality by eliminating the background noise also appear
to interfere with the playback quality [98].
Recently released Apple AirPods [53] is a comparable product to Sony Xperia Ear. However, there are another set of
earbuds that focuses on monitoring human physiological signals and biomechanics, in addition to being an intelligent
assistant to the smartphone such as Samsung Gear IconX [99],
Jabra Elite Sport [100], and Bragi Dash Pro [54]. We next
delve into the features of the Bragi Dash Pro as an example
product.
Bragi Dash Pro comprises of 23 sensors including an
accelerometer, a heart rate monitor, and an oxygen saturation
sensor and it has the capacity of automated activity detection
for activities such as running, cycling, and swimming. It consists of a 32 bit ARM processor and 4GB of internal memory,
which allows users to store large amount of fitness data internally as well as storing any music they want to listen during the
activities eliminating the need to carry a phone. The device is
powered by a 100 mAh Li-Poly battery that can accomodate an
active time of 4-6 hours and a standby time of nearly 10 days.
The operating system of the device is called as Bragi OS.
Bragi Dash Pro offers a unique feature of real-time face-to-face
conversational language translation that allows two users wearing Bragi ear-buds to communicate with each other without
knowing the other’s language. Another feature is the possibility of using head-gestures to interact with the device which
comes useful when the users hands are pre-occupied. Being
released only in May 2017, so far no major negative reviews
were reported. The previously known issue of the fit in the
ear appears to be corrected in this version by partnering with
Starkley, a company specialised in hearing care.
To summarise, we see headsets and ear-buds are redefining
themselves from traditional communication and notification
wearables to hearables with smarter functionalities and more
focus on fitness tracking. The advantages of tracking fitness
activities using an ear-bud is two-fold. First, the accuracy can
be higher for certain types of measurements such as heart
rate [101]. Second, it allows the user to receive real time
non-invasive feedback as voice that can be useful in fitness
activities. As a result, we expect in future the hearables to
provide strong competition to wrist-bands as they can provide
advanced artificial intelligent functions such as voice assistance and feedback, real time translation, and voice commands.
However, being smaller devices fitting into the size of the
ear hearables might not be able to support the power and
processing capacities wrist bands can offer.

2) E-Textiles: E-Textiles are main clothing items that also
serve as wearables such as smart clothes and smart shoes.
E-textiles are majorly used for: i) monitoring human physiological signals and biomechanics for various applications
such as health and sports [109], [110], ii) ambient sensing
for military or hazardous environments [111], and iii) sensory/haptic applications such as therapeutic massages [112].
E-Textiles are either made of conductive and comfortable-towear materials such as conductive fibres and flexible electronic
circuits or are ordinary clothing items with conventional sensing, actuating, and data transmission modules. We classify
e-textiles into two main subcategories: Smart Garments and
Hand/Foot-worns and provide detailed descriptions of example
state-of-the-art commercial products.
a) Smart garments: Smart garments are the main clothing items such as shirts, pants, and undergarments. There is
a range of such products for monitoring human physiological signals and biomechanics. For example, Athos [64] a
Silicon Valley based start-up that produces a range of fitness

via Bluetooth. The app subsequently visualises the muscle
effort, muscle activation, heart rate, and balance and provide
feedback to users. OmSignal [113], MYONTEC [114], Cityzen
Sciences [115] and Physiclo [116] also manufacture similar
products.
Another type of smart garments monitor human vital
signs for health, rehabilitation, and safety applications.
The WEALTHY project [117], Georgia Tech Wearable
Motherboard [118], and ProeTEX [119] are early examples
of wearable health care systems that utilises knitted fabrics
to monitor essential physiological and bio-mechanical signals,

such as heart rate, breathing rate, and dehydration. More
modern consumer oriented products include Exmobaby [120],
Cyrcadia Health [121], B Maternity [122], and WEMU [123].
For example, Exmobaby is a cotton baby pyjama with a removable strap on the inside that contains fabric to measure baby’s
ECG. In addition, the pyjama also contains a thermometer and
motion detection sensors externally. The data is transmitted
to a bay station over FM and the bay station subsequently
transfers the data either via WiFi or 3G. Cyrcadia Health
has developed iTBra that uses thermal dynamic measurement
to detect breast tumours. Changes in body temperature are
picked up by sensors in the bra is transferred to the cloud and
the analysis is forwarded to a smartphone [124]. Similarly, B
Maternity clothing is a prototype product developed to monitor pregnant women and WEMU is a top designed to monitor
epilepsy patients.
Some other smart garments have ambient sensing functionalities for the safety of the wearer or as a means of data
collection. For instance, Ohmatex [125] and Viking Life [126]
introduced a sensor embedded firefighter jacket. With the
thermal sensors embedded directly to the inner and outer
fabric layers and LED displays, the outfit gives the advantage to visually indicate critical heat levels and potentially
critical situations. A battery in the liner is used to power
the whole system. Safe@Sea [127] is another example suit
for fishermen with integrated sensors that emits an emergency distress signal, if the person wearing the suit falls into
the water.
Sensory/haptic applications are another major use case of
smart garments. The Hug Shirt [65] enables people to send
hugs over distances. The embedded sensors detect the touch,
warmth, and heart rate of the sender. On the receiver side, the
actuators recreate the sensation of touch, warmth, and emotion
of the sender. Similar to the idea introduced by Hug Shirt, a
more recent E-textile product Tjacket [128] is a wearable tech
vest that is designed for children with ADHD or Autism who

the price of such a vest ranges from $399-$599, which is much
higher than regular children clothes.
The WarmX [129] is a heatable underwear for sufferers of
muscular tension and back pain. The silver-plated polyamide
threads of low resistance are woven into the underwear to
conduct current and to warm up directly on the skin. A small
battery control unit is connected to the conductive fabric to
power the heating zones. Spinovo [67] is a similar prototype
product of an upper body garment for back pain relief.
Apart from the above main use cases, there are smart garments for a range of other activities. The Solar Shirt [66] takes
use of flexible solar panels to harvest solar energy and stores
the energy in a battery. The circuit embedded on the shirt
is able to provide a suitable charging current for other handheld electronic devices. Samsung’s NFC smart suite [130] and
Lyle and Scott contactless jacket [131] allow to make card-less
payments and exchange business cards.

B. Research Prototypes
Under research prototypes, we survey the recent academic
research on novel wearable devices and also recent industrial
attempts, e.g., start-up products that are not yet commercially available, utilising the existing state-of-the-art hardware
and software capabilities as building blocks for new devices
and services. We adhere to the same classification as the
existing products section, i.e., i) Accessories, ii) E-Textiles,
iii) E-Patches, and all products are categorically compared
with existing products in Table I.
1) Accessories: Majority of research prototypes is focused
on one specific application or a small set of functionalities
where as existing accessories presented in the previous section are more often general purpose devices that provides wide

variety of services. In a way, the research community leverage the existing general purpose accessories to demonstrate
the true potential of wearables with novel services. In this section, we review the recent work related to Accessories under
three sub-categories wrist-worn, head-mounted and other
accessories.
a) Wrist-worns: Existing products in wrist-worns such as
smart watches and fitness bands are the most popular and well
refined in terms of hardware capabilities. Therefore, recent
research works in this category are primarily on augmenting
the services offered by the exisiting devices.
Smart Watches: Gait-Watch [144] is a context-aware user
authentication system based on the gait pattern recognition
from a smart watch. The authors propose a novel sparse
fusion method to improve the accuracy and the complete
system is implemented on a Samsung Gear Live smart watch
for the validation. The results show that Gait-Watch authenticate the person with 95.2% true positive rate. Similarly,
Yang et al. [145] proposed a motion based authentication
for wrist worn devices with IMUs. Simple histograms of
accelerometer signals and dynamic time warping are used to
for feature extraction and then the similarity score of features
are used for authentication. The system is implemented on a
Android smart watch. Although these systems show promise,
they are not yet ready for commercial secure authentication
due to probabilistic nature of these mechanisms. However, due
to small screen and limited user interaction, continuous and
implicit authentication mechanisms seems to be more practical
approach for smart watches. Along with the advanced sensing
capabilities, smart watch has the potential to be used in number of continuous and implicit authentication mechanisms in
future.
Gruenerbl et al. [34] designed a smart watch application that
assists untrained people with performing Cardiopulmonary
Resuscitation (CPR). Using the acceleration data on the watch,
the application gives feedback on frequency and compression depth to guide the aider. It was found that the ratio
of participants who managed to stay in the ideal depth and
frequency ranges was much higher for those with the assistance of the application. Xu et al. [35] showed that the finger,
hand, and arm gestures can be accurately identified by monitoring movements of the tendons passing through the wrist
using an accelerometer and a gyroscope attached to a smart
watch. Specifically, the authors built a classifier that can recognise the characters written by the index finger on a surface
with 95% of accuracy. Extending this work to detect continuous writing with multiple words and writing in the air is left
as a potential future work. Even though the wearables such
as smart-rings can be used to detect finger writing more accurately, wide availability of smart watches and fitness bands
makes this work more deployable than others.
Wrist Bands: In the recent past, researchers have been
trying to take wrist bands beyond the health and fitness
monitoring devices. These activities can be categorised in to
novel authentication mechanisms and complex activity recognition. Wearables are now driving the research on continuous
and implicit authentication mechanisms because wearables
are designed to be worn through out the day. To this end,

majority of the cases. In general, research prototypes of

when the proposed methods become mature enough, we can

Smart Eyewear: Despite the enormous publicity received
for the launch of Google Glass [46], still it remains as a
research prototype that is not yet available in the consumer
market. However, the initial release of prototypes immensely
contributed to the research and development activities in the
category of head-mounted devices. In a nutshell, Google Glass
is a pair of glasses that allows user interaction through a
touchscreen and voice control and also provides the ability
of capturing photos and videos. It contains sensors similar
to smart watches such as accelerometer and gyroscope and
runs a version of Android operating system. Google Glass
is powered by a battery of Li-Poly type with a capacity of
570 mAh. A number of design issues were raised by the product reviewers as well as academics who experimented with the
limited devices released. For example, many users complain
about the battery life. The glass was able to record only 30
mins of video without charging and most users considered this
insufficient. Also, when in operation the glass produces heat

A. Security Threats to Wearables
We present work that highlighted security threats to wearables under three categories: 1) Threats to Confidentiality,
2) Threats to Integrity, and 3) Threats to Availability.
This classification is standard in network security [163] and
has been used in multiple other studies [164]–[166]. Threats
to Confidentiality involves the scenarios where attackers get
unauthorised access to information using techniques such
as eavesdropping the wireless channel. Threats to Integrity
includes the cases where attackers alter resources or information without authorisation. Threats to Availability are the
situations where attackers act to deny services to the entities
who are authorised to use them.
1) Threats to Confidentiality: As our product survey highlighted, majority of existing wearable devices use Bluetooth
Low Energy (BLE) as the major means of communication. However, BLE equipped wearable devices have been
reported to be vulnerable to attacks that impact the confidentiality such as eavesdropping [167]–[170] and traffic analysis [167], [171], [172]. Another body of work [173]–[175]
reported scenarios of using wearable devices to gather information about user’s other devices such as her PIN to unlock the
smartphone. We below discuss existing studies that reported
and analysed these attacks threatening confidentiality in wearable communications.
a) Eavesdropping attacks: Eavesdropping is the unauthorized real-time interception of a private communication
and these attacks are significant security threats to wearable
systems as they can expose user’s personal information to
an attacker. Also, they may end up being entry points for
other forms of attacks. As mentioned in Section II majority
of wearable devices are utilising BLE communication protocol that can suffer from eavesdropping attacks in some
scenarios. In particular, there are specialised hardware such
as Ubertooth [176] and Bluefruit LE Sniffer [177] that allow

2587

adversaries to detect and follow the communication chain of
a BLE wearable device.
Fawaz et al. [168] reported number of issues related to the
implementations of the BLE advertisement process in consumer wearable devices. More specifically, authors found that
some of the existing wearables do not have a proper implementation of the MAC address randomisation defined in the BLE
specification as a privacy preserving provision. For instance,
it was found that some devices do not use address randomisation at all whilst some implementations were insufficient as
they were only flipping few bits of the public addresses. Also,
there were concerns in the duration a random address is kept
active. These implementations enable adversaries to eavesdrop
on the wireless channel and follow the advertisement packets
to track a BLE device easily.
The privacy leakage from the advertisement was also identified in other studies that involve security analysis in fitness
trackers [169], [170]. For example, Goyal et al. [169] identified
the key privacy and security vulnerabilities of the two most
widely adopted wearable fitness trackers, Jawbone UP Move
and FitBit Charge. To analyse the security of Bluetooth LE
communication, the authors set up a Bluetooth protocol stack
bluez and the GattTool tool to contact and interact with the
tracker and discovered that both trackers have not implemented
address randomisation. Thus, the attacker can track the user
over time by eavesdropping on the device’s communications
and exploiting the public device address.
Similarly, recent Open Effect Report 2016 [170] provided a
comparative analysis of the privacy and security features of fitness tracking devices. The authors investigated Bluetooth LE
privacy provision in number of fitness tracking devices such as
Fitbit Charge HR, Jawbone UP 2, Garmin Vivosmart, Apple
Watch, and Xiaomi Mi Band. The findings claim that all tested
devices, except Apple Watch, use the static device addresses
that allowed attackers to track user information such as location, time of fitness activities, and reversing user profile by
eavesdropping on these devices’ communications. Ryan [167]
described how to perform eavesdropping on a BLE device
communication by using the Ubertooth sniffer tool. This work
also demonstrated how to successfully bypass the encryption
of BLE wearables that use BLE legacy pairing by breaking
the encryption key.
b) Traffic analysis attacks: In the context of wearables,
traffic analysis attacks are the processes of monitoring traffic exchanged between wearables and its base and/or server
and make inferences from patterns of the communication.
For instance, Das et al. [171] discovered privacy leakages
in relation to the Bluetooth LE communication between the
fitness tracker and the smartphone including User Tracking,
User Activity Detection, and Person Identification. The authors
observed that the fitness trackers constantly advertise even
when they are in close proximity of the smartphone. By sniffing on the BLE advertisements and leveraging the unchanged
device address, adversaries are able to map a specific device
to a specific user. Consequently, the consistent advertising and
the use of static address in BLE fitness trackers make them
vulnerable to User Tracking. The authors also discovered the
correlation between the BLE data traffic of a Fitbit and a

c) Information gathering attacks: As mentioned
previously under Eavesdropping Attacks and Traffic Analysis
Attacks, passive monitoring of data transmissions on wearable
devices allow adversaries to collect information exchanged
between a wearable and its hub. Consequently, such collected
information can be used to conduct serious information
gathering attacks such as breaking the key exchange in BLE
wearable device pairing or to collect information about user’s
other devices such as smartphones.
Ryan [167] demonstrated an attack that can break the LE
legacy pairing of BLE communications of wearables. This
work exploits the fact that in LE Legacy pairing, all pairing related information values other than a secret Temporary
Key (TK) are transmitted over the air in plaintext. The TK
is used to generate a Short Term Key (STK) at both devices
that is then used to encrypt the link between them. Then, the
encryption key called Long Term Key (LTK) is generated and
distributed between two devices for future connections over
the encrypted link by STK. For breaking the encryption key,
Ubertooth tool is used to eavesdrop on the plaintext transmitted values. An offline brute-force attack is then used to
guess the TK. Once TK is cracked, the STK is easily recovered. Consequently, the LTK will be cracked and attackers are
able to decrypt future conversations between BLE devices.
Therefore, wearables used BLE legacy pairing are prone to
the LTK cracking attack that relies on the eavesdropping and
brute-forcing.
Maiti et al. [173] investigated the vulnerabilities of using
wearables such as smart watches for side-channel attacks.
This work demonstrated that leveraging the motion sensors
in smart watches as side-channel to infer sensitive user information is even better than motion sensors in the smartphone.
Specifically, the adversary can make use of the wrist movements perceptible by a target’s smartwatch motion sensors to
infer the target’s keystrokes on a smartphone numeric touchpad. Adversary gains access to the smartwatch by installing a

malicious application on it to record activities of the on-board
accelerometer and gyroscope sensors.
Similarly, Liu et al. [174] demonstrated a practical sidechannel attack to infer user inputs on the keyboards by exploiting sensors in smart watches. By extracting the motion data
from the accelerometer in the smart watch and modelling the
captured movements using displacement or motion directions,
the authors can decode the keystrokes in a numeric keypad of a
Point of Sale (POS) device and a normal QWERTY keyboard.
Recently, Wang et al. [175] demonstrated a practical attack
to infer a user’s personal PIN sequence by exploiting her
wearable devices including smart watches and fitness trackers,
when he is accessing a key-based security system. In particular, the attack model exploits the unique patterns of key
entry activities from the sensor data captured by wearables
to develop distance estimation and slope-based moving direction derivation schemes that captures the limited movements of
the hand between two consecutive key presses. Furthermore,
the attack model applies the backward PIN-sequence inference
algorithm to reveal the user’s complete PIN sequence, leveraging both the spatial and temporal constraints of the key entry.
This methodology is reported to achieve a high success rate.
To extract the sensor readings of wearables, adversaries can
simply sniff Bluetooth LE communications between the wearables and their paired smartphone or install malicious app on
the wearables.
d) Other attacks: In addition to three common threats
to confidentiality discussed above, there are a limited number
of work targeting the vulnerabilities of wearable systems or
the associated applications. For example, Goyal et al. [169]
analysed the security aspects of the Android apps of Jawbone
and Fitbit. Authors recreate a scenario where a smartphone is
infected by a malicious application that can access the received
raw data from the tracker. The finding is that the Jawbone
application sends main information about the device to third
parties for different processing capabilities. In both Jawbone
and Fitbit devices, the application preferences and data are
stored unencrypted on the device. The local databases storing all user activities are in plaintext allowing access to any
attacker. While Fitbit allows users to choose which data will
be shared, the users of Jawbone UP Move tracker do not have
any control or ownership over their data.
A recent study carried out by HP [178] showed that all
tested consumer smartwatches lacked some form of critical
security measures such as data encryption or user authentication. For example, it was found that 50% of the devices did
not have a basic screen lock implemented and two devices
could be paired with an attacker’s phone in a stolen scenario.
Moreover, 70% of the devices push their software updates
unencrypted.

quickly to get established in the market and as such security
is not their primary focus at this stage. Also, another factor can be the trade-off between the constrained resource of

2) Threats to Integrity: Integrity is an important security
requirement for information systems especially for wearable
systems where collected data is usually sensitive and private. It
is important to make sure that data is not altered in transit and
being received by authorised parties only. Several work in the
literature have assessed wearable device systems with respect
to data integrity and highlighted potential vulnerabilities and
we discuss such work below under three attack categories;
i) Modification Attacks, ii) Replay Attacks, and iii) Masquerade
Attacks.
a) Modification attacks: As data transmission between
wearable devices is typically wireless on the air, the data is
prone to be modified or altered. In these types of attacks,
the adversaries after either intercepting the traffic exchange
of wearable devices or gaining access to the information,
can modify the content of exchanging packets or change the
timestamp of data packets.
Haataja and Toivanen [179] demonstrated the Just Works
model in Bluetooth LE pairing makes wearable devices vulnerable to modification attacks. Specifically, the information
exchanged between wearables and their base is modified, i.e.,
the input/output capabilities information in the pairing packet
exchange is changed to “No Input No Output”, so that the
victim wearables choose to use the Just Works pairing mode
allowing the attacker to intercept and modify subsequent data
exchanged between two victim devices.
Rahman et al. [180] found vulnerabilities in personal fitness
data storage and transmission in popular trackers from FitBit
and Garmin which use the ANT protocol for the communication between a tracker and its base - a smartphone. This work
identified several fundamental vulnerabilities and then simulated attacks to exploit these vulnerabilities. First, the Fitbit
tracker does not use encryption during user login via the client
software which means user passwords are sent to the Web
server in plaintext. Second, both FitBit and Garmin do not
use encryption or authentication whilst uploading data. Third,
there is no mutual authentication process during the pairing
between Garmin tracker and its base, i.e., the base authenticates the tracker but the tracker does not authenticate the base.
The authors built two attack tools called Fitbite and GarMax to
exploit the identified vulnerabilities in Fitbit Ultra and Garmin
Forerunner trackers. Results showed that FitBite can discover
tracker devices within transmission range and capture their
fitness data during data uploads. GarMax can directly download fitness information from the tracker as the Garmin tracker
does not authenticate the base. FitBite and GarMax are able
to modify and inject fitness data on neighbouring trackers.
Recently, integrity of the timestamps in sensor data from
wearable healthcare devices is discussed in [181]. The correct
timestamps of medical data is crucial for medical practitioners
and insurance providers and attackers/malicious users might

attempt to modify the timestamps to obtain monetary benefits.
The authors demonstrated that they can tamper the timestamps of measurements from a smart blood pressure monitor
called Fora Diamond Cuff BP, by simply changing the time
on the paired smartphone clock while taking the blood pressure. A similar attack was also performed on an activity
tracker, Withings Pulse O2. Moreover, another timestamp tampering attack was done by using the Apktool to decompile the
Android apps supporting the Fora and Withings blood pressure
monitor devices and making modifications to the source code,
and recompiling code in an Android smartphone. The source
code modifications will make sure that the timestamps of medical data will be tampered when they are being uploaded to
the smartphone.
The Open Effect Report [170] discovered that while most
fitness device applications used HTTPS to encrypt their communication with the servers the Garmin Vivosmart’s application does not provide HTTPS transmission and exposed
the sensitive fitness data to the unauthorized third parties.
This work identified that the fitness data generated by several
wearable devices can be falsified by motivated parties.
b) Replay attacks: In replay attacks, attackers obtain
a copy of the valid data packet uploaded by the wearable
device to the server and replay it with the objective such as
impersonation or data corruption. The only work we found
in this category related to wearables was by Li et al. [182]
where authors demonstrated a possible replay attack in a
commercially available insulin delivery system consisting of
a glucose meter, an insulin pump, a remote control and a
Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP). The communication frequency between the remote control and insulin pump
is 915 MHz. The USRP can receive and generate signal in the
same frequency band through an attached 915 MHz daughter
board and an antenna. By eavesdropping on the communication between devices, attackers are able to determine the
packet format and learn the device type, its PIN, the therapy or glucose level, and medical condition of the patient as
they are transmitted in plaintext. In addition, by using bruteforce method it is possible to figure out the Cyclic Redundant
Check parameters used in the insulin delivery system. Then,
by using a different value in the counter field of the
packet, replay attack was explored for reporting an outdated
glucose level.
c) Masquerade attacks: In masquerade attacks the
attacker impersonates or clones an authenticated device to
act as the legitimate node for stealing data or injecting fake
information to the system.
Naveed et al. [183] identified threats of “external device
mis-bonding” in Bluetooth-enabled Android phones. In fact,
any apps with the Bluetooth permission running on the authorised Android phone can communicate with the external device
which is paired with the phone. Due to the lack of a secure
bonding between healthcare devices and their corresponding
apps in the paired phone, the malicious app running on the
Android phone collects the bonding information from the medical device and delivers it to the adversary. The adversary then
clones the legitimate device and places the clone in the proximity of this device. It is possible to inject fake data into the

In summary, the attacks against data integrity in wearable
systems are accomplished due to the lack of authentication
methods and/or the unencrypted data exchanged over the air
between wearable devices. We noticed as of now threats to
integrity are not as commonplace as threats to confidentiality.
Nonetheless, several work highlighted some possible attacks
in medical wearable devices for which the data integrity is
much more critical compared to consumer wearables. Majority
of the attacks are possible due to the vulnerabilities in data
confidentiality and solutions for those will also alleviate threats
to data integrity.
3) Threats to Availability: The major type of attack against
availability is Denial of Service attacks that attempt to interrupt
the communications of wearables and their base or inject huge
useless information to flood the wearable device’s storage
capacity. We found two such types of example attacks in the
existing body of research literature.
Goyal et al. [169] discovered that the FitBit Charge tracker
is susceptible to Denial of Service (DoS) attacks which can
keep the tracker always busy by making it respond to every
request to prevent the legitimate device syncing with the
paired tracker or to prevent the unpaired tracker from pairing
with the mobile phone. Previously reported attack tools (see
Modification Attacks) [180] Fitbite and GarMax [180] were
able to prevent the Fitbit and Garmin trackers from recording
the user’s valid data by injecting a huge amount of fake data
exceeding the storage capacity of the trackers. In addition,
the FitBite attack tool could simulate attack scenarios where
adversaries continuously query the trackers in its vicinity to
drain their batteries.
Similar to the other threats we explained previously, current
attacks denying service is possible only because of implementation shortcomings by the manufactures and can be expected
to be resolved in next generation of products. Nonetheless,
it is essential to continuously look for such vulnerabilities in
wearable products, especially the health related products such
as insulin pumps which can’t accomodate downtimes.
B. Proposed Security Solutions for Wearables
Having explored the existing threats and vulnerabilities in
current consumer wearables, in this sub section we survey
solutions proposed by the research community to address

threats towards communication security in wearables. We
present the solutions according to the type of security threat
they are attempting to address. There were many solutions
that target threats to both confidentiality and integrity and we
therefore present them in one section.
1) Ensuring Confidentiality and Integrity:
a) Key generation and exchange: Encryption is the defacto method of ensuring confidentiality in communication
over a network. All the commonly used wireless protocols
in wearables offers some form of MAC layer encryption.
However, as we highlighted in Section III-A, MAC layer
encryption might not be sufficient in some cases. As a result,
number of work focused on supporting higher layer encryption protocols. However, when it comes to wearables, its
resource constraints such as CPU, memory, and energy do not
allow to implement advanced encryption methods. For example, Snader et al. [184] measured that in order to encrypt a
block of data using ECDH-ECDSA key exchange, requires
6,000 times higher energy compared to symmetric encryption.
Differences in such magnitude is crucial in wearables due to
the limited battery power. Also, the limited payload size in
some protocols such as Bluetooth LE, does not allow to transfer the longer keys in single message and thus key exchange
can be disrupted significantly under unreliable channel conditions. To this end, number of solutions were proposed for low
power encryption protocols and key exchange mechanisms on
wearables.
Several studies focused on mechanisms that allow two
wearables to agree on a symmetric key without the
need of transmitting the complete key over the wireless channel [184]–[187]. Venkatasubramanian et al. [185]
proposed Physiological-Signal-based Key Agreement (PSKA)
for secure inter-sensor communication for body area networks.
Nonetheless, PSKA is also applicable for wearables. In PSKA
two sensor nodes agree to a symmetric (shared) cryptographic
key, in an authenticated manner, using physiological signals
obtained from the wearer. More specifically, first the sender
generates a random symmetric key which will be hidden using
a feature vector generated from the physiological signal. This
hidden key is then sent to the receiver who will be able to
recover the random key using its own version of feature vector (generated by the receiver using its own measurements of
the physiological signal). Main challenge to address here is
how to compensate the difference between the feature vectors
and the measurements taken from the two nodes can be different according to their placement. To solve this, authors used
the fuzzy vault technique in cryptography. Fuzzy vault allows
to lock (hide) a secret (S) in a construct called a vault using
a set of values A. Once the vault has been locked, it can be
unlocked only with another set of values B that has a significant number of values in common with set A and that suits
the scenario where physiological signal is measured from two
nodes. Revadigar et al. [186] used a similar approach with the
accelerometer signal.
Hu et al. [188] proposed Ordered-Physiological-Featurebased Key Agreement (OPFKA) that also follow a same
approach as fuzzy vault techniques. First, the sender and the
receiver collect Inter Pulse Intervals (IPIs) simultaneously to

Fuzzy vault techniques originally comes from biometric
authentication, and some work have demonstrated that an
attacker may be able to guess the legitimate points in the
vault [188]–[190]. However, compared to fingerprints or any
similar form of static biometrics, signals such as pulse rate and
accelerometer are more dynamic and noisier and as a result it
expected to be difficult for an attacker to identify the legitimate
points.
An alternative approach to fuzzy vault based key establishment is key generation [191]–[193]. These methods directly
create the keys rather than relying on vault based techniques
to conceal the key and exchange. For example, Shi et al. [191]
proposed ASK-BAN, where key extraction is done based on
measurements of the wireless channel characteristics between
a controller unit (CU) and an on-body sensor node. First,
device authentication ensures the sensor devices that communicates with the CU are on the same body of one person,
using the stable channels between on-body devices compared
to the off-body devices that have highly unstable channels to
the CU due to simple body movements that the patient artificially performed during the process. Simultaneously, secret
keys are extracted between every authenticated on-body device
and the CU, utilising their unstable channels.
Another approach to key distribution is the use of out of
band channels. For instance, Snader et al. [184] proposed
CryptoCop encryption protocol for wearable mobile devices.
The main idea behind CryptoCop is to utilise the charging
times of the wearables (e.g., via USB cable by connecting to
a laptop) to upload the key information in comparison to a
conventional key exchange happening over the wireless channel. To address the issues of packet size and unreliable channel
conditions, CryptoCop uses AES in counter mode in contrast
to CBC (Cipher Block Chaining) and CFB (Cipher Feed Back)
modes used in classical protocols. The performance of the
protocol is evaluated using wearable heart rate monitors.
Another out of band key exchange mechanism SecureVibe
by Kim et al. [194] includes an ultra-low power battery
drain resistant wakeup mechanism and a vibration-based,
lightweight cryptographic key exchange scheme between the
wearable medical device and the external device by exploiting the advantages of vibration channel such as extremely
short range, direct physical contact requiring and highly userperceptible. Specifically, the authors proposed an enhanced
two-feature On-Off Keying scheme for vibration channel to
enable acceptable bit rates for key exchange. In the wakeup
mechanism, the vibration signal is detected through the vibration measurement of an accelerometer in the wearable medical
device. A high-pass filter is used to eliminate the lowfrequency noise from patient movement or internal organs.

The RF module is only waked up if a high-frequency vibration signal is detected after the filtering. After waking up the
RF module in wearable device, a shared symmetric encryption key is exchanged between the wearable and the external
device. In the key exchange scheme, the external device generates a key w and modulates it into vibration signal. Upon
receiving the vibration signal, the wearable uses two-feature
OOK demodulation scheme to convert signal to a bit string
w’. The wearable then encrypts a predefined, fixed confirmation message using w’ and transmits it to the external device
through RF channel. The successful decryption of the confirmation message using key w at the external device implicates
the successful key exchange and subsequent communication
is encrypted by using the key w.
Kim et al. [195] proposed an encryption module to
enhance secure data transmission between wearable devices
with lightweight micro-controller MSP430. The Advance
Encryption Scheme (AES) with optimal code size is implemented and compiled using “msp-gcc” for MSP430. In the
designed wearable device’s system architecture, the encryption
module with two interfaces for setting secret key and encrypting a cipher block is placed between the application layer and
transmission layer. As a result, all healthcare applications can
use this service before transmitting data to the cloud server.
To summarise, low power Key Generation and Exchange
schemes usually attempt to facilitate a key agreement between
the sender and receiver without actually transmitting the key in
the wireless channel using some common data both the sender
and receiver has access to, e.g., vital signs of the wear or using
another secure channel to transfer the key. Nonetheless, majority of the above work operate under the premise that wearable
devices are not capable enough to do asymmetric encryption due to their resource constraints and as a result secure
key sharing methods are required for symmetric encryption
key sharing. More recent work [196] have showed that some
state-of-the-art wearables are capable of asymmetric encryption without significant overheads. We believe this trend might
continue in some wearable device types such as smart watches
and fitness bands. As a result, low power key generation and
exchange procedures are likely to be used by more compact
devices such as smart ear-buds.
b) Authentication protocols: Authentication protocols
allow securely transfer authenticated data between two entities. Such protocols are essential as encryption alone does
not necessarily guarantee integretiy in communication where
malicious nodes can impersonate servers or relay units of
wearables to obtain unauthorised access to data.
Rahman et al. [197] proposed FitLock, a lightweight defence
system to protect Fitbit. FitLock implements a bind procedure
which allows the user to bind his new tracker to his social
network account, and an upload procedure which assures the
security for the Fitbit communication protocol. During the
bind procedure, the BindTrackerUser protocol between the
user, tracker and the Fitbit Web Server (WS) is conducted
that takes user account ID, tracker ID, shared secret symmetric encryption key between the tracker and the WS, and
database Map structure of the WS as input arguments. After
several steps exchanging these arguments, the user enters a

In a following work, Rahman et al. [180] proposed
SensCript, a secure protocol for data storage and transmission
in fitness centric social networks with lightweight personal

private data capture, tracker injection, and user account injection, but also to defend against attackers that physically capture

and written on tracker during data sync procedure.

the work in [198] integrated a Java Card Technology-based
smart card (JCard) into the wearable device (WD) providing the wearable with the ability of NFC connectivity and

authentication flows. One is authentication flow is between
the intermediate device and WD-NFC-JCard whereby the user
registers a trusted immediate device with the WD-NFC-JCard.
Then a NFC communication channel is set up between the
WD-NFC-JCard and immediate device to exchange and verify public key certificates and digital signature. The other
authentication flow is mutual authentication between the WDNFC-JCard and the remote server assuming that they are aware
of and trust the public key of each other. In this mutual
authentication, a trusted immediate device is used to forward
massages exchanged between them.
In order to protect Bluetooth-enabled health/medical
Android devices from the external Device Mis-Bonding,
Naveed et al. [183] proposed Dabinder, an OS-level access
control that automatically generates and enforces secure bonding policies between device and its authorised apps. Dabinder
controls app-device bonding in both pairing procedure and
communication stages. More specifically, once a device is activated, the user performs the pairing procedure between the
device and its authorised apps whereby Dabinder generates
a bonding policy which associates each device to its official
apps. When an app attempts to establish a Bluetooth connection with a device, a policy enforcement mechanism checks if
the app is associated in the bonding policy to the device. If the
app is in the device’s bonding policy, the request is allowed

to proceed, otherwise, it is declined. In addition, Dabinder
performs an un-pairing controller to manage pairing relation
between the phone and a device. Specifically, when an app
tries to un-pair the phone with a device, un-pairing controller
will find whether a bonding policy between the device and
this app is available. If there is no bonding policy found, the
app is reported as unauthorized and its un-pairing attempt is
stopped.
Authentication Protocols majorly attempt to address the
challenges related to data integrity and ensures that the sender
is communicating with the right receiver. The solutions we
discussed in this category come together with a encryption
solution and no wearable related work is found to tackle the
authentication challenges alone. Apart from software based
solutions, we found that there are hardware based solutions
tailored for NFC-enabled devices, that might find applications
in smart jewellery.
2) Ensuring Availability: As described in Section III-A,
threats to the availability of wearables are limited and as a
result not many work focused on that aspect of wearable security. In [197], the proposed solution FitLock not only protects
Fitbit from data capturing and data injection attacks but also
thwarts the DoS and Battery Drain attacks. As all communications between the tracker and Web server are encrypted
with their shared key and each communication session has a
monotonically increasing session ID, FitLock prevents attackers from obtaining data in uploading data process or replaying
the requests. The tracker simply drops the invalid requests or
requests with old session IDs, counters. As a result, it does
not consume resource for processing those requests.
C. Summary of Communication Security
In this section we surveyed the work that identified communication security threats in wearables and highlighted
proposed solutions to those threats. Several work highlighted
the issues of wearable device’s communication in terms of
confidentiality, integrity, and availability caused by improper
implementations of communication technology such as lack
of encryption, insufficient authentication methods, and not
using MAC address randomisation. Majority of the work highlighted vulnerabilities threatening confidentiality and integrity.
The proposed solutions predominantly focused on two aspects;
key generation and exchange procedures and authentication
protocols that address threats to both confidentiality and
integrity.
We also noticed that existing work are mainly focusing on
investigating communication security threats in BLE enabled
wearables as it is the most popular choice of communication
among consumer wearables. There were some limited work in
vulnerability analysis on NFC and ANT/ANT+ and we believe
further security analysis is required in these protocols as limited footprint devices such as smart jewellery are likely to be
using these protocols in future when they become mainstream.

A. Battery Advancements
Batteries are currently a major design factor in wearable
devices. To maintain the growth of the wearable market, batteries used for wearables should be compact, efficient, and long
lasting. In the last few years, we have witnessed an improvement in the batteries technology trying to meet the wearable
constraints by considering the battery size, shape, capacity,
and reliability [201].
There are different kinds of batteries which are used in
present day wearables [201], [202]. Lithium is still the key
element in wearables batteries because it is the lightest of all
metals and it operates at a high voltage [202]. Lithium coin or
button cells batteries are one of the widely used batteries for
small devices such as remote controls, hearing aids, watches,
and some of the earliest wearable devices [201]. These types of
batteries are well-suited to small and low-power wearables as
they are compact, light-weight, cost-efficient, and fairly safe.

However, they are not rechargeable and have high internal
resistance [203].
On the other hand, Lithium-ion (Li-ion) and Lithium
Polymer (Li-Poly) batteries are becoming the most common
in many wearable devices including smartwatches, wristbands and head-mounted wearables as discussed in Section II.
Both the Li-Poly and the Li-ion batteries are rechargeable,
lightweight, and have high power density. However, they
require special circuitry to keep them from exploding. They
usually come with protection circuitry that ensures the safe
operation of the battery [204]. The Li-Poly battery (also called
Lithium-ion polymer or a pouch cell design battery) is lighter,
more flexible, and have improved safety than the Li-ion battery. On the other hand, the Li-ion batteries are cheaper in
cost and have a better energy density than the Li-Poly [205]
but they generally suffer from aging even when not in use.
Recently, graphene-based batteries have emerged as an alternative. They are promised to be more efficient and flexible with
higher energy density and storage capacity than all of the current battery types and is expected to advance the wearable
technology. However, they are still under development and
expected to be much more expensive than other batteries [206].

batteries technology to meet the wearable constraints, batteries
are still one of the largest limitations in advancing the wearable
technology. Battery technologies have not evolved in the pace
the wearable products are advancing with respect to the hardware/software features and as a result, research community
has looked into the possibility of minimising the power consumption of wearables. We next discuss the available research
studies which achieve energy efficiency through minimising
power consumption.

Sensing can be highly power consuming as a result of using
many sensors in the wearable and the continuous sampling of
such sensors. Minimising sensing power consumption can be

However, this can degrade the performance of the system.
Therefore, minimising sensing power consumption without

1) Sensor Selection: Sensor Selection is a method to minimise sensing power consumption by selecting subset of
sensors to be turned on and turning off other sensors to save
power. For example, turning off power hungry sensors such as
GPS and WiFi can significantly minimise sensing power consumption. Consequently, some research studies investigated
the use of low-power inertial sensors such as accelerometers and gyroscopes instead of the power hungry GPS sensor
for pedestrian tracking applications [207], [208]. Inertial sensors are known to consume much less energy than GPS.
However, they can add various challenges when used for
tracking, such as different phone placements and phone orientation which might affect the performance of the system. To
address these challenges, Zhu et al. [207] introduced a robust
dead reckoning algorithm and an error-tolerant map matching
algorithm which achieves outdoor pedestrian tracking using
low power inertial sensors without affecting the performance
of the system. Similarly, Abdesslem et al. [208] proposed
a system for indoor/outdoor pedestrian tracking which can
use the low-power accelerometer sensor more often and the
more expensive GPS sensor less frequently. They relied on
the intuition that when the user is not moving, using sensors
for localisation is unnecessary. The accelerometer can thus be
used to detect when the user is not moving and then stop
GPS sensing to save energy. They showed that their system
can reduce sensing energy consumption by more than 58% for
localising a user indoor and outdoor.
Although inertial sensors are known to be energy efficient compared to GPS and WIFI, the continuous sampling
of many inertial sensors included in the wearable can be also
power consuming. Zappi et al. [209] introduced a sensor selection scheme to reduce inertial sensing power consumption for
human activity recognition application. The system exploits
the redundant and unreliable accelerometer sensors and relies
on a meta-classifier that fuses the information of classifiers
operating on individual sensors. Sensors are selected according to their contribution to classification accuracy as assessed
during system training. Their results showed that the lifetime
of the system can be significantly extended while keeping high
recognition accuracies.
On the other hand, Wang et al. [210] proposed an energy
efficient mobile sensing system (EEMSS) which not only
recognise user activities/states but also state transitions. They
incorporated a hierarchical sensor management scheme which
selectively turns on the minimum set of sensors to monitor
user state and triggers new set of sensors if necessary to
achieve state transition detection. By powering only a minimum set of sensors and using appropriate sensor duty cycles,
the authors showed significant improvement in device battery

life (more than 75%) while maintaining both high accuracy
and low latency in identifying transitions between end-user
activities. Moreover, Grordon et al. [211] proposes a sensors selection method for activity recognition which leverages
the predictability of human behaviour to conserve energy. By
predicting human behaviour, sensors which are not needed to
recognise the probable activities from each other can be turned
off conserving energy. Their results showed significant energy
savings of up to 84% without greatly impacting recognition
rates.
2) Adaptive Sampling: Adaptive sampling is a widely used
method to reduce sensing power consumption by reducing the
sampling rate of sensors. However, this reduction should be
achieved without affecting the information precision and the
system performance. Krause et al. [212] analysed the trade-off
between reducing the accelerometer sampling rate of eWatch
wearable device and the system performance in human activity recognition application. They used a collection of selective
sampling strategies in order to reduce the number of required
sensor readings. Their results indicate the existence of a sampling threshold, below which accuracy falls dramatically. By
selecting this sampling threshold, the lifetime of the device
can be extended by a factor of four without a significant loss
in activity recognition accuracy.
On the other hand, Yan et al. [213] studied the tradeoff between reducing the accelerometer sampling rate and
human activity recognition accuracy separately for each activity. They showed that such tradeoff is activity specific which
means different activities require different sampling rates to
achieve the desired accuracy levels. Based on that finding
they introduced an activity-sensitive strategy, called A3R,
which adapts the sampling frequency in real time, based on
the activity type. They showed that for users running the
A3R application on their Android phones an overall energy
savings of 20-25% could be achieved. Following this idea,
Qi et al. [214] proposed AdaSense algorithm which uses a
lower power single activity event detection most of the time.
It only resorts to multi-activity classification when an activity change is detected. Using Genetic Programming approach,
the authors further reduced the sampling rates of both single
activity event detection and multi-activity classification, leading to 39.4%-51.0% smartphone energy savings compared to
the A3R application.
While it is possible to extend the battery lifetime of
wearables through the previously mentioned energy-efficient
sensing solutions, battery-powered wearables cannot provide
sustained operation without the need for battery recharge and
eventually replacement. This motivates many researchers to
explore energy harvesting solutions to provide self-powered
wearables. In the next subsection, we discuss the available
energy harvesting-based solutions.

In this section, we provide a relatively high-level review
of existing works that investigate harvesting energy from
the ambient environment to build self-powered wearable

their usages in other disciplines, we encourage readers refer

In the rest of this section, we introduce recent advancements
and applications of energy harvesting in wearable devices.
Depending on the source where the ambient energy is harvested from, current EH-based solutions can be classified as;
1) Kinetic Energy Harvesting, 2) Thermoelectric Energy
Harvesting, and 3) Solar Energy Harvesting.
1) Kinetic Energy Harvesting (KEH): KEH has emerged
as a viable option to power the wearables by converting
the kinetic energy of human motion into electrical energy.
Several research studies started to build practical wearable devices powered by human motion in different form
factors [218]–[220].
Shoes is considered the most popular form factor in
designing KEH-powered wearables. The history of building shoe-based self-powered wearable can back to the late
nineties, during which the researchers in MIT started to
integrate magnetic generator with shoes [221]. In an earlier
work of Antaki et al. [222], the authors discovered that the
ground reaction forces associated with the heel strike and
toe-off phases of the gait can generate the largest amount
of energy during human walking. In this study, a piezoelectric array-based EH shoe has been built to generate
electric energy from human gait. Similarly, in the work of
Kymissis et al. [223], a piezoelectric generator is placed inside
the shoe and can generate 1.1-1.8mW average power during
walking. They have proved that the generated energy is able
to power the RFID transmitter to broadcast signal periodically. In addition to piezoelectric based solutions, in the work
of Duffy and Carroll [224], an electromagnetic generator was
used inside the shoe and an RMS voltage of 500mV could be
generated by the prototype at a 5 Hz frequency.
In a more recent study conducted by Huang et al. [225],
a prototype of self-powered shoe is presented. The prototype
consisted of an accelerometer and Bluetooth wireless communication unit which are powered separately by the energy
harvested from each of the two feet, and coordinated by ambient backscatter. Such that, the accelerometer can sense the
activity of the user, while the Bluetooth can transmit the
sensing results to the smartphone.
Many works in KEH-powered wearables also built their prototypes in form factors other than shoes. As reported in the
works of Granstrom et al. [226] and Feenstra et al. [227], a
novel energy harvesting backpack has been built, which can
effectively generate electrical energy through the differential
forces between the wearer and backpack. Their experiment
results shown that, in average, approximately 0.4mW of power
can be generated using their prototype, which is certainly sufficient to power some electronics with low energy consumption.

In the work of Yang and Yun [228], a fabric wearable platform embedded with piezoelectric energy harvester has been
designed, which can be worn on the elbow joints and fingers.
Their experiments shows an average output of 0.21mW can be
generated by the fabric from the movements of elbow joints
and fingers.
2) Thermoelectric Energy Harvesting (TEH): Thermoelectric energy harvesting (TEH) converts the temperature
differences between the ambient environment and human body
into electrical energy to power wearable devices.
The most popular TEH-powered wearable device is in the
form-factor of watch. In 1980, Bulova introduced the prototype of Thermatron [229]. Thermatron harvests energy from
the temperature difference between the back of the watch,
which contacts with the wrist of the wearer, and the top of
watch, which contacts with ambient air, to power the quartz
caliber. A similar example of TEH-powered watch is the
Seiko Thermic wristwatch [230], which also uses the temperature gradient between wrist skin and atmosphere to generate
energy. In the work of Leonov et al. [231]. A watch-size thermoelectric wrist generator was fabricated, and can generate
approximately 250µW in average during the daytime, using
the temperature difference between human skin and ambient
environment. They further proved the practical use of their prototype by using it to power a wireless pulse oximeter (SP O2
sensor) [232], [233].
As reported, the power consumption of the oximeter is
typically 62µW, while their thermoelectric wristwatch can
generate approximately 200µW during the daytime, which
is sufficient to power the whole system. Following the success of this work, they developed a battery-free body-powered
electroencephalography (EEG) to monitor electrical activity
of human brain [234], and electrocardiography (ECG) shirt to
record the electrical activity of heart [235].
3) Solar Energy Harvesting (SEH): Because of the abundant energy source and high power density, energy harvesting
from solar and ambient light has been widely studied in powering and extending the operational time of wireless sensor
networks [236]–[238]. In generally, solar energy harvesting
can achieve a power density up to 100mW/cm2 under outdoor
sunlight and 100µW/cm2 in indoor environment [215], which
is sufficient to power many wearable systems with low-energy
consumption.
Similarly, wrist watch is the most widely used form factor
for SEH-powered wearable devices. Many manufacturers have
released their own SEH-powered wrist watch [239]–[241]. For
instance, the SEIKO has launched the first Solar watch [239]
in 1977. The watch utilises a solar cell to harvest energy
from ambient light and power an rechargeable battery. With
1 minute quick harvesting from sunlight, it enables up to one
hour regular operation of the watch. Misra et al. [242] a wristworn solar-powered wearable platform has been designed and
built. The platform can continuously monitor the photoplethysmogram (PPG) of the wearer during daily activities.
In addition to wrist watch, Dionisi et al. [243] proposed
and designed a solar energy harvesting based self-powered
autonomous T-shirt for measuring different vital signs,
including respiration rate, heart rate, and body movement.

Energy harvesting is gradually finding its way in wearable
devices. It has shown great potential in mitigating the power
constraints of today’s battery-powered wearable devices, and
in improving the operation life of many wearable applications. In this section we have surveyed the existing efforts
in the design and development of energy harvesting-enabled
wearable devices. Depending on the source where the ambient energy is harvested from, we focused on three different
categories:1) Kinetic Energy Harvesting, 2) Thermoelectric
Energy Harvesting, and 3) Solar Energy Harvesting, and
discussed the most representative wearable prototypes in
those categories. We can see that shoes are the most popular type of form in designing energy harvesting powered
wearable devices [221]–[223], [225]. This is mainly because
of the potential of high energy harvesting rate from the
human heel strike. Another popular form of energy harvesting wearable devices is wrist watch, which is able to harvest
energy from all kind of energy sources. Examples such as
kinetic [218], thermoelectirc [230], [231], and solar powered
watches [239]–[241]. Moreover, with the recent advancements
in MEMs-based energy harvester, the form factor of energy
harvester are shrunk in the order of millimetres. This makes
it possible to embed those MEMS-based energy harvester into
many tiny wearable devices that have very limited form factor.
The feasibility of utilising a particular energy harvesting
technique for powering the wearable device is definitely application specific. It is hard to conclude which technique is more
preferable than the others. For instance, for the kind of devices
that will be carried or worn by the users during their daily
activities, such as fitness trackers and smart-shoes, kinetic
energy harvesting would be a good candidate, as more energy
could be scavenged from the users’ daily movement. However,
for the application scenarios such as elderly health monitoring
where the momentum of the user is low, kinetic energy is not
a good choice. Instead, solar energy harvesting, could be very
useful in those cases.
D. Summary of Energy Efficiency
Although battery technology has progressed over the years,
it was only a linear progress compared to electronic devices
which have shown an exponential growth [245]. Proposing
new materials and architectures, in addition to improving
surface structure, and chemical reactions, can advance the

technology of batteries for wearable devices. On the other
hand, minimising the power consumption of wearables through
energy-efficient solutions is a very active area of research as it
can extend the battery lifetime of wearables. However, users
still need to recharge or replace the battery of their wearable at some point. For instance, the very recent Apple Watch
can only supply power up to 18 hours of operation, and thus
needs daily recharging [246]. The need for frequent recharging makes the wearable less desirable, especially in health
applications such as aged-care, where the users may easily
forget to recharge their wearables. Therefore, providing selfpowered wearables through energy harvesting technology is
highly desirable. Energy harvesting (EH) is a fast growing
area which attracts considerable efforts from both research
community and industry. We can expect that, future advancements in energy harvesting will change our way in powering
wearable devices. Indeed, significant advancements have been
recently made in the EH hardware technology leading to
many off-the-shelf products available at low cost [247]–[249].
These developments point to future wearables and mobile
devices that will be equipped with EH hardware to ease the
dependence on batteries and potentially provide self-powered
wearables [250].
