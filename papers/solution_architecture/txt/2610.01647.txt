Abstract—In this article, we present our vision
and recent advancements toward creating a decentralized system capable of learning from realtime data within buildings to support sustainable
and privacy-preserving smart environments. Our approach promotes the concept of the building itself as
the data center, aligning with the principles of edge
computing to safeguard confidentiality and reduce
reliance on external cloud infrastructure. This is
particularly valuable in humanitarian contexts, where
data sovereignty, energy efficiency, and infrastructure constraints are critical. We detail a lightweight,
"Kubernetes-like" orchestration framework for deploying AI services within such environments and
demonstrate our progress in implementing AI algorithms on low-power, cost-effective microcontrollers
such as those in the Arduino ecosystem. By enabling
in-situ learning directly on sensors or microcontrollers, our work aims to bring intelligent services
to resource-limited settings, fostering autonomy, resilience, and sustainable development in vulnerable or
underserved communities. The contributions in this
article are related, firstly, to our project "Online Machine Learning Algorithms for Embedded Systems"
and the evaluation of two new online algorithms.
Secondly, we envision a cloud-fog-edge architecture
based on the KOptim and FIWARE components,
and we propose a methodology for coupling them.
Experimental results of the online algorithms are also
presented, showcasing real-world traces.
Index Terms—AI-driven decision-making under
uncertainty in real-world settings, Smart Technologies
and IoT, Real-world deployments of autonomous AI
in Smart Building, Online machine learning.

I. I NTRODUCTION
The principal aim of this paper is to advance
inclusive and sustainable technology research in the
domains of smart building Information and Communication Technologies (ICT), electrical engineering, communications, and edge computing. With
a humanitarian focus, we explore technological
solutions that minimize environmental impact and
are accessible to under-resourced or marginalized
communities. Our approach advocates for alternatives to energy-intensive cloud computing and
centralized infrastructures, proposing instead ecologically responsible, locally deployable systems
based on cost-effective microcontrollers.

The ICT sector is increasingly scrutinized for its
substantial environmental footprint, driven by high
energy consumption in computation, storage, and
networking infrastructures [1], [2]. In 2023, global
electricity consumption from ICT was estimated
at around 2 %, with the figure rising to 4.4 % in
the United States [3]. Forecasts suggest that global
Data Center (DC) electricity usage may reach 3%
by 2030 [4]. In addition to carbon emissions, artificial intelligence (AI) and high-performance computing (HPC) technologies require significant water
and rare metal resources, the latter often being
non-recyclable. These trends raise critical questions
about the sustainability and equity of digital transformation, particularly in humanitarian contexts
where resource constraints are pronounced.
Simultaneously, electricity use in buildings accounted for nearly 60% of global consumption
growth in 2024, with natural gas demand also rising
by 1 % in residential and commercial sectors [4].
The intersection of energy-intensive computing and
increasing building energy use presents a compelling case for rethinking infrastructure. This paper promotes edge computing as a potential pathway to mitigate these pressures, emphasizing in-situ
intelligence that reduces the need for data transmission and centralized processing. While the overall
environmental cost of edge computing relative to
cloud services remains underexplored, the promise
of reduced energy usage, enhanced data privacy,
and local autonomy warrants deeper investigation.
Our research problem is thus framed by the needs
of individuals, public institutions, or private entities
managing activity within buildings—whether optimizing operations through embedded AI or regulating high-usage environments to conserve resources.
We present two use cases aligned with these goals.
In both, we explore the design and deployment of
an embedded AI system built on edge computing
principles. Such a system must operate independently within the building, continuously learning
from real-time sensor data to support resilient,
efficient, and sustainable outcomes.

The structure of this paper is organized as follows: In Section II, we provide definitions relevant
to our domain, present a usage scenario, and review
related literature. Section III details two categories
of experiments undertaken, encompassing the materials employed and the methodologies adopted.
The initial experiment focuses on cloud orchestration in conjunction with FIREWARE, while the
subsequent experiments pertain to the application
of online machine learning algorithms at the edge.
Section IV offers an analysis and interpretation of
the results, situating them within the established
theoretical framework. We also explain the drawbacks of our proposed method. Finally, Section
V offers conclusions and proposes directions for
future research.
II. D EFINITIONS AND R ELATED W ORKS
A. Important definitions
NIST, a US government agency, defines cloud
computing as "a model for on-demand network
access to a shared pool of configurable resources
(e.g., networks, servers, storage, applications, and
services) that can be quickly provisioned and released with minimal management or provider effort". This IT shift offers many benefits, redefining
the landscape. Cloud computing’s flexibility surpasses on-site solutions, allowing data and application access from anywhere with the Internet. Its
scalability, accessibility, security, and collaborative
features make it vital for enterprises and research
centers, promoting innovation, agility, and digital
transformation.
Edge computing executes programs close to the
request sources for rapid response. In his IEEE
DAC 2014 keynote [5], Karim Arabi defined it as
computing outside the cloud, at the network edge,
for applications requiring fast data processing.
NIST defines IoT (Internet of Things [6]) as "the
network of devices with the hardware, software,
firmware, and actuators to connect and exchange
data." IoT connects devices to the Internet and is
related to edge computing, though they differ: edge
computing processes data locally, while IoT devices
typically send data to the cloud. As IoT devices
gain computing power1 , this distinction is fading.
Fog computing’s importance is growing, with nodes
closer to users reducing network latencies. Fog
computing [7] enables efficient data processing via
intermediary nodes between the cloud and IoT
objects.

that balances architectural designs across cloud,
fog, and edge nodes. Let us imagine that a municipality needs to manage its social housing stock and

B. Related work on cloud orchestrator
Contemporary cloud orchestrator frameworks
predominantly depend on container management tools engineered by diverse corporations
and open source communities, including Docker
SwarmKit [8], Apache Mesos [9], Red Hat OpenShift [10], and Google Kubernetes [11].
Docker SwarmKit [8] is crucial for container
scheduling, assigning containers to nodes using
filtering and selection. Mesos [12], an Apache
project, is a resource-sharing layer for cluster
frameworks, with Marathon 3 managing applications on it. Marathon launches containers anywhere
in the Mesos cluster, ensuring fault tolerance, but
it can also place them on specific nodes using
constraints.
RedHat OpenShift [10] is a container orchestration platform enabling web application development and deployment without dedicated servers.
Managed via CLI or Web interface [13], it supports
backend services, APIs, stateful apps, big data,
AI/ML, edge, serverless, and event-driven computing.
2 https://intensiscore.m2intenses.com/
3 https://mesosphere.github.io/marathon/

Google Kubernetes [11] is a leading open-source
tool for automating the deployment, scaling, and
management of container-based applications. It employs Pods, which can operate on-premises or in
public clouds. Clusters consist of a Kubernetes
master and several worker nodes. The scheduler
assigns Pods to nodes by first filtering out nodes
that don’t meet the requirements, leaving Pods
pending if resources are lacking, and then scoring
and selecting the best node from the remaining
options.
In this work, we highlight the latest advances
in KOptim [14], which offers a generic framework
for scheduling and resource management. Compared to current Kubernetes-based frameworks and
scheduling strategies, KOptim has two main benefits: (i) it simplifies container resource configuration
through Service Level Agreement (SLA) classes,
and (ii) it enhances online container scheduling
using a Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM)
algorithm. KOptim serves as a generic framework
for various Kubernetes distributions, including K3s
and MicroK8s, which are tailored to Fog and Edge
computing environments. The crucial reason for using KOptim is its ability to manage dynamic SLA,
meaning that instead of specifying strict constraints
on CPU, RAM, networking, and disk resources, we
rely on a range of values, thus providing more flexibility and adapting to resource-constrained devices.
However, in this paper, we do not explain all the
workings of the KOptim mechanics due to space
constraints in the article, and we refer the reader to
the bibliography.
C. Related work on AI for smart buildings
Building operating costs are mainly due to
HVAC systems, which require monitoring of indoor
temperature, weather, equipment consumption, and
occupancy. Secondary factors enhancing thermal
understanding include precise schedules, sunshine,
and HVAC heat/power ratio. Environmental and
equipment data are critical for smart buildings, integrating through BIM (Building Information Management) and BMS (Building Management Systems).
We consider online machine learning algorithms
[15], [16] where data arrive sequentially and update
the predictor at each step. Batch learning generates a predictor by processing all training data at
once. Continuous learning involves ongoing model
improvement amid continuous data, handling the
gradual influx of new information. The approach
involves utilizing an offline algorithm constrained
by a window of limited size, which is particularly
necessary given the limitations inherent in embedded devices. We implement drift control through
various methodologies; for example, by organizing
the data post-processing by the offline algorithm,

followed by sorting and systematically selecting
and eliminating data points at regular intervals. This
method aims to maintain diversity throughout the
process.
D. Related work on IoT benchmarking, workload
emulation characterization
The work done for this article has its roots in
the project "Online Machine Learning for Embedded Systems"4 , whose objective is to offer online
algorithms for embedded hardware. The project is
a collection of algorithms in Python or C/C++ covering the most useful machine learning algorithmic
expressions.
This project is closely related to complementary
needs in our context, as those in [17]–[19]. In [17],
the authors introduce U-TOE, a universal toolkit
intended to support IoT designers and researchers.
It integrates features of a low-power embedded
operating system, a generic model transpiler and
compiler, a built-in performance evaluation module, and an open-access remote IoT testbed. The
authors release an open-source implementation of
U-TOE and illustrate how it can be leveraged to
experimentally assess the performance of different
neural network models on a broad spectrum of
low-power IoT boards built on widely used microcontroller architectures. U-TOE enables straightforward, reproducible, and customizable comparative
performance studies across diverse IoT hardware
platforms simultaneously. In contrast, our work
concentrates not on neural network methods but on
online machine learning algorithms.
In [18], the authors present a straightforward yet
broadly applicable method to model the workload
of representative IoT applications, aiming to provide a realistic and reproducible means of emulating IT loads for data centers. Their method decomposes IoT applications into application primitives,
which are then used to emulate the workload that
the modeled application would generate. The paper
ends with a case study demonstrating IoT Workload
Emulation in the BodenTypeDC experiment, where
innovative cooling strategies for data centers were
evaluated under realistic workload conditions. By
contrast, in our scenario, the architecture is fixed
through the use of KOptim and FIWARE.
In [19], the authors tackle the problem of workload characterization, which is a fundamental step
for conducting performance and Quality of Service
engineering analyses. A system’s workload is defined as the collection of all inputs it receives from
its environment over one or more time intervals.
Characterizing this workload involves identifying
the nature of its basic components and providing a
quantitative and probabilistic description of these
components in terms of arrival processes, event

counts, and service demands. Although multiple
workload characterization studies have been proposed across various domains, IoT workloads have
largely been overlooked. This gap is precisely what
the paper aims to fill, and it additionally includes

III. E XPERIMENTS
A. Materials and methods
Our experimental research is organized into two
distinct branches. The first branch focuses on the
implementation of the proposed cloud orchestrator
integrated with a FIWARE component [20], [21].
The FIWARE Foundation is instrumental in the
establishment and implementation of open-source
standards crucial to the development of modular
and interoperable smart solutions. These standards
facilitate the creation of such solutions efficiently,
economically, and quickly, while mitigating the risk
of vendor lock-in and fostering FIWARE as a sustainable and innovation-centric business ecosystem.
In our case, we propose a FIWARE standard to
make the cloud orchestrator interoperable with a
database of policies for dynamic Service Level
Agreements.
The second branch pertains to the implementation of machine learning algorithms tailored for the
Arduino ecosystem, specifically adapted for the microcontroller context. We illustrate the challenges
of building online solutions, and we conduct experiments on real datasets from the smart building
sector.
B. Results
1) KOptim and FIWARE from a methodological
point of view: In paper [22], KOptim is introduced as a Kubernetes framework devised under
the fogSLAs project [23], aiming to streamline
resource management by associating Service Level
Agreements (SLAs) with containers in a cloud
infrastructure. Despite frequent discussions around
SLAs, the deployment or sharing of open-source
solutions remains infrequent. The novel contribution of KOptim lies in its reliance on dynamic
SLAs, signifying that these agreements can evolve.
To achieve this, a range of values has been implemented in place of the conventional fixed resource
quantities typical of traditional cloud environments.
Current open-source Service Level Agreement
(SLA) implementations predominantly concentrate
on defining structures rather than on providing
comprehensive toolkits for production management. Notwithstanding, they exhibit a standard

pattern for SLA parameters, necessitating the definition of a range to indicate compliance and
a timeframe to evaluate any violations. Among
the array of open-source solutions, it is observed
that FIWARE [20], [21] uniquely offers extensive
management tools, including an SLA definition
framework and a dashboard for monitoring active
SLAs. Considering FIWARE’s aspiration to establish itself as a standard in distributed systems, it
presents an ideal platform for the development of
our dynamic SLA prototype. The fogSLA project
holds the potential to augment Fiware’s capacity to
support pure microservice or as-a-service hosting
methodologies.
We implemented an architecture in which the
KOptim scheduler communicates with the FIWARE
environment. We used the WS-Agreement scripting
from FIWARE’s SLA Framework to define SLA
classes for managing KOptim container limits. We
are now working to set up a monitoring solution
for dynamic resources and SLOs. The complete
architecture is depicted in Figure 2, where we assume that an operator in the Smart Building sector
manages a private cloud based on KOptim. The
KOptim scheduler deploys containers for running
online machine learning algorithms (see forthcoming subsections). Koptim can deploy services on
microcontrollers, but also on fog nodes (see Figure
1) to collect information produced at the edge
level. Depending on the traffic between the fog
and edge levels, some fog nodes can become busy,
necessitating a new negotiation of the resources
associated with services. At this time, KOptim asks
the FIWARE component to find the "best" policy
and resource number that does not violate the SLA
expressed by the smart building operator.

2) Data mining from the Perret Tower with the
GHA dimension reduction algorithm: This study
focuses on applying a dimensionality reduction algorithm to a dataset originating from the Tour Perret building in Grenoble. Dimensionality reduction
involves the transformation of data from a highdimensional space into a lower-dimensional space,
ensuring that the reduced representation retains
essential characteristics of the original data, ideally
approximating its intrinsic dimension. The Tour
Perret dataset comprises up to 15 dimensions. Our
objective is to achieve a two-dimensional representation to facilitate more effective visual analysis. In
this context, we demonstrate the implementation of
the GHA-PCA algorithm [24].
We now discuss the adaptation of the abstract
algorithm to fit our requirements for running on
embedded devices and, more importantly, to transform it into an online algorithm.
The developed code utilizes the Eigen library
for matrix operations, which is a popular choice in
C++ for linear algebra tasks. This C++ code retains
the core functionality of the original Python code
while adapting it to the syntax and conventions of
C++. The program will process the data in blocks
of 1024 lines of the Perret Tower data [25], display
the iteration number at each step, and update the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors accordingly. In the
end, the results will be saved in a CSV file and
displayed graphically.
a) Explanation: We have introduced a constant W that defines the size of the buffer (1024
lines). The main loop now runs through the data in
blocks of 1024 lines. At each iteration, we display
the current iteration number and the lines currently
being processed. For each block of 1024 lines,
we apply the GHA algorithm to each line of the
block. If the total number of lines is not a multiple
of 1024, the last block will be smaller. We use
min(W, n − i) to handle this. The Eigen template
library already manages memory dynamically.
The header file ghapca.h contains the declarations of variables, constants, and shared functions
needed for the source code6 to be compiled into
a file. In a second program7 , there is a special
feature, namely, a section where the data is normalized. This practice allows for transforming the

b) Analysis: The differences between the results in C++ and Python8 programs can be attributed to several factors. The C++ program processes the data in blocks of 1024 rows, while the
Python program processes the data sequentially.
This may affect how the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are updated. The initial parameters, such
as the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors, may be
initialized differently in the two programs, which
may influence the final results. The C++ program
uses an online approach to update the eigenvalues
and eigenvectors, while the Python program uses
a batch approach. This may lead to differences in
the convergence of the results. The Python program
ignores rows without payload, which can reduce
the number of data rows processed, while the C++
program does not seem to have this logic. The
C++ program normalizes the data by ignoring some
columns (1, 3, and 6), while the Python program
normalizes all columns. This can lead to differences
in the values of the processed data. The possible
causes are i) Constant column: Columns 1, 3, and
6 may contain a constant value (e.g., all elements
are 0, 1, or some other fixed value).; ii) Missing or
poorly formatted data: If columns 1,3, and 6 contain
missing or poorly formatted data, it could lead
to a situation where all values are the same after
processing; iii) Data loading issue: The CSV file
might not be loaded properly, resulting in incorrect
values in columns 1, 3, and 6.
c) How to bypass the previous difficulties related to data quality: We implemented the following solutions. If columns 1, 3, and 6 are not important for your analysis, you can ignore them when
normalizing. If columns 1, 3, and 6 are important
but contain constant values, you can replace these
values with a default value or a small variation to
allow normalization. If normalization is a problem,
you can consider using standardization (subtract the
mean and divide by the standard deviation) instead
of normalization. This works even if the values
are constant (although the standard deviation is
zero in this case, which would also require special
handling).

groups such that data points in the same group are
more similar to other data points in the same group

the centroids as small as possible. The “means”

IV. D ISCUSSION
This work aims to develop an AI-based building
management system with two main components.
The first automates AI algorithm deployment to
manage load fluctuations by integrating dynamic
SLAs. We propose using the established opensource FIWARE standard to decouple SLA management. This is necessary as the Kubernetesbased scheduler remains a closed system, accessible
through FIWARE.
We have developed and tested two online AI
algorithm examples that are foundational to machine learning and useful in the context of smart

buildings. The key challenge is to create memoryefficient solutions for our microcontroller library.
Our solutions apply to the ARDUINO ecosystem,
and some algorithms are also designed for the
Micropython ecosystem, another platform for microcontroller programming.
Regarding the development aspect of our work,
limitations include a time lag between ecosystem
algorithm developments (ARDUINO and Micropython), an incomplete understanding of online algorithms for building management, and the need for
feedback on additional datasets.
More generally, it’s important to revisit the assumptions underlying this work, which some may
disagree with. For example, we assumed that the
cloud orchestrator was ultimately a black box that
we didn’t control. Therefore, the orchestrator is
assumed to run in a private cloud that we don’t
own.
On the other hand, we assumed that the SLA
management module, using FIWARE, which is
open source (for the part we’re interested in), could
be deployed within the building, along with the
measurement sensors. The "building is the data
center" approach is, in our case, somewhat loose.
We could very well use a cloud orchestrator that
would also be deployed within the building. Our
choice is driven by the fact that a building manager
(a municipal authority, for example) often manages
several buildings, so deploying an orchestrator for
each building is undoubtedly more expensive. This
is a sizing issue that, while important, is not addressed in our article.
Furthermore, on the downside, we believe our
proposal is less suitable for a situation where we
need to train a general model (in the machine learning sense) for all buildings. Indeed, this scenario
would require uploading all the data to the cloud,
which can generate network traffic. However, by
placing an orchestrator in each building and using
federated learning techniques [27], we could simply
exchange model "summaries" to build a general
model available in each building. This could be a
potential extension of our work.
V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS
This paper seeks to consolidate clearly formulated perspectives for edge-AI researchers, system
architects, policymakers, and domain specialists,
enabling them to investigate approaches, frameworks, and governance structures that uphold responsible autonomy and transparency in practical
AI deployments.
This paper outlines our perspective and recent
progress towards developing a decentralized system
that learns from real-time building data to support
smart and sustainable environments with a focus
on privacy. We propose treating the building itself

as the data hub, adhering to edge computing concepts to protect privacy and lessen dependence on
external cloud services.
This is especially important in humanitarian settings, where data ownership, energy conservation,
and infrastructure limitations are crucial. We describe a lightweight orchestration framework akin
to "Kubernetes" for the deployment of AI services within these environments and exhibit our
advances in applying AI algorithms on low-power,
affordable microcontrollers, such as those in the
Arduino ecosystem. By facilitating on-site learning directly on sensors or microcontrollers, our
initiative aims to introduce intelligent services to
resource-constrained settings, enhancing autonomy,
resilience, and sustainable growth in vulnerable or
underserved communities.
In summary, our future work aims to address
the challenges we introduced in the discussion
section, specifically those related to the delay in
advances in ecosystem algorithms (ARDUINO and
Micropython), a better understanding of online AI
algorithms for building management, the need for
feedback on additional datasets, and finally, those
related to federated learning if the building manager
needs to have a general model of what is happening
in the buildings they manage.
