A BSTRACT
Edge intelligence requires models to sense continuously in real time and to keep
adapting on-device, all under tight compute, energy, and memory budgets. Although spiking neural networks (SNNs) enable efficient event-driven inference,
standard surrogate-gradient backpropagation (BP) serializes updates and blocks
ongoing inference. We investigate Bidirectional Spike-Based Distillation (BSD)
as an on-device learning principle that lets edge SNNs learn while inferring. BSD
couples a stimulus-driven forward network with an independent target-driven reverse network and aligns their intermediate representations through local objectives. Because the two pathways have disjoint computation graphs until alignment, forward inference, reverse inference, and stage-wise updates can run concurrently. On 25 benchmarks spanning the five sensing modalities of SOUL, BSD
stays within 3.8 percentage points of matched BP baselines on average, while
only its forward branch is needed at deployment. The learned representations also
transfer well to few-shot class-incremental learning without replaying past data.
By removing the dense floating-point backward chain, BSD reduces the projected
training latency to 0.72× and the estimated training energy to 0.36× that of BP.

I NTRODUCTION

Edge computing brings intelligent processing directly to the sensors and end users that generate
continuous data streams (Shi et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2019). A defining trait of such deployments—
whether in visual monitoring, keyword spotting, or wearable activity tracking—is that devices must
sense continuously and in real time; they cannot simply stop watching or listening. Meanwhile, the
operational environment is inherently non-stationary: after initial deployment, devices frequently
encounter novel situations, such as a new user’s voice or an unfamiliar spatial layout (Zhou et al.,
2019; Zhang et al., 2026), and must therefore keep updating their models (McMahan et al., 2017;
Parisi et al., 2019). Standard gradient-based training, however, imposes a strict sequential dependency: every forward pass must be followed by a complete global backward pass, which blocks
the device from processing new inputs during the update (Di Yu et al., 2024). Integrating continual adaptation into always-on sensors therefore requires the ability to learn while inferring, so that
ongoing prediction and parameter updates overlap instead of blocking each other.
Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are particularly attractive for edge deployment because their sparse,
event-driven communication and stateful temporal dynamics naturally suit sequential sensor streams
(Roy et al., 2019; Davies et al., 2018). However, although SNNs greatly reduce overall inference
cost on neuromorphic hardware, their standard training methods are at odds with concurrent learning. Surrogate-gradient backpropagation (BP) propagates continuous floating-point errors through
every layer and time step (Neftci et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2018; Shrestha & Orchard, 2018). This

global dependency chain not only prevents inference during training but also incurs high latency and
energy costs. Prior studies of on-device training show that naively ported, globally coupled training
workloads are prohibitively slow and energy-intensive on battery-powered devices (Cai et al., 2020;
Lin et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023). ANN-to-SNN conversion does not fundamentally avoid this problem either, since it adds a sequential two-stage pipeline that still relies on dense ANN computation
(Deng et al., 2020). Sparse spiking inference alone therefore cannot remove the blocking behavior
and resource bottleneck of standard gradient-based training workloads.
To enable learning while inferring, we studied Bidirectional Spike-Based Distillation (BSD) as a
direct learning principle for edge SNNs. Originally proposed as a biologically plausible learning
algorithm (Wang et al., 2026), BSD dispenses with the global error-backpropagation chain. Instead,
it couples a stimulus-driven forward spiking network with an independent, target-driven reverse
spiking network, and aligns their intermediate representations with local relaxed contrastive (ReCo)
objectives (Lin et al., 2023) that provide layer-wise learning signals (Figure 1). Because the two
branches run independently until their local losses are computed, neither depends on the other’s
intermediate outputs. This decoupling allows inference, reverse inference, and local updates to
execute concurrently on parallel hardware, so that an edge SNN can learn while inferring.
Beyond concurrency, this independence also reduces training time and energy. By avoiding a
floating-point error chain across all layers and time steps, BSD reduces the overall computational
workload. Its layer-local gradient computations are mutually independent, which exposes parallelism and shortens the critical path of each training step. Moreover, replacing dense floating-point
backpropagation with spike-based reverse inference substantially lowers the operation-level energy,
bringing routine on-device adaptation within practical battery budgets.
Finally, the efficiency of local learning matters only if the learned representations remain strong. In
dynamic edge scenarios, novel concepts typically arrive with only a few labels, and privacy constraints often preclude replaying historical data (Hersche et al., 2022). This setting is formalized
as few-shot class-incremental learning (FSCIL) (Tao et al., 2020). Evaluating BSD under FSCIL,
we show that even without global backpropagation, representations learned through local alignment
remain discriminative and forward-compatible, supporting rapid adaptation to novel classes.
We evaluate BSD on the five edge-sensing modalities of the SOUL benchmark (Deng et al., 2025):
vision, motion, acoustic, wireless, and neuromorphic sensing. Our contributions are:

R ELATED W ORK

Edge computing and online learning. Edge intelligence deploys inference and adaptation near
data sources under tight computing, memory, and energy budgets (Shi et al., 2016; Zhou et al.,
2019). Model compression and compact architectures reduce inference cost (Han et al., 2016; Lin
et al., 2020), and neuromorphic hardware exploits event-driven dynamics for low-power temporal sensing (Roy et al., 2019; Davies et al., 2018). Recently, the SOUL benchmark standardized
cross-modal evaluation of edge SNNs (Deng et al., 2025), and a modality-aware study showed that
the efficiency benefits of SNNs vary across sensing modalities and hardware platforms (Du et al.,
2026). Beyond static deployment, edge devices must adapt to novel concepts with scarce labels and
without replaying past data, a challenge formalized by FSCIL (Tao et al., 2020). Prototype classifiers enable low-overhead class expansion (Snell et al., 2017), while classifier evolution, semantic
distillation, and forward-compatible representations mitigate catastrophic forgetting (Zhang et al.,
2021; Cheraghian et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022). C-FSCIL bounds the trainable memory footprint
(Hersche et al., 2022), and SAFA-SNN performs sparse on-device adaptation of SNNs (Zhang et al.,
2026). These works make FSCIL a natural testbed for on-device adaptation under tight budgets.
Training spiking neural networks. Because SNN efficiency depends on event sparsity, simulation latency, and hardware support, low inference cost does not by itself imply efficient training
(Deng et al., 2020). Direct training with surrogate gradients assigns credit across layers and time
steps (Neftci et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2018; Shrestha & Orchard, 2018), and later work reduces its
latency and memory footprint (Meng et al., 2022; 2023). Training cost has been further reduced
through temporal gradient re-weighting (Deng et al., 2022), online updates that avoid storing the
history (Xiao et al., 2022; Bellec et al., 2020), and sparsity-promoting objectives (Kundu et al.,
2021). However, these methods remain tied to global backpropagation, whose cross-layer error
chain serializes execution and blocks ongoing inference. Decoupled learning methods break this
dependency through feedback alignment, direct feedback alignment, local error signals, synthetic
gradients, and greedy layer-wise objectives (Lillicrap et al., 2016; Nøkland, 2016; Mostafa et al.,
2018; Jaderberg et al., 2017; Belilovsky et al., 2019). For SNNs, DECOLLE learns from local readouts (Kaiser et al., 2020) and EC-SNN splits deep SNNs across edge devices (Di Yu et al., 2024);
beyond SNNs, Counter-Current Learning trains two opposing networks (Kao & Hariharan, 2024).
BSD (Wang et al., 2026) follows this direction with two opposing spiking pathways that are aligned
locally at matched stages. Yet whether BSD is viable across edge sensing modalities, whether it supports concurrent inference and learning, and how well its representations generalize under few-shot
incremental learning have not been systematically studied.

F EW-S HOT C LASS -I NCREMENTAL L EARNING

Few-shot class-incremental learning (FSCIL) models settings in which the label space expands after
deployment under tight supervision and storage constraints (Tao et al., 2020). Learning starts with
a base session D0 over a class set C 0 , which is used to learn the initial representation. The system
then encounters an ordered sequence of incremental sessions D1 , . . . , DQ . Each incremental session
q > 0 introduces a class set C q that is disjoint from all previously observed classes (C q ∩ C j = ∅
for 0 ≤ j < q) and provides only K labeled support samples for each of its N new classes (N way K-shot).

4.4

T IME - AND E NERGY-AWARE BSD E XECUTION

A BSD training step consists of four phases (Figure 2): forward inference (TF ), reverse-branch
inference (TR ), local loss and gradient computation (TL ), and parameter update (TU ). The decoupled structure of BSD provides two sources of concurrency. First, because the forward and reverse
branches have independent computation graphs until the ReCo objectives are formed, forward and
reverse inference can run concurrently, so the branch latency on the critical path is max(TF , TR )
rather than TF + TR . This property underlies learning while inferring: an edge system can keep
inferring on incoming sensor data while target-driven learning signals are computed alongside. Second, because each stage is trained by its own local loss (Section 4.1), the gradient computation of
stage i (taking time TL,i ) depends only on the representations recorded at alignment point i and need
not wait for error signals from later layers. Likewise, the disjoint parameter blocks can be updated
independently (taking time TU,i ). In contrast, BP must propagate errors sequentially through the full
network depth, which serializes the backward workload and yields a step latency of TF + TL + TU .
With P parallel workers assigned to stage-level tasks, local gradient computations and parameter
updates can run concurrently, and the ideal BSD step latency becomes

Locality also reduces energy. BP propagates continuous floating-point errors through every layer
and time step, which requires dense multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations amounting to roughly
twice the dense forward workload. BSD replaces this backward chain with event-driven reverse
inference, whose synaptic operations act on binary spikes and therefore reduce to accumulations
(ACs), plus local gradient computations confined to each stage. This substitution is the main source
of the training-energy savings quantified in Section 5.4.

We evaluate BSD on the SOUL edge-sensing benchmark (Deng et al., 2025), which provides a
unified, reproducible protocol for 25 tasks spanning five sensing modalities: vision (image classification), motion (activity recognition from inertial signals), acoustic sensing (environmental sound,
keyword, and music-genre classification), wireless sensing (activity, identity, and gesture recognition from Wi-Fi channel state information, radar, and point clouds), and neuromorphic sensing
(event-based vision and audio). The individual datasets are listed in Table 1.
To feed these heterogeneous raw streams to SNNs, we preprocess them into tensors of regular shape
and scale. Images are normalized to [0, 1] and lightly augmented with standard spatial transforms.
Motion signals are segmented into fixed windows (typically 128 time steps) that keep an explicit
time axis for recurrent processing. Audio waveforms are converted into 64 × 64 single-channel logmel spectrograms. Wireless measurements are standardized and reshaped into matrices that follow
the native antenna layout or into voxel grids. Event streams are binned in time and rasterized into
polarity-separated frame sequences. Each BSD model and its matched BP baseline consume exactly
the same preprocessed tensors and train/test splits (including the base and incremental sessions in
FSCIL), so the learning rule is the only experimental variable. Dataset details, tensor layouts, and
modality-specific augmentations are given in Appendix C.

84.89%). The gap is small on most tasks: it is within 3 points on 11 of the 25 benchmarks and
within 5 points on 19. BSD tracks BP most closely where the baseline accuracy is high; for example,
it exceeds 90% on all motion benchmarks. The remaining gap is concentrated on noisier or datalimited tasks such as Widar3, BullyDetect, and GSC.
The results on wireless tasks are partly affected by batch-size constraints. Because of the memory
footprint of their large input tensors, several wireless benchmarks were trained with small minibatches of 4 to 16 samples. As analyzed in Appendix E, the contrastive term of ReCo needs enough
instance pairs per batch to shape the intermediate representations effectively. Since deployment
uses only the forward spiking branch and BSD substantially reduces training latency and energy
(Section 5.4), we regard this level of accuracy as a favorable trade-off for real-time edge adaptation.

C ONCLUSION

This work studies BSD as an on-device learning principle for edge SNNs. To avoid the blocking
behavior and resource cost of surrogate-gradient BP, BSD splits computation into a stimulus-driven
forward branch and a target-driven reverse branch aligned by local objectives. With disjoint computation graphs until alignment, the two branches and their local updates can run concurrently, letting
edge devices learn while inferring. On 25 benchmarks spanning five edge sensing modalities, BSD
stays within 3.8 percentage points of matched BP baselines on average while deploying only the
forward branch, and its representations transfer well to 5-shot class-incremental learning. Branchand stage-level parallelism reduces the projected step latency to 0.72× that of BP, and the estimated
training energy is lower on all 25 datasets (geometric mean 0.36×). These results position local
bidirectional distillation as a promising foundation for always-on edge learning.

R EPRODUCIBILITY S TATEMENT
The authors have made extensive efforts to ensure the reproducibility of the empirical results reported in this paper. First, detailed dataset characteristics and preprocessing procedures are documented in Appendix C. Second, comprehensive implementation details, covering network architectures, hyperparameter settings, training procedures, and evaluation protocols, are provided in
Appendices C and D. All accuracy results are averaged over three runs, and the source code, configurations, and plotting scripts will be released upon publication.

E THICS S TATEMENT
Our experiments use public benchmark datasets spanning human activity, identity, audio, wireless,
and event-camera sensing; each dataset was collected under the protocols of its original study, and
this work collects no new human data. Wireless and camera-free sensing can enable monitoring or
identification without a subject’s awareness, so applications built on such models should be deployed
only with informed consent and appropriate access control. On-device processing reduces the need
to transmit raw sensor data, but does not by itself guarantee privacy or prevent misuse. Finally, our
efficiency figures are operation-level estimates and projections rather than measurements on specific
hardware, and should be interpreted as such.

C.2

M ODEL A RCHITECTURES

Feedforward models. Image, spectrogram, event-frame, and matrix-shaped inputs are processed
by a convolutional SNN whose blocks comprise convolution, optional batch normalization, LIF
dynamics, and 2 × 2 pooling, followed by flattening and a linear readout. Channel widths, pooling
locations, and kernel sizes are adapted to each input resolution (e.g., channels [128, 256, 256] for
CIFAR10-DVS). The BSD reverse branch mirrors the forward block structure with nearest-neighbor
unpooling and reverse convolutions, and the two branches are aligned at every block output.
Temporal models. Sequence tasks use the BSD RNN (a single recurrent layer with independent
forward input/hidden/readout weights and reverse output/hidden/input weights), the spiking LSTM
(whose analog hidden state passes through an LIF neuron so that the recurrent signal is spiking), or
a GRU variant, all sharing the same forward/reverse alignment interface. Hidden states are aligned
at every time step between the two branches, as defined in Section 4.2 and Appendix B.1.
BSD and BP correspondence. Every BP baseline reuses exactly the forward architecture of its
BSD counterpart—same layer widths, kernels, pooling, and readout—and is trained end-to-end with
the same surrogate gradient. BSD adds the label-driven reverse branch, which is used only during
training and discarded at deployment, so the comparison isolates the effect of the learning rule.

FSCIL C ONFIGURATION

FSCIL experiments use CIFAR-100 with a fixed class order (split seed 2025): session 0 contains
75 base classes, and each of the five incremental sessions introduces 5 novel classes (5-way) with 5
labeled support examples per class (5-shot), sampled with support seed 2025. The base session trains
the BSD CNN for 50 epochs on all base-class training samples; afterwards the forward backbone
is frozen. Prototypes are compared with cosine similarity, and the scores of two feature levels (−2
and −5) are fused with weights 0.85 and 0.15. Novel prototypes are built from view-averaged
support features (the original and horizontally flipped views), optionally evaluated with the same
view averaging at test time, and projected toward the subspace spanned by the base prototypes with
strength α = 0.3, following the prototype-space projection of SAFA-SNN (Zhang et al., 2026).

D.1

E NERGY C OUNTING RULES

Our energy estimates distinguish two classes of arithmetic. When the input of a layer is a binary
spike train, each synaptic multiplication degenerates into an accumulation and is counted as one
AC; when the input is continuous-valued (e.g., raw sensor inputs or pre-LIF activations), the operation is counted as one multiply–accumulate (MAC). Following the 45 nm operation-level model of
Horowitz (2014), we assign EMAC = 4.6 pJ and EAC = 0.9 pJ. All SNN time steps are included
through the multi-step tensor shapes. For spike-driven layers, we measure the mean firing rate of
the actual binary input on the recorded batch and count ACs as the layer’s dense synaptic operations
scaled by this rate, so no firing-rate assumption is needed.
For BP, we count the forward pass layer by layer using operation hooks on every convolution, linear, and recurrent-cell call. The backward pass is estimated as twice the forward dense workload,
accounting for the weight gradient and the input gradient of every layer; this global backward chain
is the dominant training cost of BP.
For BSD, the forward and reverse branches are each counted with the same rule. The reverse branch
is driven by the spike-encoded label, so most of its operations are ACs, which is one source of
BSD’s energy advantage. Because each hidden level is updated only by its own local objective,
the backward computation of a level stays within that level and is counted once per level; there
is no cross-layer error chain. The ReCo objective adds the cost of its similarity matrices: at each
alignment point we count B 2 D MACs for the forward similarities, with B the batch size and D the
representation dimension, and twice that for their backward pass.
Summing the two operation classes over all counted components gives the per-sample energy of one
training step,
Etrain = EMAC × NMAC + EAC × NAC = 4.6 pJ × NMAC + 0.9 pJ × NAC ,

(23)

which is the energy model used in Section 5.4; Table 4 reports the resulting NMAC and NAC for
every dataset. These estimates are analytic, operation-level quantities: the constants come from a
45 nm literature model rather than measurements on a specific device, so the values should be read
as a hardware-independent proxy for training cost, not as measured energy on edge or neuromorphic
hardware.

L IMITATIONS AND F UTURE D IRECTIONS

Our efficiency results are operation-level estimates and timing profiles collected on general-purpose
GPUs; the models have not yet been deployed on actual edge devices. Validating the measured
and projected costs on real hardware is therefore an important next step. In particular, the projected training-time reduction assumes that the two branches and the local-gradient tasks can execute concurrently, so approaching the theoretical optimum may require specialized compute devices
or neuromorphic chips that expose this parallelism.
Second, the architectures to which BSD has been applied are still relatively limited. Our evaluation covers convolutional, recurrent, and gated-recurrent models, and it remains to be explored

the attention-based reverse branch and its alignment points require a dedicated design.
