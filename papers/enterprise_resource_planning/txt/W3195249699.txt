INTRODUCTION

Digitalization increasingly permeates the private and public sectors, affecting all types of organizations and their practices (Hodges & Grubnic, 2010; Jeacle, 2021; Kinder, 2010; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019). Regarding public administration, researchers and practitioners have articulated a new paradigm of “digital government,” which promises more
integrated, agile, and holistic public sector organizations (Andrews, 2019; Gil-Garcia et al., 2018). One technology frequently mentioned in this context is cloud-based computing (Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014;
Yau-Yeung et al., 2020). Cloud-based computing has increased in popularity due to its promise of enhanced flexibility
and a lower cost of deployment (Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019). This technology entails a large part of an organization’s IT
infrastructure being outsourced to an external vendor or service provider. Basically, cloud-based computing enables
actors to access applications and data on-demand from anywhere in the world at any time (Buyya et al., 2010). This
allows a convergence of IT efficiency and business agility (Marston et al., 2011), with the possibility of rapidly scaling a
system up (or down) based on a “pay-as-you-go” model. Given this promissory discourse surrounding digital technologies, it can be expected that—in the spirit of advancing digitalization of the public sector—the use of cloud-delivered
systems, such as cloud-based enterprise resource planning (CERP) systems, will become more common in the future.
As will be illustrated below, some public sector organizations are already using CERP systems and gaining experience
with them.
In this paper, we explore how the implementation of a CERP system in a public sector organization affects its management accounting function. Although research has discussed CERP systems from a conceptual perspective, in-depth
empirical research on whether (and, if so, how) this technology affects the role of public sector management accountants is lacking. Prior research has demonstrated that, while not a straightforward endeavor, public sector management accountants often have an ambition to establish themselves as business partners (Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018;
Järvinen, 2009; Mack & Goretzki, 2017; Moll & Humphrey, 2007; Paulsson, 2012). Cloud-based solutions appear to
be capable of facilitating this aspiration as they are often presented as having the potential to unburden management accountants from transactional activities, thereby freeing up resources for more “value-adding” activities. Moll
and Yigitbasioglu (2019, p. 4), for example, state that “unlike an on-premise ERP system, cloud delivery should allow
accountants to spend less time backing up their data.” Becker and Heinzelmann (2017) further mention that information produced through cloud-based technologies requires management accountants’ skills verifying its reliability, reasoning that this might create “a major opportunity for management accountants to deepen and stabilize their aspired
role of business partner” (p. 226).
Although cloud-based technologies might be regarded as potentially enabling for public sector management
accountants, empirical research cautions us not to leap to conclusions. Empirical research draws an ambiguous picture of how technologies driving standardization in organizations impact the role of management accountants. Some

authors find that these technologies can facilitate a business partner role by relieving management accountants of routine activities (e.g., Goretzki et al., 2013; Järvenpää, 2007; Scapens & Jazayeri, 2003). Others, however, have demonstrated that they may lead to ambiguity around management accountants’ roles (e.g., Caglio, 2003), even triggering tensions within the organization that hinder business partnering (Dechow & Mouritsen, 2005; Goretzki et al.,
2018; Heinzelmann, 2017; Jack & Kholeif, 2008). Taken together, prior research shows that management accounting is not necessarily IT-enabled (cf., Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Lindvall & Iveroth, 2011) but, at times, may be an
IT-constrained occupation. Thus, despite the current “hype” surrounding digital technologies, one needs to be careful
when assuming that CERP systems are (unconditionally) enabling for public sector management accountants and their
role performance. Rather, a nuanced analysis is required to evaluate how the promissory discourse of cloud-based systems materializes in practice and how this affects management accountants working in public sector organizations.
Responding to calls to explore the effects of cloud-based solutions on management accountants (Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019; Yau-Yeung et al., 2020), we conducted a single
case study of a large Swedish public sector organization (referred to as PubliCo). Prior research teaches us that to
understand how technologies affect the roles of management accountants, one needs to pay attention to the dynamics
around the design and use of those systems—as well as the actors involved (see, e.g., Ezzamel & Burns, 2005; Goretzki
et al., 2018; Vaivio, 1999). Correspondingly, a research perspective that melds the technical and the social is appropriate. Accordingly, we draw on the technology power loop model (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992) directing our attention
to: how the CERP system is developed; who “controls” the system, and ultimately decides on the features to include;
the ideology embedded in the system; the kinds of expertise inscribed in the system; and how this, in turn, enables or
constrains public sector management accountants to mobilize their specific expertise in performing their roles.
Our findings suggest that cloud-based technology assumes an ambivalent role in the context of the work of public sector management accountants. On the one hand, the CERP system enabled central management accountants
to mobilize their specific expertise by eliminating manual work, increasing transparency, and making central management accountants feel more comfortable with the numbers. On the other hand, the CERP system constrained local
management accountants trying to mobilize their local expertise through the production of “tailor-made” information for their managers. In contrast to on-premise ERP systems, cloud-based systems are not only standardized and
centralized but also externally managed, which makes it more difficult for local management accountants to inscribe
their expertise into these systems and influence their design. We detail how the features of cloud-based technologies,
such as limited upfront customization and outsourcing, posed a challenge for PubliCo’s local management accountants
and limited their ambitions to act as business partners. The case of PubliCo also illustrates that some of the features
of cloud-based technologies (Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019;
Yau-Yeung et al., 2020) can indeed be beneficial for central management accountants—while creating challenges and
problems for local management accountants. As such, our case adds to the discussion about obstacles to public sector
management accountants’ endeavors to establish a business partner role (see Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018; Mack &
Goretzki, 2017; Moll & Humphrey, 2007; Paulsson, 2012), while also emphasizing the importance of a nuanced perspective when studying the effects of cloud-based technologies on the finance function in public sector organizations.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the relevant literature and conceptual background for our study. The research methodology is described in Section 3, followed by our empirical findings in Section 4. The final sections provide a discussion and conclusion.

cloud-based computing is proclaimed to be a key digital trend for both private and public sector organizations informing their strategies (Bharadwaj et al., 2013). The cloud is presented as an efficient and adaptive IT infrastructure that
can be accessed and shared on-demand among different end-users through web applications (Buyya et al., 2010),
whereas a third party hosts the software on a remote server storing and processing the data. So-called software-as-aservice (SaaS) is an increasingly popular cloud-based alternative to the traditional on-premises software setup (Feng
et al., 2018). It is a software licensing and delivery method that enables organizations to reduce physical hardware
storage and to outsource IT management and services. It is a service model whereby the consumer can use the provided applications running on cloud technology, without having to manage the underlying infrastructure (NIST, 2011).
Cloud-related services are generally described as being more economical for organizations due to a flexible provision
of services and other cost efficiencies (Huang et al., 2015). With SaaS, the user no longer maintains the physical servers
or cloud-based application software, instead paying a subscription for using the provider’s already developed applications while accepting limited possibilities for customization. An organization’s shift toward SaaS thus entails giving up
some control over its IT infrastructure (Buyya et al., 2010).
Cloud-based ERP systems (CERP) are based on both cloud-based computing and SaaS, providing organizations with
new opportunities for efficiency and agility in managing their IT demands. Like traditional ERP systems, they include
multiple organizational functions such as accounting, procurement, and production; however, the software is no longer
stored or developed in-house. Bryson and Sullivan (2003), for example, argue that these systems result in more efficient operations and the ability to manage cash flows more effectively by having predetermined monthly IT costs. The
organization can focus on its core operations and does not need to recruit and retain expensive IT personnel. Olson
(2007) outlines further benefits in terms of a reduction in capital expenditure for software and updates, continuous
access to the latest technology, and more flexible and agile IT capability. Table 1 summarizes the main differences
between traditional ERP systems and CERP systems.
Despite the promissory discourse associated with cloud technology, research on how this technology affects organizations and their finance functions is relatively scarce. Empirical examination of this phenomenon is crucial because,
despite its promised advantages, cloud technology “produces significant management challenges, including maintaining strategic control, managing cloud services and dealing with the organizational, cultural and integration challenges”
(Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014, p. 485). Several scholars have highlighted the need for research that adds to our understanding of whether and how cloud-based solutions affect the role of management accountants (Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019; Yau-Yeung et al., 2020).

2.2

CERP systems and the role of the public sector management accountant

The above-mentioned developments surrounding digital technologies present an interesting area for research on public sector management accountants who have long been known to embrace a varying set of tasks (Holmgren Caicedo
et al., 2018; Mack & Goretzki, 2017; Moll & Humphrey, 2007; Paulsson, 2012). Their traditional role was to deliver routine financial analysis, reports, and information, which has been characterized of less relevance for decision-making

CARLSSON-WALL ET AL .

181

and strategy formulation. Such a bean counter role (Friedman & Lyne, 1997; Granlund & Lukka, 1998; Järvinen, 2009)
focuses mainly on the provision of backward-looking financial information. However, as part of the new public management (NPM) movement (Paulsson, 2012), public sector management accountants have developed more enlarged
ambitions to adopt increasingly strategic and advisory roles (Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018; Mack & Goretzki, 2017).
Such a business partner role is oriented toward interdisciplinary activities and linked to accountants’ interactions with,
and interventions in, managerial work in a more forward-looking manner (Burns & Baldvinsdottir, 2005; Goretzki &
Messner, 2019; Järvenpää, 2007; Järvinen, 2009).
Although the bean counter and business partner roles are often presented in a dichotomous manner, recent studies within the public sector have problematized their unduly strict distinction (Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018 ; Mack
& Goretzki, 2017; Paulsson, 2012). Paulsson (2012 p. 391), for instance, criticized this “classic” typology by asserting
that “it is difficult to find one single label like bean counter or business partner that fits the role of the management
accountant.” Mack and Goretzki (2017) illustrated this point by demonstrating how management accountants often
occupy an in-between position and do not enact one stereotype only. Looking at the promissory discourse on cloudbased technologies and the complex role public sector management accountants occupy, the following research question arises: How do CERP systems enable the role performance of public sector management accountants? Addressing this
question allows us to mobilize a public sector context to answer recent calls for research on the effects of cloud-based
technologies on management accountants.
Overall, our study is situated in a broader domain that looks at the relationship between new technology and the
role of management accountants. This domain has produced a somewhat ambiguous picture of the effects of technology on management accountants, especially concerning their ambitions to be(come) business partners (Becker &
Heinzelmann, 2017). Some studies argue that ERP systems can facilitate a business partner role because they release
management accountants from routine activities and grant them more time for business analysis (Byrne & Pierce,
2007; Sánchez-Rodríguez & Spraakman, 2012; Scapens & Jazayeri, 2003). Also, if management accountants interact closely with managers throughout the implementation process, they can build closer relationships and develop
a better understanding of the business and managers’ information needs (Caglio, 2003; Goretzki et al., 2013). The fact
that ERP systems drive standardization and integration has been presented as a facilitating factor for the management accountant. ERP systems increase transparency and allow accountants to see what is going on in the different
parts of the organization, enabling them to better control activities (Quattrone & Hopper, 2005). Other studies, in contrast, argue that ERP systems do not necessarily create positive effects for management accountants. For instance, the
implementation of an ERP system may support operational managers by making them more independent of the finance
function. This relates to the IT-enabled diffusion of accounting expertise throughout the organization, whereby managers start working with accounting information without involving accountants (Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018; Hyvönen et al., 2009; Kurunmäki, 2004).
Further, prior studies have also outlined how the design and functioning of an ERP system impacts the role of the
management accountant. Jack and Kholeif (2008) illustrated how the introduction of an ERP system was associated
with the reversion of the management accountant’s role to more bean counter types of activities. Heinzelmann (2018)
makes similar observations and argues that “the implementation of IT systems does not necessarily steer management accountants’ roles towards more management support and analysis,” rather sometimes “more routine work and
reporting (‘dirty work’) emerges from the extensive use of IT systems” (p. 479). In a further article, Heinzelmann (2017)
explores how an ERP system’s conceptual underpinnings affect the management accountant. Examining how accountants in a multinational context experience SAP R/3, he shows that since the system draws on a “German accounting
logic,” its implementation can create challenges for management accountants in non-German speaking countries who
often must adjust the way they work to the specifics of the system.
Heinzelmann’s (2017) study highlights the importance of considering the ways and the extent to which (cloudbased) technology affects the (public sector) management accountant. What expertise is inscribed in a system and
how does that define the types of information produced (cf., Ezzamel & Burns, 2005; Vaivio, 1999)? Prior research has
noted that local management accountants feel that standardized systems do not grant them sufficient flexibility to

produce information that can be used for business partnering. Goretzki et al. (2018) showed how local management
accountants rely heavily on locally developed and maintained Excel spreadsheets to produce information that better

Accordingly, CERP systems are a double-edged sword for public sector management accountants—enabling and
constraining their role. Hence, it is crucial to acknowledge that the implementation of a CERP system is not merely a
technical process. It also involves various expertise groups interacting and trying to make the system work for their
purposes (Dechow & Mouritsen, 2005; Wagner et al., 2011). Thus, to understand how CERP technology affects public
sector management accountants one needs to understand how these systems are developed, the nature of the expertise inscribed in them, and how, in turn, this enables or constrains actors to mobilize their expertise. The so-called
technology power loop model (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992) is a suitable analytical tool for understanding this and will
be explained in the following section.

2.3

The technology power loop model

The technology power loop model was developed by Scarbrough and Corbett (1992) and highlights how technologies are made to work, as well as how the role of expert groups may change as they seek to influence their development. Accordingly, this model conceptualizes a dynamic interplay between technology and power. In this regard, it
directs our attention to how a technology is developed; who “controls” its development and decides on the features to
be included in its development; the kind of ideology underlying the technology; and the types of expertise inscribed in
it (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). Expertise is of relevance because actors leading the process of technology development draw on their specific expertise to (re-)design a system, shaping which group(s) of actors control a technology.
Ultimately, this will also affect the extent to which a technology enables and/or constrains the expertise of a range of
actors connected to it, including the extent to which technology may also (re-)define their expertise. The model allows
for an examination of the potential tensions between different groups (both internal and external to the organization)
involved in the development of the technology and vying for control over it. Thus, and of relevance to our study of
the implications of CERP systems for public sector management accountants, “[t]his model [thus] illustrates a dynamic
conception of expertise and a central role for technologies in supporting [or not supporting, certain] forms of expertise”
(Newman & Westrup, 2005, p. 261).
Newman and Westrup (2005) used this model to explain how an ERP system was made to work and how different
internal and external groups sought to influence its development. Considering the development of an ERP system as a
contested arena, they argued that management accountants are (just) one group among many and it is not given that
they will come out “on top” in such contests. The destruction of the monopoly of the finance function in the development of accounting information systems (AISs) can thereby shift expertise from management accountants to senior
management and/or other groups, such as software vendors. The authors concluded that the success of an ERP system is not a purely technical issue, but rather depends on how both internal and external stakeholders interact with
a system (and each other). However, they also argued that while accountants might cede some of their control of the
system to other groups, they “collectively and individually make ERP systems work as they reposition themselves from
being providers of information to becoming information analysts” and that this is another “instance of where the skills
of accountants are needed to make the best use of these technologies” (Newman & Westrup, 2005, p. 269).
The technology power loop model provides an interesting conceptual frame for our study. CERP systems have been
discussed as potential facilitators for business partnering by unburdening management accountants of routine activities (e.g., Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019). Additionally, these systems are argued to advance
the provision of decision-making information (Strauss et al., 2015), which improves the information base and support

that management accountants can offer their managers. Commentators have further argued that as IT expertise is
no longer needed in-house, system development can be realized faster. CERP can present a digital leap forward in a
public sector organization’s technology because it introduces automatic upgrades, improves accessibility, and moves
the system from the organization to the vendor. In terms of the technology power loop model, however, such outsourcing has the potential to create challenges for management accountants as vendors have a degree of control over
the system and its development (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). Making a CERP system work—for the organization and
management accountants as a specific occupational group—is more than an issue of the system’s (technical) design elements and in-house (technical) expertise. It is an ongoing dynamic interaction between the CERP system and various
groups of actors, such as management accountants at different organizational levels (Goretzki et al., 2018; Heinzelmann, 2017), in-house system developers, vendors, and service providers.
Pertinently, Scarbrough and Corbett (1992) note that the development of a technology is not a neutral process—
but is always shaped by certain ideologies influencing the meaning(s) that actors attach to technology and reproduce
through its design and implementation. The authors argue that “[i]deology filters and shapes the way in which people
perceive, understand and relate to a given technology, and it is the meanings attached to technology that define the
technically possible and the technically desirable” (p. 74). They also argue that “ideologies embodied in technology not
only serve to define the ‘legitimate’ role and status of the user, but also the role and status of those who control the
technology” (p. 75). The purposive introduction of a CERP system with its underlying premises, promises, and potential
constraints reflects a certain ideological view of the potential role of technology within an organization and for various
related groups of actors. Mobilizing the technology power loop model as our analytical tool thus directs our attention
to whether and how public sector management accountants use a CERP system to perform or even expand their roles,
as well as the ways and extent to which other groups of actors can take control of and benefit from these systems. How
this played out in practice in the context of our case study of PubliCo is elaborated below.

RESEARCH METHODS

To capture the complexity of a CERP system implementation, we draw on a single case study (Ahrens & Dent, 1998;
Lukka & Modell, 2010). We do so to unpack the effects of how cloud-based systems are used in practice and how they
are implicated in the performance of management accountants’ role. The case organization—referred to as PubliCo
throughout the paper to preserve anonymity—is a large, internally diverse, Swedish municipality. It is composed of
multiple divisions including city planning, culture, labor, sports, education, environment, elderly care, housing, traffic,
real estate, waste management, and parking. Each division functions like a stand-alone organization with divisional
managers and a local finance function. As illustrated in Figure 1 below, most of the management accountants work in

the divisions (hereafter called “local management accountants”), with only 10% working at the headquarters (hereafter called “central management accountants”).1
We knew from personal contacts that PubliCo was implementing a CERP system and these contacts facilitated our
access to the field in 2018. PubliCo implemented the CERP system in late 2015 in two different phases and we managed to get access to divisions from both phases. When our study commenced, early adopting divisions (Division1 and
Division 2) had worked with the system for two and a half years and the other divisions (Division 3, Division 4, and
Division 5) had only completed the implementation less than a year ago. On entering the field, our general aim was to
“track” the effects of this implementation on management accountants “in action.” However, the implementation of
this new system turned out to be a sensitive issue (Ahrens & Dent, 1998). Correspondingly, maintaining access was
challenging as interviewees were either reluctant to talk about the change process or simply too busy (partly because
of the new CERP system). Despite this, we managed to anchor our project at multiple levels in the organization and
were able to interview four central and 12 local management accountants, four central directors, and two central
systems designers. In total, 20 interviews were conducted with 22 different interviewees during the spring of 2018
(see the Appendix for full interview details).2 It was important to interview both central and local level management
accountants to understand if they were affected similarly or differently by the new system. The directors and system
developers from the headquarters provided us with details about the ideas behind the implementation of the CERP
system, as well as its design and how the organization “functions” in general. The semistructured interviews were conducted on-site and face-to-face, lasting between 40 and 75 min. All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed.
We asked questions about the interviewees’ daily work, routines, processes, relationships, as well as communication
around and experiences with the CERP system (including a comparison to the old system). We also focused on the
implications of this for the management accountants. In addition to our interviews, we reviewed both internal and
publicly available documents.
In line with an abductive research approach (Lukka & Modell, 2010), data collection and analysis were highly integrated and proceeded iteratively. It became apparent throughout the research process that the technology power loop
model (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992) constituted a fruitful sensitizing device to analytically capture the nuances of
the CERP implementation. In qualitative research, it is important to remain open to emerging issues (Lukka & Modell,
2010). In our case, this mainly related to the emergence of differences between how the implications of the new system
were perceived by central and local management accountants and their varying attitudes toward it. Although previous
literature had described how the implementation of a standardized and integrated system can change the role of management accountants within an organization toward either increased business partnering (Järvenpää, 2007; Goretzki
et al., 2013; Scapens & Jazaveri, 2003) or bean counting (Jack & Kholeif, 2008; Heinzelmann, 2018), we found both
consequences within PubliCo.
All researchers were involved in the data analysis, wherein the empirical material was first arranged in terms of
common themes, such as management accountants’ roles (e.g., tasks, processes, and relationships), and specifics linked
to cloud technology (e.g., external system providers, limited customization). The narratives resulting from these parts
of the analysis are presented in Section 4.1 and Section 4.2. We then related our data to the main theoretical concepts of the technology power loop model (i.e., how the CERP system is developed, who “controls” it, what ideology is underlying it, what kind of expertise is inscribed in it, and how this, in turn, affects the management accountants). This part of our analysis, reported in Section 4.3, detailed the development and control of the CERP system. We
observed that responsibility for technological development became increasingly centralized and outsourced. Drawing on the technology power loop model, we interpreted this as resulting from a shift in the ideology underlying system development at PubliCo. Theorizing our data, we detailed the kind of expertise that was inscribed in the CERP
system and how this, first, enabled central management accountants’ expertise (see Section 4.4) and, second, constrained local management accountants’ expertise (see Section 4.5). A further element of our analysis (see Section 4.6)
examined the consequences of the increasingly centralized and outsourced development and control of the CERP
system on local management accountants’ mobilization of their specific expertise in performing their role. As such,
the technology power loop model was pertinent in guiding the analysis of the empirical data. Through organizing and

structuring the empirical material into quote tables based on the categories deduced from the technology power loop
model (described above) and categories developed inductively from our data (i.e., the need to differentiate between

With respect to generalizability, it is noteworthy that statistical generalization is not the primary objective in a
qualitative case study (Gobo, 2008; Scapens, 1990). The intention is to make sense of the case at hand, to get to the
bottom of a specific problem, concern, or question and develop fine-grained knowledge about it. Qualitative case studies in this sense draw on the notion of analytical generalization and do so in two ways (Polit & Tanano Beck, 2010). First,
when articulated in a manner that is authentic and credible to the reader, abstracted explanations for specific processes, actions, or events represent theorizations that can be transferred to other cases. Subsequent studies can then
apply these explanations to other cases to further extend the explanations—for example, to different organizational
contexts. If the theory developed from a previous case provides explanations of the new cases, it could be said to be
(analytically) generalized, as it not only explains a specific case but also other and possibly more diverse cases. The
explanations thus have a theoretical resonance that goes beyond mere empirical description. Second, explanations
derived from a particular case could be extended to other cases because they have similar theoretical characteristics.
In this way, theoretical arguments are used to apply the explanations of this case to a broader set of phenomena.

EMPIRICAL FINDINGS

4.1

Introducing PubliCo and its management accountants

PubliCo’s headquarters are responsible for governing and coordinating municipal planning, IT development, administration, and human resources. It serves a management function and has the responsibility to ensure viable short- and
long-term financing through active financial governance. The central finance function is responsible for drafting the
budget proposal (including all divisional budgets) for approval by municipal politicians. A central IT team, which is part
of the municipality’s finance function, is responsible for all central accounting and financial IT systems (including the
implementation of the CERP system).
PubliCo has a long-term vision that embraces four overall objectives, with these encompassing 26 central level
strategic goals related to, for example, equality and antidiscrimination, safety, climate and environment, sustainability,
innovation, digitalization, and availability. These strategic goals are imposed on every division, which means that some
or even all divisions could be jointly responsible for achieving a specific strategic goal.
PubliCo’s most important financial target is to have a balanced budget. However, when this goal is fulfilled, the
focus shifts to evaluating and assessing the value PubliCo has provided for its stakeholders through the fulfilment
of the 26 strategic goals. These assessments are publicly available for stakeholders. As all operational activities are
performed by the divisions, they are ultimately responsible for achieving the strategic goals. To properly exercise their
responsibility, the divisions usually break the strategic goals down into more concrete divisional goals.
Both central and local municipal management accountants perform bean counter oriented tasks related to performance evaluation, reporting, and financial follow-ups, as well as business partner oriented activities related to decision support, analyses, and the management of strategic issues. More specifically, the local management accountants’
bean counter oriented tasks consist of providing financial information to headquarters for financial follow-up and production of financial statements. This information helps top management evaluate divisional performance. The central management accountants’ bean counter oriented tasks relate to the financial follow-up of the divisions and the
preparation of the financial reports for the whole municipality. As PubliCo is highly decentralized, central management accountants usually do not get involved in the divisional operations.

One of the most important business partner oriented tasks for both central and local management accountants
involves designing and working with performance measures. For the central management accountants, this involves
forecasting and assessing the degree of fulfilment of the strategic goals and presenting their assessments to the top

4.2

The new CERP system within PubliCo

The decision to implement a CERP system was taken by the municipal council following a long period of consultation
whereby individuals from different divisions were invited to propose specifications to be included in the public procurement documents. As the divisions worked with a variety of on-premise local AISs, the aspiration was that the
new CERP system should make reporting faster and simpler, increase cost efficiency by decreasing administrative
work, and simplify future system development. Further, it should also enable the management accountants to focus
on “value-creating” tasks:
We hope to free up time for the management accountants in the divisions, time they can allocate to
analyses and support. The idea is not to save money [by laying off management accountants], it is more
that we want better quality. The time they save should be used to create value for the divisions. (Headquarters Finance Director 3)
Vendors were then invited to bid on the project if they fulfilled the regulatory requirements mandated by PubliCo.
Several bids came in and the central IT team decided on a vendor. The chosen CERP system includes several modules
(procurement, accounting, inventory, project management, and billing) and serves a unified purpose of cloud integration. Additional systems, such as local customer relationship management (CRM) systems, were linked or integrated
to the CERP system. The modules have a standard format, requiring the adaption of all divisions to them. The CERP
system thus replaced the different local AISs for procurement, accounting, inventory, and so on, which were used in
the respective divisions of the municipality.
The resultant contract was drafted to include two external parties: the application system vendor (ASV) and
the service provider (SP). The ASV is responsible for developing and updating the system and the SP oversees server maintenance, technical support, testing, and implementations. The users access the system through
the cloud via a web application and, in the event of a problem, can contact the SP for support. The communication between the ASV and PubliCo is conducted solely via the central IT department and focuses on
updates and development requests. The CERP system was implemented gradually in the divisions so as not
to overburden the central IT team. The setup and communication flow around the system is illustrated in

The development and control of the CERP system

A crucial feature of the CERP system was the centralization of the IT development process, creating a standardized
way of working throughout the entire organization. This led to a major change in PubliCo as to how systems were
developed and who controlled technology (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). Local management accountants described
the pre-CERP era as a “pain-free process”: they would work together with the local IT team to develop local on-premise
AISs that catered for their local needs, allowing them to provide divisional managers with the information required to
manage their strategic goals. With the introduction of the CERP system, however, the responsibility for the system
shifted to the central IT department and (partly) to the external ASV and SP as well. The central IT department became
responsible for managing the implementation process and coordinating with the external partners:
We do not do any coding and programming [ourselves]. We are responsible for the processes. If there
is something with the code, then [the ASV] is doing that. We have an opinion about what processes
and modules work or not and how we want to change the system. Then maybe the [ASV] says it is not
possible in the standard solution, then we decide if we need an adaptation. If so, we tell them what to
develop and they do the programming and coding. (Headquarters System Developer 2)
Although the decentralized divisions were used to having a free rein to develop their local systems, with the CERP
system every modification to the off-the-shelf solution had to be evaluated and approved by the central IT department. Throughout the implementation of the new system, the central IT team thus assumed a stronger position in the
organization. It established itself as a driver of standardization, efficiency, and technological advancements, approving
only a few of the local change requests:
. . . we are the ‘owners’ of the process. If there is a problem, we are responsible. The [SP or the ASV]
would never do any major things without talking to us. And we do not authorize any changes if there is
not a major thing of importance for the whole municipality. (Headquarters System Developer 1)
This was seen as a relatively radical step. Prior to the CERP system implementation, the divisions controlled their
systems and could develop and adjust them at will. The local management accountants thus felt that their role had
been circumscribed—while the central IT team had improved its position. System Developer 1, who had acted as the
project leader for the CERP system implementation, explained how the IT department had grown and occupied a more
strategic role within PubliCo:

188

CARLSSON-WALL ET AL .

We [the central IT team] have two responsibilities. The first is making sure the system works. We purchase that service externally. The users call our service provider if they need support. Our second focus,
our main focus, is strategic. What should we do? How should we do it? What projects should we go for?
What are the effects? What choices should we make?
Connecting our observations to the technology power loop model (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992) and especially the link between the development and control of technology, despite PubliCo’s highly decentralized structure, the responsibility for technological development became increasingly centralized and outsourced. The central
IT department took control of the new system, as will be further elaborated in Section 4.6. In contrast to Newman and Westrup (2005), however, the central IT department’s position did not result from a battle between different occupations. Rather, drawing on Scarbrough and Corbett (1992), we can interpret this as resulting from
a shift in the ideology underlying system development at PubliCo. Centralizing development was seen as crucial to reducing activities within the central and local finance functions that were “non-value adding.” We can
interpret this as an ideological change as to how technology empowers management accountants to perform
a business partner role. In the pre-CERP era, the belief was that this could be achieved through highly localized (on-premise) AISs, wherein management accountants (in conjunction with local IT) were both key producers
and controllers of the systems. The ideology underlying the CERP project, however, was that standardized and
integrated systems developed and maintained externally would allow management accountants to “outsource” a
large part of their bean counter activities and, thereby, concentrate on using ready-made information for more
detailed analysis. Consequently, the control of the system by the central IT department was a by-product of a new
“techno-ideology,” rather than the result of an occupational competition between the local and central management
accountants.
In the following section, we elaborate how local systems replaced by the CERP system affected the work of central
and local management accountants.

4.4
Central management accountants’ perceptions of the CERP system—An enabler
for centralized expertise
In our case, centrally located management accountants developed a positive attitude towards the CERP system and
acknowledged that it helped them perform their tasks. Prior to CERP, they had to manually transfer data from the
local systems into the group reporting tool, which they perceived not only as a significant time drain but also a source
of potential errors. The new system minimized this risk of errors by automating information flows and reducing the
need for manual data input:
[Before the CERP implementation] it was the classical, often with Excel spreadsheets . . . But now they
upload the data in [the CERP system] so we see it directly . . . Much easier for us since we do not need to
adjust and cut and paste the data from Excel to our system. [. . . ] It is better and safer [now] . . . Nothing
should be wrong. It is the taxpayers’ money so there should be no errors. (Headquarters Management
Accountant 1)
For them, the CERP system had improved data quality and made them feel more comfortable with the numbers:
The [CERP system] is where the balance sheet and income statement are, that is the closing of the
books. It needs to be correct here . . . One should not sit with various files and Excel. It is not safe . . .
the new system is much safer and better. (Headquarters Management Accountant 2)
This view was also reinforced by the finance directors:

As outlined above, the beliefs underlying the CERP system materialized in the central management accountants’
experiences of the system. For them, it made life easier and allowed them to use the information produced through
the system for activities that they felt created more value for the organization. Like the central IT department, the new
system indirectly facilitated their position in the organization. The fact that stricter standards helped the central management accountants to consolidate information in a faster and “safer” way, with this being recognized by the finance
directors, led to an overall positive attitude at the headquarters level toward the centralized and partly outsourced
approach to developing the system.
In Section 4.5 below, we will elaborate how this led to tensions among the local management accountants who felt
that the new systems did not enable them to use their local expertise (see Vaivio, 2004) to perform a business partner
role.

4.5
Local management accountants’ perceptions of the CERP system—A constraint
on performing local expertise
When talking about the CERP system, many of the local management accountants acknowledged the advantages
accruing to their headquarters. Interviewees, for example, mentioned that the new system would allow the central
management accountants to create “nicer reports” and enable them to better carry out their specific tasks:

A lot, as I see it, is about getting a common financial reporting structure. So, headquarters benefits if
everyone reports in the same way. (Division 4 Management Accountant 1)
I see also clear advantages with the templates which are the same for the whole municipality. So, if one
of us [local management accountants] quits or changes jobs, there is a standard routine, and it is the
same for everyone. (Division 2 Management Accountant 1)
Local management accountants also realized that this uniformity could even improve the communication among

I think over time we talk much more to the other divisions and share knowledge now that we all are in

A lot of manual work, this is a huge change for us. It takes more time. It is not good but there is nothing

Their main concerns related to a perceived lack of flexibility associated with the new system and an increase in

several previously individual accounts had to be consolidated into one. The purpose of this was to create more comprehensive accounts of the organization and to enable top management and central management accountants to effectively follow-up and evaluate goal achievement at a group level. However, major efforts were needed from the divisions
to construct such uniformity across their data, as previous highly customized codes and processes had to be changed
to accommodate municipality-wide uniformity. Some of the local management accountants considered this mandate
intrusive and did not see any advantages from altering their previous (and, in their view, highly satisfactory) working practices. When asked how these changes had been communicated from the central office to the divisions, a local
management accountant described his antipathy as follows:
The unified account-plan was to be able to see things together. But there weren’t many benefits for us
[local management accountants]. I felt [. . . ] this wasn’t for us but for someone else. (Division 4 Management Accountant 1)
Indeed, one of the motives of implementing the CERP system in the first place was to improve the reporting for
the municipality as a whole, leading to increased transparency across PubliCo. Some local management accountants
opposed this as they were used to being highly independent in terms of their daily operations and control of their
local systems (cf., Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). Hence, they perceived the ambition to increase organization-wide

Handling of invoices did not work well, so to make it work we had to keep some parts of our old system.

When it comes to the invoicing module for example, it is now much worse. It takes more time for us to

systems reflected the local management accountants’ local expertise and enabled them to support their managers. By shifting to the CERP system, they saw themselves confronted with an off-the-shelf solution that forced

constrained the local management accountants. This frustrated local management accountants’ ability to apply their
expertise to produce information to support their divisional business managers’ work (cf., Scarbrough & Corbett,
1992). They felt that the CERP systems embedded “foreign” beliefs about how things are supposed to work, constraining their ability to invoke “vernacular accounting” (Kilfoyle et al., 2013) practices that had proved their usefulness in
previous situations advising and supporting their business managers. Given the local management accountants’ negative attitude toward the CERP system, we discuss below the extent to which they were able to influence the system’s
(further) development (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992).

Before, I had full access and I could just go into the system and change it. Now I don’t have any authority

Our case illustrates how a cloud-based system with restricted opportunities for customization can be welcomed by
central management accountants but create frustrations on the part of their local counterparts. Prior to the implementation of the CERP, local management accountants were involved in developing their local AISs and could inscribe their
local expertise in those systems. However, they are now confronted with an off-the-shelf solution that only grants limited influence (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). Influence on the system is only possible if they have a compelling case in
that their request is beneficial for the whole organization, contributing to making processes easier, more efficient, and
safer within PubliCo. Considering this, the new system setup triggered power dynamics (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992)
that disciplined local management accountants in line with a centralized structure—even though the implementation
of the CERP system was not part of a broader centralization strategy.

DISCUSSION

The case of PubliCo demonstrates how the meanings and attitudes that central and local management accountants
attach to a CERP system relate to the technology’s ideological underpinnings, as well as their specific understandings of how technology can (or should) enable their work (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). This was especially noticeable
when analyzing the shift within PubliCo with respect to how the role of the AIS “framed” the management accountants’
work and systems development (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992, p. 75). PubliCo’s “old” system landscape resulted from
a strong belief that local flexibility in the development of (on-premise) AIS would facilitate the work of the finance
function. The introduction of the CERP system in this sense reflected different beliefs and assumptions about how

CARLSSON-WALL ET AL .

195

accountants work with information and how technology is supposed to empower them to cultivate a business partner role. Considering the overall ambition within PubliCo to produce “better, faster and safer” reports on all levels of
the organization, the introduction of the CERP system reflected a shift from a focus on a local production of tailormade information via on-premise systems toward a company-wide consumption of prefabricated information produced through a ready-made (standard) system developed and maintained externally.
Examining the introduction of a CERP system, our study showcases how ideological changes regarding the role of
technology in the finance function shapes management accountants’ ability to control AISs and how this affects how
those systems enable or constrain accountants’ expertise and role performance. We thus demonstrate how the introduction of a CERP system can trigger dynamics and tensions in what Scarbrough and Corbett (1992) refer to as an
organization’s technology power loop. Before the CERP project, local management accountants within PubliCo took
part in the development of their own on-premise AISs. In terms of system development, they “controlled” those systems and were able to flexibly inscribe their divisional expertise as well as their managers’ information needs into
their systems (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). The local management accountants’ position in the technology power
loop was, in turn, reinforced through their self-developed systems as the latter mainly drew on and reproduced the
accountants’ codified expertise. Although this setup empowered the local management accountants and allowed them
to perform a business partner role, it posed problems for their central counterparts. In the pre-CERP era, central management accountants were arguably detached from the (local) system development. The central management accountants were not involved in the system development, they had no control over the local systems that produced the numbers they were to work with, and they merely “consumed” the numbers their local counterparts delivered to them.
Although their main issue was not that they were not in charge of the local systems, central management accountants often struggled with the consequences of the highly localized setups. For example, consolidating various types
of local information and aligning different reporting formats efficiently and accurately was perceived as challenging
and cumbersome. Central management accountants also felt that this strong reliance on the (old) systems based on
the local management accountants’ expertise further constrained them in demonstrating their respective expertise
vis-à-vis their superiors. From their perspective, the (old) system did not allow them to properly act as central business partners who provided more advanced analyses for the finance directors and other senior managers. In contrast,
they were required to act as bean counters—whose main task was to bring order into a messy information set so that
local information could be used more confidently and meaningfully at the central level. The fact that the new CERP
system provided numbers in a standardized format thus made it easier for the central management accountants to
accomplish their desired role, providing top managers with “nicer” reports and devoting more time to analytical and
“value-adding” tasks.
Although the purpose of the CERP system was to enable both local and central management accountants to pursue their work objectives, the central management accountants developed a more positive attitude toward it than
their colleagues at the divisional level. The central management accountants felt that the shift toward standardization
and integration triggered by the CERP system led to less manual work, reduced the risk of errors in the consolidated
reports, and freed up resources so that they could perform more analytical tasks. The local management accountants,
however, experienced the opposite situation. For them, a major issue was how the CERP system was developed and
who controlled it (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). The management of the implementation process was assigned to PubliCo’s central IT team, which designed the system in collaboration with external partners. This enabled the central IT
team to effectively control the system (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). As customization of the CERP system was difficult, the central IT team approved local change requests only if the whole organization benefited from them. As a result,
the local management accountants were no longer able to flexibly inscribe their expertise into the system (Scarbrough
& Corbett, 1992). This was an issue for them because they were used to locally developing and controlling their systems and to producing their numbers, considering this “production” element as a crucial part of their business partner
role. The local management accountants considered that the specific ideological underpinnings of the CERP forced
them to hand control of the system to others, confronting instead a role as “mere” consumers of a product that did not
satisfy their needs. They struggled with how the CERP system constrained them in performing what they saw as more

196

CARLSSON-WALL ET AL .

value creating tasks and how (in comparison to their central counterparts) they now had to perform extra manual work
to make the new system work locally. Consequently, their business partner role was curtailed by the implementation
of the CERP system.
Looking at the different attitudes that central and local management accountants developed toward the CERP system, we can see that although both focal groups in our analysis belong to the same occupation, they framed the role
of technology in performing their expertise differently (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992). While
local management accountants framed technology as a tool that should enable them to draw on their local expertise to produce tailor-made information for their local units, central management accountants viewed it as a tool
allowing them to consume prefabricated, “high-quality” information to assure efficient and risk-free accounting processes throughout the entire organization. Given this, cloud-based solutions seem more enabling and (hence) appealing for centrally located rather than local management accountants. This helps to qualify the limits of the more general
observation of Newman and Westrup (2005), who champion a viewpoint that technology provides an opportunity for
accountants to shift “from being providers of information to becoming information analysts” (p. 269). In comparison,
our case points to the fact that not all accountants experience this change in an equally enabling and transformative
way.
It is important to emphasize that we could not identify a strategic agenda behind this shift that curtailed the work
aspirations of the local management accountants (in the divisions) and improved that of their central counterparts (in
the headquarters). As such, we did not observe an overt and increasing ambition to drive centralization within PubliCo.
Rather, the sincere belief expressed by the finance directors was that management accountants at both the central and
local level would benefit from the CERP system. By not having to develop and maintain the systems themselves and
by performing less manual work related to the consolidation of the heterogeneous information sets, it was believed
that business partnering would be facilitated within both the headquarters and divisions of PubliCo. While the central management accountants indeed benefited from the implementation of the CERP system, the local management
accountants felt constrained and frustrated by it.

CONCLUSION

This study provides insights into how the promissory discourse of cloud-based technologies materializes in the practices of the finance function in a public sector context. More specifically, it examines the experience of management
accountants in a large, decentralized public sector organization with a newly introduced CERP system. Drawing on
the technology power loop model (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992) to understand a single case study of PubliCo, a large
Swedish municipality, our study extends the literature on how technology affects management accountants, both in
a public sector setting (Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018; Mack & Goretzki, 2017; Paulsson, 2012) and more generally (e.g., Caglio, 2003; Goretzki et al., 2013; Heinzelmann, 2017, 2018; Jack & Kholeif, 2008; Newman & Westrup,
2005; Sánchez-Rodríguez & Spraakman, 2012; Scapens & Jazayeri, 2003). Building on the findings of previous research
pointing to the ambivalence of the effects of technology on management accountants (e.g., Caglio, 2003; Dechow &
Mouritsen, 2005; Goretzki et al., 2013; Heinzelmann, 2017, 2018; Jack & Kholeif, 2008; Lindvall & Iveroth, 2011;
Scapens & Yazayri, 2003), we have been motivated to develop a more nuanced understanding of this phenomenon.
We do so by detailing how the meanings that central and local management accountants attach to a CERP system
and the attitudes they develop toward it, relate to how the system was developed, who “controlled” it, its ideological
underpinnings, and the kinds of expertise that were inscribed in the system.
We demonstrate that technology adopts an ambivalent role for public sector management accountants and conclude that the implications of digital technologies, such as cloud-based systems (see Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019), differ
not only between organizations, but also affect management accountants differently within the same organizations.
Our findings hence emphasize how difficult it is to predict the effects that a specific technology has on the finance
function in (and within) different organizations. The present study also reminds us to be cautious about the current

CARLSSON-WALL ET AL .

197

“hype” surrounding digitalization and not to regard digital technologies as “universal” enablers of the finance function
(see also Quattrone, 2016). The case of PubliCo illustrates that while some of the features of cloud-based technologies (Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019) can indeed be beneficial for
central management accountants, those technologies also create challenges for local management accountants. Our
case thereby exemplifies the limitations of cloud-based systems that are especially pertinent for local management
accountants who are used to developing and controlling their local systems to ensure that they cater for the specific
needs of their units, while supporting them in performing a local business partner role. We show that CERP systems are
underpinned by a specific ideology that can undermine how local management accountants frame the role of technology for their work, with CERP systems seeming to align more with the beliefs and assumptions of central management
accountants focusing more on the consolidation of standardized and integrated systems. As such, the case of PubliCo
demonstrates that the dynamics and tensions of the technology power loop are not only shaped by different actors’
competing for control over systems (see Newman & Westrup, 2005), but also by ideological shifts (see Scarbrough &
Corbett, 1992) concerning the role of these systems within the organization or specific functions, such as the finance
function in this case.
Accordingly, our study extends prior work that has highlighted differences in the impact of new technologies on
central and local management accountants (Goretzki et al., 2018; Heinzelmann, 2017; Newman & Westrup, 2005). We
show that off-the-shelf cloud solutions can raise tensions in large and diversified public sector organizations and pose
challenges especially for local actors who do not have full control over these systems and are thereby limited in their
attempts to mobilize them in the context of their specific expertise. While we acknowledge that problems around customization may also arise for on-premise standardized ERP systems (see, e.g., Wagner et al., 2011), research demonstrates that these problems can be mitigated through collaborative development processes whereby local accountants mobilize expertise associated with their local systems to exert influence on the design of a new central system
(Goretzki et al., 2018). This seems more difficult in a cloud-based environment wherein adjustments are expensive,
difficult, and require formal approval. Consequently, this not only frustrates local management accountants but also
undermines their expertise (Scarbrough & Corbett, 1992), which is often ignored in a cloud environment where it is
not always possible to use local systems as knowledge transformation devices (Carlsson-Wall et al., 2019; Goretzki
et al., 2018; Kilfyole et al., 2013). However, in diversified public sector organizations especially, flexibility in designing
systems is crucial for local management accountants to support decision-making and control processes. Constraining
this flexibility may undermine the developments that local public sector management accountants achieved during
the NPM movement—inhibiting a desired business partner mentality (Paulsson, 2012). Cloud technology runs the risk
that, at the local level, management support reverts to an “old fashioned” approach whereby accounting processes
are standardized, cumbersome, and not accommodating of local needs. Coping with an inflexible cloud-based system
might therefore add to the list of challenges that public sector management accountants experience when trying to
be(come) business partners (Holmgren Caicedo et al., 2018; Mack & Goretzki, 2017; Paulsson, 2012).
We furthermore contribute to the literature by providing in-depth empirical insights into the organizational consequences of cloud-based systems that form a new and hitherto rarely discussed phenomenon in the field of (public sector) accounting (Becker & Heinzelmann, 2017; Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014; Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019; Strauss
et al., 2015; Yau-Yeung et al., 2020). The notion of commodified systems is taken to a new level by CERP systems and
their SaaS delivery method. These technologies involve a greater degree of standardization and provide fewer possibilities for localization. Therefore, to a greater extent, public sector organizations need to adapt to these systems
and not vice versa, which contributes to a loss of authority with respect to system design. In this sense, the introduction of a CERP system can reflect what we refer to as an ideological shift; a shift from a focus on local production
of tailor-made information toward an organization-wide consumption of prefabricated information. This shift can be
linked to what Quattrone (2016) described in the context of the increasing digitalization of management accounting:
“We nowadays consume accounting figures [. . . ] as we buy groceries in supermarkets, without knowing how the food
has been processed, technically alienated from the manufacturing process, with labels there to reassure us about the
ingredients but nothing about whether these are the right nutrients for us” (p. 120). Our findings illustrate that central

198

CARLSSON-WALL ET AL .

management accountants (in contrast to the local management accountants) seem to feel more comfortable with, and
even enabled by, consuming prefabricated information produced by externally developed and maintained CERP systems. Cloud-based computing thus has advantages for some parts of an organization or for certain actors but may not
be suitable for all organizational units’ or actors’ needs. Considering public sector management accountants, our study
demonstrates that in the wake of the implementation of new digital technologies, a reversion back to a bean counter
role may occur for some (local) management accountants—especially those who are used to being involved in system
development processes, viewing those systems as important enablers of their role performance. Our study thus illustrates that role changes in the work of management accountant in the context of new (digital) technologies are not as
straightforward as is sometimes depicted in the current digitalization discourse (see also Bhimani & Willcocks, 2014;
Moll & Yigitbasioglu, 2019).
Future empirical research is needed to better understand the intricacies of implementing and working with cloudbased solutions. For example, what are the implications for power relations and management control when the IT
infrastructure is located outside an organization? How should public sector organizations account for vendor risk?
How should management control systems be designed to take into consideration a vendor’s accountability? Future
studies that address the implications, uses, risks, and control features in this new setting, and that further explore
the extent to which CERP systems produce different organizational effects compared to traditional ERP implementations, are needed. Furthermore, cloud technology implies that external parties, such as vendors or service providers,
gain a larger role in the technology power loop and thus have a greater impact on the role of management accountants. Accordingly, we call for future research on the relationship between external parties and management accountants during system development processes. Finally, we see a need for greater theorization of CERP implementations,
especially in terms of notions such as time and emotion. As our case demonstrates, CERP systems affected how management accountants spent their time (Feldman et al., 2020), changed development rhythms (Ancona et al., 2001) and
caused feelings of frustration and antipathy (Baxter et al., 2019; Carlsson-Wall et al., 2021). Accordingly, we encourage
future research that more deeply probes the temporal and emotional dimensions of CERP developments and implementations.
